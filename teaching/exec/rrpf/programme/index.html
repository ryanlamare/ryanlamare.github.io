APPLIED
GAME THEORY
Strategic thinking in sales and beyond
Two full days  ·  in person  ·  Approx. 20 participants

Game theory is the art and science of strategic thinking, where your best course of action requires consideration of what the other side intends to do. Almost every commercial interaction can be structured as a set of stragic games. Yet we often make the mistake of deciding what outcome we want and blindly advancing towards it, seeing ourselves as operating within a narrow vacuum, rather than asking what the other party will rationally do in response and choosing our strategy accordingly.

This programme is built to change that mindset, and to teach the fundamental building blocks of a game theoretic way of doing business. Each module introduces a core game theory concept, tests that concept using interactive games, and applies the lessons learned to real-world situations. Although technical rulesets underpin each game, the programme is almost entirely applied and non-technical in nature, using lived experiences rather than abstract mathematics to teach the core concepts. By the end of the two days, participants will leave with a working vocabulary for how to use game theory to diagnose strategic situations, a way to resolve these situations to find equilibrium and benefit their rational self-interests, and a clear understanding of where the theory holds and can be challenged.

How the programme will run

The programme will include eight modeuls, and each will run for roughly an hour and a half. Modules will be highly interactive in nature. We will use a combination of lectures, readings, short films, and other media to convey key concepts. Each moeule will begin with a short overview of the idea and theory, will involve a discussion of applications to real-world situations, and will include at least one game plus a debrief. Having two full days will allow us to cover a survey of game theory practices and gives us enough time to dive into each topic beyond a superficial level.

All materials and games will be available as web pages that participants can access on their phones or laptops, and the front-of-room screen carries. The materials will remain available afterwards at a fixed web address for participants to use at any point in the future.

Before the programme

Optional pre-reading

No preparation is required, and the two days are self-contained. But participants who would like a grounding beforehand will find the below core text useful, and its chapters map nicely onto the modules. The reading is written for a general audience and contains no mathematics beyond arithmetic.

Avinash K. Dixit and Barry J. Nalebuff, The Art of Strategy: A Game Theorist’s Guide to Success in Business and Life (W. W. Norton; paperback edition 2010, ISBN 978-0-393-33717-4). Most relevant chapters for our programme: “Tales of strategy”, “Games solvable by backward reasoning”, “Prisoners’ dilemmas and how to resolve them”, “A beautiful equilibrium”, “Choice and chance”, “Strategic moves”, “Making strategies credible”, and “Interpreting and manipulating information”.

The eight modules

1

What Is Game Theory, Anyway?

We are all game theorists, whether we realize it or not. This module teaches the core requirements to excel at game theory, including common knowledge, best response, and equilibrium. You will also learn the number one rule of game theory, which is that you must be able to put oneself in the other side's shoes in order to get the best outcome for yourself.

Example GameI'm Thinking of A Number. Participants will be asked to guess the number that I'm thinking of from 1 to 100. You'll get five guesses. The catch is, I'll tell you whether you're too high or too low after each guess. This game will teach us the basics of common knowledge, equilibriumm, and putting yourself in the other's shoes.
outcomesCommon knowledge; equilibrium; game theory paradoxes
2

Sequential Games: Looking Forward, Reasoning Backward

When moves are ordered rather than simultaneous, games are solved from the end backwards. This session covers game trees, backward reasoning, and charging a complete course of action to find equilibrium. We also cover the (dis)advntages of being the first-mover, and the irrationalities and limitations associate with sequential reasoning.

Example gameThe Proposal. Participants are split into teams of two, where one side acts as the "proposer" and the other acts as the "responder". The proposer has $1,000 available to offer, and the responder must either accept or reject whatever the proposer puts on the table. The catch is, if the responder turns the propser down, the money is lost for both sides.
outcomesDecision trees; rollback equilibrium; limits of sequential reasoning
3

Simultaneous Zero-Sum Games

This module covers the first of several simultaneous move games, where parties select their strategies concurrently. Under simultaneous move conditions, some situations involve dividing a fixed quantity, whereby one side's gain is the other’s loss. Here we also learn dominant and inferior strategies, how to read a payoff matrix quickly, and what happens to competitive positioning when the available choices are continuous rather than a limited set of options.

Example gameHotdogs on the Beach. Two hotdog vendors must decide where to position their carts along a beach in order to attract the maximum number of customers. Where should each of the place their carts? And is their choice of cart placement socially optimal?
OutcomesDominant and inferior strategies; Hotelling games and their applications
4

Prisoner’s Dilemmas Sample module

The prisoner’s dilemma is the structure in which each side, reasoning correctly and in its own interest, arrives at an outcome both would have rejected. Its most expensive commercial form is the price war. This session builds the structure, plays it out at length, and then works carefully through the exits, including the important distinction between the exits that are available to a commercial team and the ones that are not.

The gameThe Renewal. Six rounds of annual contract renewals between two suppliers of an interchangeable product. Each round, each team decides in private whether to hold its price or undercut. Two rounds carry double stakes, and before each of those the two sides may meet for two minutes if both agree to. Nothing said in those meetings binds anyone.
They leave withThe four routes out of a price war that are actually available to them, and a clear line marking where the textbook routes stop being lawful.
5

Coordination and Focal Points

A great many failures are not conflicts at all. Both sides want the same outcome and still fail to reach it, because wanting the same thing is not sufficient when there is more than one way to get there. This session covers games with several stable outcomes, the focal points that pick between them, and how expectations rather than incentives end up deciding.

The gameThe Focal-Point Round. A rapid sequence of coordination questions answered privately with no discussion permitted, then revealed together. The group finds out how much silent alignment it already has, and precisely where that alignment breaks.
They leave withA way to construct a focal point deliberately instead of hoping one appears, and an explanation for why the first number put on a table so often turns out to be the answer.
6

Mixed Strategies and Being Hard to Read

Where any pattern in your behaviour can be observed and exploited, consistency becomes a liability and the correct strategy is to be deliberately unpredictable. The session works the logic through on a case where the arithmetic is transparent, then turns to its ordinary commercial form, which is deciding what proportion of something to inspect, audit or enforce when covering everything is not affordable.

The gameRead My Move. Repeated rounds against an opponent whose only job is to predict you, scored on how often they succeed. Followed by a team exercise setting an enforcement rate against a stated budget, with the results compared across teams.
They leave withA sense of when being reliable is costing them, and a method for setting an inspection or enforcement rate that changes behaviour without approaching full coverage.
7

Information Asymmetries

One side almost always knows more than the other, and knows that it does. This session distinguishes cheap talk from a signal that costs something to send and is therefore worth believing, covers screening, meaning the design of a choice that makes the other party reveal itself, and works through adverse selection and what happens to a market when buyers cannot tell quality apart.

The gameClosed Session. A committee has to reach a decision. Each participant holds a private role and private information, and no one knows who else holds what. The decision the room reaches is usually not the one the information in the room supports, and the debrief traces exactly where it went.
They leave withA test for distinguishing a costly signal from cheap talk, and the beginnings of a habit of designing choices that make a counterparty sort itself.
8

Strategic Moves: Commitment, Threats and Promises

You are not obliged to play the game you were handed. By acting before the game begins, and specifically by removing your own options in a way the other side can see, you can change what they will rationally do. This session covers commitments, threats and promises, and then the problem on which all three actually depend, which is whether anyone believes you.

The gameThe Standoff. Four rounds of a renewal negotiation in which each team holds three commitment cards, one of each type. Playing a card is public and cannot be undone. Deals score, no deal costs both sides, and several teams will commit themselves straight out of an agreement they wanted.
They leave withA checklist for making a commitment credible, and the discipline to recognise the moment commitment has turned into sunk cost and escalation.

Putting it together

The final half hour introduces nothing new. The eight ideas are set against one another so that participants can see which situations they are actually in, and the closing discussion is deliberately theirs: where in their own work each structure appears, which of the habits is worth adopting first, and what they would do differently in the next negotiation they walk into.

The through-line
Decide what the other side will do before deciding what you want. Then change the game rather than playing it harder.

Practicalities

Format
Two consecutive full days, in person, 09:00 to 16:30.
Group size
Up to twenty participants, working in four teams of five that stay together across both days.
Room
One room with a screen, tables arranged for four teams, and either four breakout spaces or four corners far enough apart that teams cannot overhear one another.
Participants bring
A phone or laptop each. No printed materials are used or needed.
Tailoring
The games are set in neutral fictional situations by design, so that the structure is visible and nobody spends the session arguing about the case facts. The commercial application is built in the debriefs, which is where participants supply their own examples. Two or three anonymised situations provided in advance sharpen those discussions considerably.
Materials afterwards
All handouts, scorecards and slides remain available at a fixed web address for participants to return to.

Facilitator

Ryan Lamare is Professor of Employment Relations and Human Resource Management at the London School of Economics, and has taught applied game theory at postgraduate level for over a decade in both the United States and the United Kingdom. His teaching is deliberately hands-on and non-mathematical, built around games and their debriefs rather than lectures, and aimed at the logic of strategic interaction as it appears in ordinary commercial and working life. This programme is offered in a personal capacity.
