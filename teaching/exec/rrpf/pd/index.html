Applied Game Theory, Module 4 — Prisoner's Dilemmas and How to Overcome Them

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 21: Title slide

Module 4 of Applied Game Theory: Prisoner's Dilemmas and How to Overcome Them. Facilitator: Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 21: First a game, then we talk

The session runs from 14:15 to 16:15. Fifty minutes playing a game, twenty minutes on what happened, twenty minutes on the structure of a prisoner's dilemma, and thirty minutes on how to overcome one. Nothing is explained before we play, and that is deliberate.

Slide 3 of 21: Two stations, one junction

Aura Fuels and Buco's Service Station face each other across the same junction on a busy A road. Aura has been there thirty years, but there is family pressure to hang up the spurs. Buco's is the newest upstart, ready to make a mark, but its investors want returns. You sell identical fuel to identical drivers, and price is the only thing that separates you. Every Monday morning you post your price on the roadside sign. You post it without seeing theirs. Once both signs are up, every driver can read both prices all week, and so can the other station. Six trading weeks.

Slide 4 of 21: The payoffs

The payoff matrix. Aura is the row player and Buco's the column player, and each cell reads as Aura's weekly margin followed by Buco's. Both post one pound fifty: twelve thousand pounds each. Aura posts one pound fifty while Buco's posts one pound forty: two thousand for Aura and eighteen thousand for Buco's. Aura posts one pound forty while Buco's posts one pound fifty: eighteen thousand for Aura and two thousand for Buco's. Both post one pound forty: nine thousand each. The arithmetic behind it is that one pound fifty earns thirty pence a litre and one pound forty earns twenty pence, and the cheaper station takes almost all the traffic.

Slide 5 of 21: How we play

Six rules. The game runs six weeks and you post one price each week, either one pound fifty or one pound forty. You get three minutes with your team beforehand. You give me your price privately and both signs go up together. You have no contact of any kind with the other station except around weeks three and six. In weeks three and six all profits double, and before each of those weeks the two stations may meet for two minutes, but only if both sides ask for it. Your goal is to make as much money as you can across the six weeks.

Slide 6 of 21: The board

The running scoreboard, filled in live. A row for Aura and Buco's in each of the two matches, a column for each of the six weeks, and a total.

Slide 7 of 21: What it paid

Three reference points to read your score against, remembering that weeks three and six pay double. If both stations post one pound fifty every week, each finishes the six weeks on ninety-six thousand pounds. If both post one pound forty every week, each finishes on seventy-two thousand. If one station holds at one pound fifty every week while the other posts one pound forty every week, the one that held finishes on sixteen thousand and the other on a hundred and forty-four thousand.

Slide 8 of 21: What happened

Four discussion questions. What price did you post in week one, and what was your reasoning? What changed once you saw the other station's sign? Did anyone hold at one pound fifty after being undercut, and what did that decision cost? And if the two of you met before week three, did the promised agreement survive the week?

Slide 9 of 21: The prisoner's dilemma

The structure has a name and this is the version it is named after. Two suspects are held separately and each chooses whether to stay silent or confess. Both silent gives one year each. One confesses while the other stays silent: the confessor goes free and the silent one gets twenty-five years. Both confess gives three years each. Two cells are marked. Both confessing is the Nash equilibrium, ringed in red: it is where correct individual reasoning takes both players. Both staying silent is ringed in teal as the Pareto-optimal outcome, because both players do better there. The whole difficulty of the game is that the second cell is unreachable by individual reasoning alone.

Slide 10 of 21: A dominant strategy

Defection dominates: undercutting is the best response to any choice the other side makes, so it is what you play. But both of you undercutting lands both in sub-optimal circumstances. Nobody has to be greedy or naive to fall into this trap. Individually correct reasoning will produce a collectively worse result for the parties.

Slide 11 of 21: The general form

Take the story away and every prisoner's dilemma has one shape. Each side chooses between the collectively beneficial action and the individually beneficial one. Both collective pays A to each, both individual pays D to each, and if one defects while the other cooperates the defector gets B and the cooperator gets C. The ordering that makes it a dilemma is B greater than A, greater than D, greater than C. Any situation whose payoffs fit that ordering is a prisoner's dilemma, whatever it is about.

Slide 12 of 21: Banana Republic and J. Crew

Both price a near-identical blue dress shirt without knowing what the other will charge. At eighty dollars each they make seventy-two thousand dollars each. If one drops to seventy while the other holds, the cheaper firm makes one hundred and ten thousand and the dearer one makes twenty-four thousand. If both drop, each makes seventy thousand, which is the ringed equilibrium. In practice J. Crew cut first, Banana Republic followed, and the price war took both close to bankruptcy.

Slide 13 of 21: Southwest Airlines

Southwest does not allocate seats, so boarding position decides where you sit. It sold EarlyBird check-in for ten dollars, which automatically checked you in early and secured a good seat. Paying was individually rational, so almost everyone paid. Once almost everyone had paid, the advantage disappeared: Southwest now states that it cannot guarantee an A group seat, the price has risen, and the airline collects a large annual revenue from passengers who each bought their way back to where they started. Further reading is linked on the slide.

Slide 14 of 21: Big Tech and AI

Big Tech is building AI capacity faster than it can prove demand for it, and estimates suggest the spending will not peak until 2028, at around one trillion dollars. Davidson Kempner's chief investment officer put the logic plainly: you have to invest in it because your peers are investing in it. A slower build matched to real demand would leave every firm better off. Think of Anthropic's call to pause AI development. Further reading is linked on the slide.

Slide 15 of 21: Cities and subsidies

Firms put governments into this structure deliberately. Cities compete to attract or retain employers with tax breaks and subsidies, and corporations receive at least eighty billion dollars a year from cities this way. When General Motors looked for a home for Saturn in 1985, thirty states made offers. When United Airlines put its headquarters out to bid, ninety cities applied. The Governor of Illinois once called for a truce among competing cities, and almost nobody signed it, because a city that stops bidding while its neighbours continue simply loses the employer. The matrix is the same one you have already seen.

Slide 16 of 21: Repetition alone is not enough

The obvious hope is that facing the same rival repeatedly makes restraint rational. Backward induction says otherwise. If both stations know the game ends at week six, week six has no future to protect, so both cut. That makes week five the last week that matters, so both cut there too, and the reasoning runs back to week one. On this argument cooperation should never appear at all. In practice it appears constantly, so what does the argument leave out?

Slide 17 of 21: The solution, Axelrod's tournament

Robert Axelrod asked under what conditions cooperation emerges among self-interested players with no authority to enforce it. He invited game theorists to submit computer programs, paired every entry against every other, and ran two hundred rounds per pairing. Scoring was three points each for mutual restraint, five for defecting alone, one each for a mutual defection and nothing for cooperating alone. All the best game theorists in the world played each other. The theoretical maximum was one thousand. A pair that cooperated throughout scored six hundred each, and a pair that defected throughout scored two hundred each. So who won?

Slide 18 of 21: Tit for tat won

The tournament was won by Anatol Rapoport with the shortest program submitted, the simplest rules of them all. Open by cooperating and never be the first to defect. If the other side defects, defect once on the next round. After that, do whatever they did on the round before. That is the entire strategy, and it beat every more sophisticated entry in the field.

Slide 19 of 21: Why it won

Four properties. Nice: it never defects first, and none of the low-scoring programs was nice. Forgiving: it holds no grudges and carries no memory of past deceptions beyond the last round. Retaliatory: it answers every defection, so nobody can profit by exploiting it. Clean: it is simple enough that the other side can work out the rule and cooperate with it. No rule is best independent of its environment, and tit for two tats, which absorbs one extra defection before responding, sometimes does better.

Slide 20 of 21: Four lessons

Axelrod's four lessons. Do not be envious, because your own total is the score rather than whether you beat the other side. Do not be the first to defect, because that decision is rarely recovered. Reciprocate in both directions, matching restraint with restraint and a defection with a defection. And do not be too clever, because nobody can cooperate with a strategy they cannot work out.

Slide 21 of 21: Where have you seen this?

Closing discussion. Name one situation in your own strategic interactions that has this structure, and say how you might overcome it. Does repetition matter? Does reputation matter?
