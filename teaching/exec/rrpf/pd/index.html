Applied Game Theory for Deal Teams, Module 4 — Prisoner's Dilemmas and Price Wars

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 13: Title slide

Module 4 of Applied Game Theory for Deal Teams: Price Wars. Prisoner's dilemmas and how to get out of them. Facilitator: Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 13: This session

The session runs one hour and forty-five minutes, from 14:30 to 16:15. Fifteen minutes on the structure, meaning what a prisoner's dilemma actually is. Ten minutes on the setup of the game. Fifty minutes playing The Renewal across six rounds. Twenty minutes of debrief on what the room actually did. Ten minutes on the exits, including where they stop being lawful.

Slide 3 of 13: Two suspects, two rooms

The classic form. Two suspects are held separately and cannot communicate. Each may stay silent or confess. Each cell is (Suspect A, Suspect B). If both stay silent, one year each. If A stays silent and B confesses, twenty-five years for A and freedom for B. If A confesses and B stays silent, freedom for A and twenty-five years for B. If both confess, three years each, which is the ringed equilibrium.

Slide 4 of 13: Why it is a dilemma

Two features together create the trap. First, confessing is the better reply whatever the other side does, which makes it a dominant strategy, so each side reaches it by correct reasoning rather than error. Second, the outcome both sides reach is worse for both than the one they passed over. This is the crucial point: nobody has to be stupid, greedy, or badly informed for this to happen. Rational play alone is sufficient.

Slide 5 of 13: The Renewal, the setup

Two suppliers, Alba and Batia, sell an interchangeable industrial product to the same set of buyers on annual contracts. Six rounds, each representing one annual renewal cycle. Each round, each team privately decides whether to hold its price or undercut the other. Teams have three minutes to agree a position before each round, and decisions are collected in private. The full handout opens on participants' phones.

Slide 6 of 13: The payoffs

The payoff matrix for The Renewal, in millions of pounds of margin. Row player is Alba, column player is Batia. Each cell is (Alba, Batia). If both hold price, plus four each. If Alba holds and Batia undercuts, minus two for Alba and plus seven for Batia. If Alba undercuts and Batia holds, plus seven for Alba and minus two for Batia. If both undercut, plus one each, which is the ringed equilibrium. Note that a price war still leaves each side with something, just far less than holding would have earned.

Slide 7 of 13: How we play

Six rules. First, six rounds, one per renewal cycle. Second, three minutes of team discussion before each round. Third, decisions are given privately to the facilitator and revealed together. Fourth, rounds three and five are the two largest buyers going to market, and all payoffs in those rounds are doubled. Fifth, before each doubled round, the two sides may send one representative each to meet for two minutes, but only if both sides agree to meet; if either declines there is no meeting. Sixth, nothing said in a meeting binds anyone. It is talk, not a contract.

Slide 8 of 13: The board

The running scoreboard, filled in live. Rows for Alba and Batia in each of the two matches, columns for the six rounds and a total. Rounds three and five are shaded to mark the doubled stakes.

Slide 9 of 13: Three benchmarks

Three reference points against which to read the board. Perfect restraint, meaning both sides hold price in every round, pays thirty-two million pounds to each side. A full price war, meaning both undercut in every round, pays eight million to each. And the punishment for misplaced trust: a side that holds every round against an opponent that undercuts every round finishes at minus sixteen million against the other's fifty-six million. Every team in the room finished somewhere between these numbers, and the interesting question is why.

Slide 10 of 13: What actually escapes a dilemma

Four things change the structure rather than the sentiment. Repetition, because a game played again tomorrow is not the game played once. Observation, because a defection nobody notices carries no cost. Reputation, because what the other side expects of you is an asset you build or spend. And enforceable agreement, because a promise a third party can hold you to is no longer merely talk. Notice that none of these require anyone to become more trusting or more generous.

Slide 11 of 13: Where that logic stops

An important qualification. Some ways of escaping a dilemma with a competitor are lawful and some are not, and the difference is not about intention. Lawful: making your own product harder to substitute, competing on terms other than price, publishing your own standard terms unilaterally, holding a walk-away price you set internally, and building a reputation for consistency with your customers. Unlawful: any discussion, agreement, or signal to a competitor about price, capacity, allocation of customers, or bid intentions, whether direct or through an intermediary. If the room chose to meet before the doubled rounds, this is the moment to look closely at what was said, because the pull towards it is exactly the point of the exercise.

Slide 12 of 13: Getting out of a price war

Four takeaways. One, change what is being compared, since a price war is a symptom of a product the buyer thinks is interchangeable. Two, fix the walk-away before entering the room, because a price war is fought by people improvising downwards. Three, do not read a lost deal as evidence of undercutting, since assuming a cut that did not happen is how a war starts by accident. Four, the first mover into a price war captures a gain that is real but temporary, and the structure that follows is not.

Slide 13 of 13: Over to you

Closing discussion. Where does this structure appear in your own work, and what does the room think holds it in place?

Why It’s a Dilemma

A single dominant strategy...
Confessing is the best response whatever the other side does.
...that's worse for both players
Yet the equilibrium they reach is worse for both than the one they could have held jointly.
The "Dilemma" is: how do parties move to the jointly cooperative, "pareto optimal" outcome?
