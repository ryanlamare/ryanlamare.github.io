Applied Game Theory, Module 1 — What Is Game Theory, Anyway?

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 23: Title slide

Module 1 of Applied Game Theory: What Is Game Theory, Anyway? Common knowledge, best response, and equilibrium. Facilitator: Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 23: This morning, in order

The running order, with no clock attached. First, a decision or a game, with a quick poll of the room. Then the types of games we'll cover. Then the first rule of game theory. Then the first game, I'm Thinking of a Number. Then the Battle of Wits. Then the second game, Guess Two-Thirds of the Average. And finally, a discussion of where you are already playing games.

Slide 3 of 23: A decision or a game?

Two figures side by side. A decision involves a unilateral choice made without regard to others' behaviors. A game involves considerations of how the other side will react to the choices made. We care about the games, and we assume three things: everyone has strategies, everyone has payoffs, and everyone is behaving in their own self-interest. Almost every commercial interaction can be structured as a set of strategic games.

Slide 4 of 23: The poll

What is your familiarity level with game theory as a strategic tool? Four options: A, never heard of it before today. B, heard of it, but never used it. C, I know the basics. D, I could be teaching this session. Answered by show of hands.

Slide 5 of 23: The types of games we'll cover

Four pairs of game types. Sequential versus simultaneous. Zero-sum versus non-zero-sum. Single play versus repeat play. Fixed rules and complete information versus manipulable rules and incomplete information.

Slide 6 of 23: Mixing art and science in game theory

Game theory, as a method of strategic thinking, involves a combination of both art and science. The science comes from calculating as many details as possible and quantifying odds or probabilities of success in a given situation. The art comes from an individual's past experiences and instincts for how to behave in a given situation. Good strategic thinkers mix both elements — our first day and a half leans on the science, and tomorrow afternoon we turn to the art.

Slide 7 of 23: Why game theory is so useful — and why it sometimes sucks

Two columns. Why it's useful: it's capable of explaining a vast array of human (and non-human!) behaviors. Originally conceived by mathematicians and economists, it was later applied to military strategy, business, psychology, biology and beyond — the application possibilities are seemingly endless. Why it sometimes sucks: there is a cold, mechanistic rigidity to it, especially with payoff matrices. It may work better in theory than it does in practice. Some of the key assumptions are super hard to meet. It may have been sold as too good to be true.

Slide 8 of 23: The first rule of game theory — it's not (just) about you!

At its core, the game theoretic way of thinking strategically requires that whatever action one side takes must affect the other side, and vice versa. This means that you cannot think one-dimensionally if you want to think strategically. Everything you do has consequences for both you and your opponent, and everything your opponent does has consequences for both of you. And, you both know this to be true! This can be thought of as the requirement for common knowledge.

Slide 9 of 23: Common knowledge and equilibrium

The ultimate goal in game theory is usually to identify an equilibrium point. This is the point at which each player is engaging in a strategy that is the player's best response to the strategies of the other players. Keep in mind, there does not have to be only one equilibrium point. Also keep in mind, an equilibrium point won't always be the most optimal solution. But at its core, game theory explores and finds strategies that generate equilibria.

Slide 10 of 23: Using common knowledge to find equilibrium

A photograph of traffic signals over a road, alongside four lines revealed in turn. I know to stop on red and go on green. You know that I know to stop on red and go on green. So when the light turns green for you, you proceed safely by using common knowledge. And therefore we get a nice equilibrium of stop-and-go traffic.

Slide 11 of 23: Except sometimes game theory sucks

The same photograph, now cropped close on the signals — and every one of them is showing red and green lit at the same time. Look a little closer at the traffic lights. You can actually file lawsuits over this equilibrium failure.

Slide 12 of 23: Game theory gets weird — here are some paradoxes

Eight paradoxes, each tagged with the module of this programme that proves it. It's better to be third than to be first or second (module 3). The leader can be best served by imitating the follower (module 2). The best way to succeed is often to let your competitors do well (module 4). You can give yourself more power when your hands are tied (module 7). The best way to play a game may be to not play at all (module 7). You're frequently better off by refusing to do what your incentives are telling you (module 4). Instability and unpredictability can be better for you than consistency (module 6). You can usually say a whole lot by saying nothing at all (module 8).

Slide 13 of 23: Game number one, I'm Thinking of a Number — the rules

A number line from 1 to 100. Six rules. I'm thinking of a number between 1 and 100. Everyone plays: you each get five guesses, one per round. You lock each guess in privately, and after each round you'll learn whether your guess was too high or too low. Scoring by speed: find my number in round one for 10 points, round two for 8, then 6, 4, 2. No conferring — this one is every player for themselves. This is the first scored game of the programme.

Slide 14 of 23: How to guess the number

A number line from 1 to 100 showing the divide-and-conquer strategy: the most efficient first guess is 50, then 25 or 75 depending on the answer, dividing the remaining range in half each time toward the answer.

Slide 15 of 23: Except…

The same number line with a narrow band highlighted between 44 and 49, and a question mark above it. Divide-and-conquer narrows you to 44 to 49. But we're not in a vacuum — I'm strategic too. So what's the right answer?

Slide 16 of 23: I am strategic, too!

A zoomed number line from 43 to 50. Read the first chapter of the pre-read and you'd say 48 — the textbook pick. But I suggested that book, so I know you've read it. Maybe 47 or 49 is "more clever". And so I picked something more clever still: 46, shown ringed in red.

Slide 17 of 23: Lessons from the number game

Four lessons. Of course, you already know to always put yourself in the other person's shoes! But also know that people are not random in their strategies. You might know something, but I know that you know it — and you should know that I know that you know it, and I should know that you know that I know that you know it, and so on! A good strategist must always be thinking multidimensionally.

Slide 18 of 23: The Battle of Wits

A clip moment: Westley and Vizzini think strategically. The Battle of Wits from The Princess Bride — two goblets, one poisoned, "to the death". Played on the room's screen.

Slide 19 of 23: The Battle of Wits as game theory

Five observations. Technically, this is a zero-sum game: who is right, and who is dead. Vizzini uses common knowledge: I know that you, so I can clearly not choose. Vizzini also tries to change the rules of the game: what in the world is that?! Both sides believe they have information they can use for a competitive advantage, but only one of them actually has asymmetric information. So the equilibrium in this game is that Westley will always beat Vizzini.

Slide 20 of 23: Game number two, Guess Two-Thirds of the Average — the rules

Six rules. Pick any whole number between 0 and 100. Lock it in privately — you get exactly one number. The person whose number is closest to two-thirds of the room's average wins. A worked example: if three people guessed 5, 25 and 60, the average is 30, and two-thirds of 30 is 20, so 25 wins. Remember, you already know the correct answer — but so does everybody else! This one is scored too: the closest guess takes the points.

Slide 21 of 23: Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

An elimination ladder from 100 down to 0, revealed step by step: 100, 67, 45, 30, 20, 13, 9, dot dot dot, 0. How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and the successive elimination of inferior strategies. Let's say everyone guessed 100 — then you should guess two-thirds of 100, which is 67, so we can eliminate 68 or above. But if nobody will play above 67, your winning strategy should be two-thirds of that: 45. And so on, ruling out everything above 30, 20, 13, 9 — all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium. So, all of those who answered 0 got it right… or did they??

Slide 22 of 23: So who won?

Three cells to be filled in live: the room's average, two-thirds of that average, and who guessed closest. And a shout-out to anyone who said 0 — the equilibrium answer!

Slide 23 of 23: Where are you already playing games?

Closing discussion. Which of your regular interactions is really a game rather than a decision? Who are the other players, and what are their payoffs? And which types of game from the list are you already playing?
