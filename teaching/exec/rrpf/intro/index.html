Applied Game Theory, Module 1 — What Is Game Theory, Anyway?

Accessible text version of a twenty-two-slide presentation. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 22: Title slide

Module 1 of Applied Game Theory: What Is Game Theory, Anyway? Facilitator: Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 22: This module

Four pictogram tiles: the first rule of game theory; game number one, I'm Thinking of a Number; the Battle of Wits; game number two, Guess Two-Thirds of the Average.

Slide 3 of 22: A few key assumptions

Three assumptions frame the two days. Strategy is everywhere. You must consider others. Self-interest is rational.

Slide 4 of 22: How to think about strategy

Strategy is studied across three traditions: business schools, labour relations, and economics. This programme is a hybrid that sits at their intersection.

Slide 5 of 22: Game theory as a key tool

Game theory adds five ideas. Consider the other side. It is never a unilateral decision; it is a broader game. It combines economics and psychology, and mixes art and science. Everyone has strategies and payoffs. Everyone tries to give their best response to others.

Slide 6 of 22: Pros and cons of game theory

On balance. Pros: it explains real behaviour and crosses every discipline. Cons: it relies on mechanistic assumptions and is often better in theory than in practice.

Slide 7 of 22: The common knowledge requirement

Game theory assumes mutual awareness: each side's choices affect them both, and each side knows that the other knows it.

Slide 8 of 22: Using common knowledge to find equilibrium

A photograph of traffic signals over a busy road. Caption: I know to stop on red and go on green, and you know that I know it. So we get a nice equilibrium of stop-and-go traffic.

Slide 9 of 22: Except sometimes game theory sucks

The same photograph cropped close on the signals, and every one of them shows red and green lit at the same time. Caption: you can actually file lawsuits over this equilibrium failure.

Slide 10 of 22: Finding equilibrium

An abstract two-by-two matrix; a red ring appears on one cell. The equilibrium is the cell where neither side wants to switch — not always the only one, and not always optimal. Clip played in the room: The Battle of Wits, from The Princess Bride — the equilibrium when one side knows the truth.

Slide 11 of 22: Types of games we'll cover

Games vary along four axes: sequential versus simultaneous; zero-sum versus non-zero-sum; single play versus repeat play; complete information versus incomplete information.

Slide 12 of 22: Game theory is a weird tool — some paradoxes

Five counter-intuitive results. One: it is better to be third than first or second. Two: the leader should imitate the follower. Three: success can mean letting competitors win. Four: tying your hands gives you power. Five: not playing can mean winning.

Slide 13 of 22: Game number one — the rules

A number line from 1 to 100, and five rules. I'm thinking of a number between 1 and 100. Everyone plays: you each get five guesses. One guess per round, locked in privately. After each round, I'll tell you higher or lower. Find it faster, score more: ten points down to two.

Slide 14 of 22: How to guess the number

A number line showing divide and conquer: the most efficient first guess is 50, then 25 or 75, dividing toward the answer.

Slide 15 of 22: Except…

The number line with a band narrowed to 44 through 49 and a question mark. Divide-and-conquer narrows you to 44–49, but we're not in a vacuum — I'm strategic too. So what's the right answer?

Slide 16 of 22: I am strategic, too!

A zoomed line from 43 to 50. Read chapter 1 of The Art of Strategy and you'd say 48, the textbook pick. But I know you've read it — so maybe 47 or 49, "more clever".

Slide 17 of 22: I am strategic, too! (continued)

You'd know I'd know you'd avoid 48 — so 47 or 49 is "more clever"… so I picked something more clever still: 46, ringed in red.

Slide 18 of 22: What are the key lessons?

Four lessons. Always put yourself in the other person's shoes. People aren't random in their strategies. You know something — but I know that you know it… Strategic thinking is always multidimensional.

Slide 19 of 22: Game number two — guess two-thirds of the average

A number line from 0 to 100, and four rules. Pick any whole number between 0 and 100. Lock it in privately. Closest to two-thirds of the room's average wins ten points. Remember, you already know the correct answer — but so does everybody else!

Slide 20 of 22: Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

A number line from 0 to 100 with dots revealed in turn at 100, 67, 45, then 30, 20, 13 and 9, and finally 0 ringed in red. Common knowledge and the successive elimination of inferior strategies run all the way down to 0, the Nash equilibrium. So all of those who answered 0 got it right… or did they??

Slide 21 of 22: So who won?

Three cells to fill in live: the room's average, two-thirds of the average, and the closest guess, which wins ten points. But also, shout out to anyone who said 0, the equilibrium answer!

Slide 22 of 22: Where we land, and what's next

Two columns: the strategic lens — never unilateral, read the other side — and game theory, with its players, payoffs and equilibrium. Module 1 set up the lens; now we start solving real games. Next: Module 2, sequential games.
