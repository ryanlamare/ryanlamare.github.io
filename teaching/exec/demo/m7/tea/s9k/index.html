You are head of buying at Selwood’s, one of the country’s biggest supermarket chains. This winter Selwood’s has promised its shoppers no price rises on everyday essentials, and its adverts say so on buses across the country.

Hartwell’s, the family firm behind the country’s best-selling tea, wants 10% more for every box from Monday. It has just borrowed to build a new packing line, and it has a reputation. Last year Hartwell’s stopped supplying a discount chain that turned down a rise, and its managing director has told the press that every supermarket will pay.

The chief executive wants you to make it plain to Hartwell’s that if it insists, its tea comes off Selwood’s shelves and Selwood’s own-label tea goes in its place. Selwood’s has done it before: two years ago it took a crisp maker’s whole range off its shelves for three months.

The crisp maker was a small brand. Hartwell’s is the best-selling tea in your stores, and your own research shows that one in ten of the people who buy it would do their whole weekly shop somewhere else if it disappeared, many of them for good. That would cost Selwood’s far more than the rise ever could, and the own-label tea is six months away from the shelves.

Hartwell’s head of sales has agreed to see you before the talks end on Friday. Prepare for your meeting.

Your objective: keep Hartwell’s on your shelves at the smallest rise you can.
