SESSION 6 · THE HIRING GAME

A game of backward reasoning in negotiations

  • Two groups of senior managers are looking to hire new employees
  • Budget constraints mean that only a maximum of three employees can be hired
  • Both teams are seeking to have maximum influence on who gets hired
  • There are 21 applicants that are all considered equally strong
SESSION 6 · THE HIRING GAME

How the Hiring Game works

  • I will alternate in asking both teams to identify unsuitable candidates
  • Each round, each team can identify a minimum of 1 and a maximum of 3 unsuitable candidates, and I’ll remove them from the group on the screen
  • When I turn to your team, tell me how many job candidates you find to be unsuitable (1, 2, or 3)
  • The team that gets the final say in who to hire (i.e. is left with the last candidate or group of candidates) wins the game
  • You have 1 minute to decide how many candidates are unsuitable each round

Solving the Hiring Game

Think forward and reason backward: whoever faces four candidates loses. Rule out just one to leave 20 with your first move, then answer every removal back to 16, 12, 8, and 4, and the final say will always be yours!
In backward reasoning, the first-mover advantage can be hugely significant!
Think about making the first offer in negotiations: you control how the game is played!
SESSION 6 · EXAMPLES

A game of backward reasoning on Survivor

  • On Survivor: Thailand, two tribes played for immunity with 21 flags: each turn a tribe took 1, 2 or 3 flags, and the tribe that took the last flag won
  • Sook Jai moved first and took two flags, leaving 19, and Chuay Gahn went on to win immunity
  • Taking one flag and leaving 20 would have won it for Sook Jai, whatever Chuay Gahn did

SESSION 6 · EXAMPLES

Boeing’s “best and final” offer

  • In September 2024, with more than 33,000 machinists on strike, Boeing made what it called its “best and final” offer: a 30% raise over four years, open until Friday
  • The union never put it to a vote: “Boeing does not get to decide when or if you vote”
  • Members turned down 35% in October, and accepted 38% in November, after 53 days on strike
  • The lesson: an ultimatum the other side sees as unfair is likely to be rejected, even when rejecting is costly
SESSION 6 · THE CENTIPEDE GAME

The centipede game

  • £100 is on the table. Player 1 can take it, or pass
  • Each pass adds another £100, and the choice switches sides
  • At £1,000 the game ends, and whoever’s turn it is takes it all
  • Play it in pairs, on your phones; this time whoever’s first name is nearest to Z is Player 1

SESSION 6 · THE CENTIPEDE GAME

The centipede game: the theory

  • Rollback theory: Player 2 will surely take the pot at £1,000, so Player 1 should take it at £900. But Player 2 should know this and take it at £800, and so on, so Player 1 should take the first £100 and end the game
  • The problem: you’re incentivized to wait, because you do better as a group if you wait!
  • A pay-it-forward line is a centipede game: it keeps going only as long as nobody grabs
Negotiations operate like this as well: even if you should settle early, most people wait as long as possible to try to claim as much of the pie as they can

What these games teach us

1
You can begin to see how negotiations can be viewed from a game theoretical lens

The value, but difficulty, of using backward reasoning

SESSION 6

The limits of sequential reasoning

1
Complexity of drawing game trees to completion (even tic-tac-toe is hard!)
2
Requirement of perfect information (in reality, uncertainty plagues us all)
3
Limits of rationality in sequential decision-making (think of the games we just played)
