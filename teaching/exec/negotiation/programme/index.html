executive programme
STRATEGIC
NEGOTIATION
Five days  ·  in person

Negotiation is the process through which two or more parties try to reach a mutually acceptable agreement. Whether we realise it or not, we negotiate every day. These negotiations occur over broad economic items like contracts, budgets, prices and deadlines, but also over smaller-scale concerns like logistics, timings, and preferences. Negotiations also occur across the spectrum of stakeholders with whom we interact on a daily basis, including suppliers, partners, regulators and our own colleagues. At times we feel good about the outcomes of these negotiations, and at times we feel frustrated by our inability to achieve what we want. Yet most of us know little about the strategy and psychology of effective negotiation.

This programme is built to make you a better negotiator, through practice and an improved understanding of the factors that underlie successful negotiations. We will teach you the theory and practice of negotiation in a highly interactive, hands-on way. You will spend much of the programme negotiating with each other using confidential role-play exercises, and then talking about what happened: which deals were reached, why some pairs created far more value than others, and which moves made the difference. You will learn not only what strategies work, but why they work, so that you can apply them to new situations. The programme builds from two-party deals to mediation and a full multi-party negotiation, and uses elements of game theory to explain why capable, rational negotiators so often end up worse off than they need to be.

By the end of the programme, you will have a good understanding of how to both create and claim value in negotiations, the psychological elements that are always present in negotiations, how third parties can help overcome barriers to settlement, the challenges and complexities present when negotiations involve teams and groups, and how to apply principles derived from game theory to your own negotiations.

What makes this programme unique

Learning by negotiating

Every session is built around a live negotiation. Participants record their deals on their phones, and every debrief starts from the room’s own results, so the lessons come from what participants actually did.

A strategic lens

Strategic thinking explains why good negotiators end up in price wars and deadlocks, and how to get out of them. A full day of the programme applies it to negotiation, from prisoner’s dilemmas to commitments, threats and promises.

Grounded in research

The programme draws on research on negotiation and dispute resolution, including my own work in this area, and on evidence about which teaching methods carry over to work.

Proven content

We use classic negotiation exercises along with hand-crafted content developed over years of teaching negotiations to professionals. Exercises include established role plays from Harvard’s Program on Negotiation and Northwestern University’s Kellogg School of Management.

How the programme will run

The programme is derived from classic negotiations courses I have taught to professionals in the United States, delivered in the interactive format used in my other executive programmes.

The programme runs over five days, with two sessions a day, each lasting around three hours with a break. Every session is built around negotiation exercises. Participants receive confidential role briefs, prepare, negotiate in pairs or small groups, record their results on their phones, and then debrief together. The debrief starts from the participants’ own results, concentrating on how the best deals were made. No prior experience with the theory and practice of negotiation is required, and the programme is conducted in the spirit of positive shared learning, where suboptimal outcomes are just as helpful and important to discuss as the ideal outcomes might be.

Before the programme

Preparation

No preparation is required before the first day. On the evening before the last day, participants spend around 30 to 45 minutes reading a confidential brief for the multi-party negotiation.

Participants who would like a grounding beforehand will find one short book useful. It is written for a general audience, and its ideas are taught through the whole week.

Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton, Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In (third edition, Penguin, 2011).

The ten sessions

1

Introduction to Negotiation

Every negotiation relies on alternatives, allowing either side to walk away. This session introduces the ideas that flow throughout the programme: the characteristics and main types of negotiation, each side’s best alternative to a negotiated agreement, and a framework for understanding a negotiation’s context, structure, process, strategy and outcomes. We also look at what most often goes wrong in negotiations, including from participants’ own experiences.

ExerciseSally Soprano. A single-issue negotiation between an agent and an organisation.
ResourcesEssentials of Negotiation, chapter 1; Get Paid What You’re Worth, chapters 1–2; Strategic Negotiations
2

Creating and Claiming Value ISample session›

Most negotiations involve more than one issue, and the parties rarely value every issue equally. This session shows how to find the issues that matter more to one side than to the other, trade across them, and build packages, akin to a non-zero-sum strategic game. It covers the three types of issues (distributive, integrative and congruent), how to create value and then claim your share of it, and the hardball tactics negotiators meet in practice.

ExerciseThe Job Offer. A scored, multi-issue employment negotiation that shows exactly how much value each pair created or left on the table. It is played entirely on phones, from each person’s confidential brief to the deal, and the debrief starts from the whole room’s results.
ExamplesThis American Life’s “Good Guys”, on asking for a “good guy discount”; Michael Scott’s buyout in The Office.
ResourcesEssentials of Negotiation, chapters 2–3; Get Paid What You’re Worth, chapters 3–4; Six Habits of Merely Effective Negotiators
3

Creating and Claiming Value II

Some negotiations are about how to divide a fixed amount, akin to a zero-sum strategic game. This session covers targets and walk-away points, the bargaining zone, anchoring and first offers, how to make concessions, and where bluffing ends and lying begins. It then turns to a dispute inside a long-term business relationship, where the parties’ positions hide the interests underneath them, and practises finding solutions that serve both sides.

ExerciseWalter Manufacturing and GG Engineering. Two neighbouring firms negotiate the sale of a small plot of land between their buildings. The Broken Bizhub. A dispute between a small business and a long-standing customer after a service goes badly wrong, in which the money is only part of the problem.
ExamplesThe turf war between two ice cream vendors in Salem, Oregon, from Epic Magazine’s “The Cold War”.
ResourcesEssentials of Negotiation, chapters 4–5; Get Paid What You’re Worth, chapter 5; The Hidden Traps in Decision Making; Negotiating with a Customer You Can’t Afford to Lose
4

Perception, Persuasion and Communication

Negotiators often see the same situation very differently, and the way an option is framed can change what people will accept. This session covers power and influence, differences in perception, communication and trust, including across cultures, and the biases that most often derail agreements, from anchoring and framing to the endowment effect and escalation of commitment.

ExerciseThe Performance Interview. A performance review between a manager and a member of their team. Hiring a Newtonian. A negotiation between parties from two fictional cultures with very different norms.
ExamplesRevisionist History’s “The Big Man Can’t Shoot”, on why Wilt Chamberlain gave up the free-throw technique that worked for him.
ResourcesEssentials of Negotiation, chapters 6–8; Influence Without Authority; Harnessing the Science of Persuasion; The Necessary Art of Persuasion
5

Third Parties and Negotiation

When two sides cannot settle on their own, a third party can help, and managers often play that role themselves. This session covers when to bring in a third party, the difference between mediation and arbitration, the styles and moves of effective mediators, and how a manager can mediate between colleagues rather than simply deciding who is right.

ExerciseDirty Laundry. A dispute between two people over a small claim, with a mediator. Telepro. A workplace conflict in which a manager has to step in between an employee and a supervisor.
ExamplesThis American Life’s “Let No Court Put Asunder”, in which a divorce mediator explains why the courts make break-ups worse; “Stuck in the Middle”, on parents caught between two strong-willed children.
ResourcesWhen Should We Use Agents?; Someone to Listen; Is There a Mediator in the House?; Keeping It Out of Court; Are You Hearing Enough Employee Concerns?
6

Strategic Thinking and Backward Reasoning in Negotiations

Much of negotiation is strategic: what you should do now depends on how the other side will respond later. This session introduces game theory as a lens on negotiation, and practises thinking forward and reasoning backward in a series of short games, including ultimatums of the kind any negotiator with a weak alternative faces. The games show the value, and the difficulty, of putting yourself in the other side’s shoes.

ExerciseThe Hiring Game. Two teams of managers take turns ruling out job applicants until one team has the final say on who is hired. Then the ultimatum game and the centipede game, played in pairs on phones.
ExamplesFreakonomics Radio’s “Are the Rich Really Less Generous Than the Poor?”
ResourcesThe Art of Strategy, chapters 2 and 11
7

Prisoner’s Dilemmas in Negotiations

Sometimes each side’s best move in negotiations leaves both sides worse off. This session uses game theory’s most famous game, the prisoner’s dilemma, to explain price wars, races to the bottom and the breakdown of cooperation between parties who would both gain from it. We then work through the ways out: making the game repeat, bringing in an enforcer, and changing the payoffs.

ExercisePrice Wars. Teams run rival petrol stations at the same junction and set their prices every week for six weeks, with the chance to meet before the weeks that pay double.
ExamplesAirline price wars; the Silicon Valley no-poaching agreements; Radiolab’s “The Golden Rule” and “Tit for Tat”.
ResourcesThe Art of Strategy, chapter 3; Changing the Game: Bob Axelrod’s Blueprint for Peace
8

Using Coordination and Strategic Moves to Shape Negotiations

Negotiations are often a coordination game: both sides would rather agree than not, but each wants the other to agree on its terms. This session looks at how negotiators coordinate, why common knowledge matters, and how commitments, threats and promises change the game to one side’s advantage, as long as they are credible.

ExerciseThe Common Knowledge Quiz. Then two games played on phones: the Hub Lease, in which an airline and its hub airport each decide whether to hold firm or give ground, and Split or Steal, in which pairs talk before they choose.
ExamplesBlack Mirror’s “Nosedive”; Reagan’s 48-hour ultimatum to the striking air traffic controllers in 1981.
ResourcesThe Art of Strategy, chapters 4, 6 and 7; Boris Johnson’s Brexit Strategy Is Straight Out of Nixon’s Cold War Playbook
9

Negotiating in Groups and Teams

Negotiations that involve teams and groups bring challenges and complexities that two-party deals do not: more issues on the table than any two parties could settle alone, and coalitions forming and breaking as the negotiation goes on. The week’s lessons come together in a single, complex negotiation over a major port development. Participants prepare their confidential roles the evening before.

ExerciseHarborco. A scored, six-party negotiation over a major port development, with government, business, labour and environmental groups at the table.
ExamplesFreakonomics Radio’s “Failure Is Your Friend”, on the Challenger launch decision and the premortem.
ResourcesEssentials of Negotiation, chapters 9–10; Agreement and Thinking Alike
10

Harborco Debrief and Wrap-Up

The final session opens with the results of the multi-party negotiation: how much value each group created, and how the strongest agreements were built. Participants then negotiate one last time, and we close on how negotiating outside the programme differs: interests are not always well defined, the other side has not learned what you have, and sometimes “no” is the best answer.

ExerciseThe Blue Buggy. A short negotiation over the price of a used car.
ResourcesEssentials of Negotiation, chapter 12

Our ultimate goals for this programme

Over the five days, participants will move from the economics of a deal to the psychology of the people making it, and from two-party negotiations to mediation and a full multi-party negotiation. They will practise every idea by negotiating with each other and see their results alongside the rest of the room.

the ideal scenario after five days together
You will leave understanding the value of alternatives, the psychology and economics of negotiation, the traps you might fall into when bargaining, and how to overcome them. You will walk away confident that you can effectively get the other side to “Yes” in your next negotiation.

Practicalities

Format
Five consecutive days, in person, with two sessions a day of around three hours each. A four-day version, without the game theory day, is also possible.
Group size
Ideally 20 to 40 participants. Most exercises are played in pairs or small groups, and the multi-party negotiation in groups of six.
Room
A flat room with movable tables and chairs, breakout spaces nearby so that pairs and small groups can negotiate in private, good wifi, and an audio and video setup for short film clips and live results.
Participants bring
A phone.
Language
Taught in English.
Tailoring
Exercises can be exchanged for others if participants have had negotiation training before, and examples can be chosen to reflect participants’ sectors.
Materials
Licensed exercise materials are provided in print for each participant, and all session materials remain available afterwards at a fixed web address.

About me

I am a Professor of Employment Relations and Human Resource Management at the London School of Economics. I have taught negotiation, workplace dispute resolution, game theory, and collective bargaining for over a decade, at Pennsylvania State University and the University of Illinois, including on the Professional MBA at the Gies College of Business, and my teaching at Illinois received the School of Labor and Employment Relations’ Faculty Teaching Excellence Award three times. I appeared on the university’s List of Teachers Ranked as Excellent by Their Students between 2015 and 2024. My research examines how organisations manage and resolve conflict, including through mediation and arbitration, and I co-edited The Evolution of Workplace Dispute Resolution: International Perspectives (2023). My teaching is deliberately hands-on: participants learn by negotiating with each other and then examining what happened. I also design and teach executive programmes, most recently an applied game theory programme for a corporate client.
