executive programme
STRATEGIC
NEGOTIATION
Five days  ·  in person

Negotiation is the process through which two or more parties try to reach an agreement that each prefers to its alternatives. Whether we realise it or not, we negotiate every day. These negotiations occur over broad economic items like contracts, budgets, prices and deadlines, but also over more small-scale concerns like logistics, timings, and preferences. Negotiations also occur across the spectrum of stakeholders with whom we interact on a daily basis, including suppliers, partners, regulators and our own colleagues. Yet most of us know little about the strategy and psychology of effective negotiation, and we routinely leave value on the table, give ground we did not need to give, or walk away from agreements we should have reached.

This programme is built to make you a better negotiator. We will teach you the theory and practice of negotiation, and will be highly interactive and hands-on in nature. You will spend much of the programme negotiating with each other using confidential role-play exercises, and then examine what happened: which deals were reached, why some pairs created far more value than others, and which moves made the difference. Each session will pair an exercise with short real-world cases and the research behind them, and will close by applying the lesson to participants’ own negotiations. The programme builds from two-party deals to mediation and a full multi-party negotiation, and uses game theory to explain why capable, rational negotiators so often end up worse off than they need to be.

By the end of the programme, you will know how to prepare for a negotiation, claim value without destroying it, create value by trading across issues, recognise the biases and emotions that derail agreements, work across cultures, use third parties well, and manage negotiations with many parties at once.

What makes this programme unique

Learning by negotiating

Every session is built around a live negotiation, and every debrief leads with participants' own results, so the lessons come from what participants actually did.

A strategic lens

Game theory explains why good negotiators end up in price wars and deadlocks, and how to get out of them. Core game theoretic ideas run throughout the programme alongside the psychology of negotiation.

Grounded in research

The programme draws on research on negotiation and dispute resolution, including my own work in this area, and on evidence about which teaching methods carry over to work.

Proven and current CONTENT

Exercises come from Harvard’s Program on Negotiation and Northwestern University’s Kellogg School of Management, and case topics include negotiations from the Gulf region as well as the growing use of AI in negotiation.

How the programme will run

The programme draws on the negotiation and dispute resolution courses I have taught for over a decade, delivered in an interactive format used in my other executive programmes.

The programme runs over five days, with two sessions a day, each lasting around three hours with a break. Every session is built around a negotiation exercise. Participants receive confidential role briefs, prepare, negotiate in pairs or small groups, and then debrief together. The debrief starts from the participants' own results, concentrating on how the best deals were made. No prior experience with the theory and practice of negotiation is required, and the programme is conducted in the spirit of positive shared learning, where suboptimal outcomes are just as helpful and important to discuss as the ideal outcomes might be.

Licensed exercise and case materials are provided in print for each participant. Session materials, polls, results and the exercises played on phones are web pages that participants open on their phones, and they remain available afterwards at a fixed web address.

Before the programme

Preparation

No preparation is required before the first day. On the evening of day four, participants read a confidential brief for the final day’s multi-party negotiation, which takes around 30 to 45 minutes.

Participants who would like a grounding beforehand will find one short book useful. It is written for a general audience, and its ideas are taught through the whole week.

Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton, Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In (third edition, Penguin, 2011).

The ten sessions

1

Negotiation Principles

Every negotiation relies on alternatives, allowing either side to walk away. This session introduces the ideas that flow throughout the programme: the difference between the parties’ positions and their interests, each side’s best alternative to a negotiated agreement, and a framework for understanding a negotiation’s context, process and outcomes. We also look at what most often goes wrong in negotiations, including from participants’ own experiences.

EXAMPLE ExerciseThe Ugli Orange. A short negotiation in which two parties both urgently need the same scarce resource.
CasesThe Pink Towels Case, where a gift from HR sets off an email dispute among staff; Taylor Swift, who re-recorded her first six albums after the original recordings were sold over her objections.
ResourcesSix Habits of Merely Effective Negotiators; Strategic Negotiations; How to Manage Conflict in the Workplace
2

Claiming Value

Some negotiations are about how to divide a fixed amount, akin to a zero-sum strategic game. This session covers targets and walk-away points, the bargaining zone, anchoring and first offers, and how to make concessions. We also look at the hardball tactics negotiators experience in practice, and how to answer them without damaging the relationship.

EXAMPLE ExerciseSally Soprano. A single-issue negotiation between an agent and an organisation.
CasesPitches on Dragons’ Den, where first offers and walk-aways happen on camera; Amazon’s contest for a second headquarters, in which 238 cities bid.
ResourcesA Behavioral Theory of Labor Negotiations; Bargaining (from The Art of Strategy)
3

Creating ValueSample session›

Most negotiations involve more than one issue, and the parties rarely value every issue equally. This session shows how to find the issues that matter more to one side than to the other, trade across them, and build packages, akin to a non-zero-sum strategic game. It also covers contingent agreements, which settle a disagreement about the future by making the outcome depend on what actually happens, so that both sides leave better off.

EXAMPLE ExerciseThe Job Offer. A scored, multi-issue employment negotiation that shows exactly how much value each pair created or left on the table. It is played entirely on phones, from each person’s confidential brief to the deal, and the debrief starts from the whole room’s results.
CasesMichael Scott’s buyout in The Office; Jack Nicholson’s Batman deal.
ResourcesGet Paid What You’re Worth; Six Habits of Merely Effective Negotiators
4

Interests, Not Positions

Negotiators who argue over positions often miss the interests underneath them. Drawing on the principled approach of Getting to Yes and cooperative non-zero-sum solutions to strategic games, this session practises separating the people from the problem, focusing on interests rather than positions, inventing options for mutual gain, and agreeing on objective criteria, particularly in disputes inside long-term relationships.

EXAMPLE ExerciseThe Broken Bizhub. A dispute between a small business and a long-standing customer after a service goes badly wrong, in which the money is only part of the problem.
CasesThe ice cream truck feud from Radiolab’s “Cold War”; two departments arguing over who pays to fix faulty parts.
ResourcesGetting to Yes; Negotiating with a Customer You Can’t Afford to Lose
5

How Negotiations Fail

Even well-prepared negotiators fall into predictable traps. This session covers the biases that most often derail agreements, including framing, anchoring, the “mythical fixed pie”, overconfidence and escalation of commitment. It then turns to emotion, considering how anger, anxiety and the fear of losing face change what people will accept, and how trust can be repaired once it breaks.

EXAMPLE ExerciseAtalanta, a negotiation to repair a business relationship after trust has broken down.
CasesEastern Airlines and the mythical fixed pie; the bidding war for Bloomingdale’s; why basketball’s worst free-throw shooter abandoned the technique that worked.
ResourcesThe Hidden Traps in Decision Making; Negotiating Rationally
6

Culture, Trust and Ethics

Negotiation looks different across cultures, and so do ideas of what is fair, polite and honest. This session covers the cultural differences that matter at the table, including face, honour, directness and trust, and how to adapt without stereotyping. It then turns to ethics, considering where bluffing ends and lying begins, what deception costs, and what changes when the negotiating is handed to someone, or something, else.

EXAMPLE ExerciseHiring a Newtonian. A negotiation between parties from two fictional cultures with very different norms.
CasesFordlandia, Henry Ford’s town in the Amazon; what people instruct AI agents to do on their behalf.
ResourcesWhen Culture Doesn’t Translate; How to Lead Across Cultures, Languages, and Geographies; Harnessing the Science of Persuasion
7

Prisoner’s Dilemmas in Negotiation

Sometimes each side’s best move in negotiations leaves both sides worse off. This session uses game theory’s most famous game, the prisoner’s dilemma, to explain price wars, races to the bottom and the breakdown of cooperation between parties who would both gain from it. We then work through the ways out: making the game repeat, bringing in an enforcer, and changing the payoffs.

EXAMPLE ExercisePrice Wars. Teams run rival petrol stations at the same junction and set their prices every week for six weeks, with the chance to meet before the weeks that pay double.
CasesAirline price wars; why AI companies have asked to be regulated; the Silicon Valley no-poaching agreements.
ResourcesPrisoners’ Dilemmas and How to Resolve Them (from The Art of Strategy); Changing the Game: Bob Axelrod’s Blueprint for Peace; The Golden Rule; Tit for Tat
8

Third Parties and Negotiation

When two sides cannot settle on their own, a third party can help, and managers often play that role themselves. This session covers when to bring in a third party, the difference between mediation and arbitration, the styles and moves of effective mediators, and how a manager can mediate between colleagues rather than simply deciding who is right.

EXAMPLE ExerciseTelepro. A workplace conflict in which a manager has to step in between an employee and a supervisor.
CasesQatar Airways and Airbus: a very public dispute and a private settlement; Rosie Bracken’s grievance over her injury record, and how a coal-industry mediator settled it.
ResourcesThe Mediation Process; Is There a Mediator in the House?; Let No Court Put Asunder; Stuck in the Middle
9

Multi-Party Negotiation

The week’s lessons come together in a single, complex negotiation. Several parties must reach agreement on a major port development, with more issues on the table than any two of them could settle alone, and with coalitions forming and breaking as the negotiation goes on. Participants prepare their confidential roles the evening before.

ExerciseHarborco. A scored, six-party negotiation over a major port development, with government, business, labour and environmental groups at the table.
CasesFootball’s European Super League, which twelve clubs announced in 2021 and abandoned within days.
ResourcesAgreement and Thinking Alike; Failure Is Your Friend
10

Putting Negotiations to Work

The final session opens with the results of the multi-party negotiation: how much value each group created, and how the strongest agreements were built. We then look ahead at how artificial intelligence is beginning to negotiate on organisations’ behalf, and how to use it well when preparing. Participants finish by planning a real negotiation of their own with the tools of the week.

EXAMPLE ExerciseParticipants work through an upcoming negotiation of their own in small groups, using the planning tools from the week.
CasesWalmart’s use of AI to negotiate with suppliers; what 180,000 negotiations between AI agents reveal about warmth and toughness.
ResourcesInfluence Without Authority; The Necessary Art of Persuasion

Our ultimate goals for this programme

Over the five days, participants will move from the economics of a deal to the psychology of the people making it, and from two-party negotiations to mediation and a full multi-party negotiation. They will practise every idea by negotiating with each other, see their results alongside the rest of the room, and leave with a plan for a real negotiation of their own.

the ideal scenario after five days together
Participants leave understanding the value of alternatives, the psychology and economics of negotiation, the traps they might fall into when bargaining, and how to overcome them. You will walk away confident that you can effectively get the other side to "Yes" in your next negotiation.

Practicalities

Format
Five consecutive days, in person, with two sessions a day of around three hours each. A four-day version is also possible.
Group size
Ideally 20 to 30 participants. Most exercises are played in pairs or small groups, and the multi-party negotiation in groups of six.
Room
A flat room with movable tables and chairs, breakout spaces nearby so that pairs and small groups can negotiate in private, good wifi, and an audio and video setup for short film clips and live results.
Participants bring
A phone, and one real negotiation from their own work to plan in the final session.
Language
Taught in English.
Tailoring
Exercises can be exchanged for others if participants have had negotiation training before, and cases can be chosen to reflect participants’ sectors.
Materials
Licensed exercise and case materials are provided in print for each participant, and all session materials remain available afterwards at a fixed web address.

About me

I am a Professor of Employment Relations and Human Resource Management at the London School of Economics. I have taught negotiation, workplace dispute resolution, game theory, and collective bargaining for over a decade, at Pennsylvania State University and the University of Illinois, including on the Professional MBA at the Gies College of Business, and my teaching at Illinois received the School of Labor and Employment Relations’ Faculty Teaching Excellence Award three times. My research examines how organisations manage and resolve conflict, including through mediation and arbitration, and I co-edited The Evolution of Workplace Dispute Resolution: International Perspectives (2023). My teaching is deliberately hands-on: participants learn by negotiating with each other and then examining what happened.
