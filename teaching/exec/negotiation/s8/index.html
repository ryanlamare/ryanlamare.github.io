SESSION 8 · COMMON KNOWLEDGE

The first rule of game theory: it’s not (just) about you!

  • At its core, the game theoretic way of thinking strategically requires that whatever action one entity does must affect the other side and vice versa
  • This means that you cannot think one-dimensionally if you want to think strategically
  • Everything you do has consequences for both you and your opponent
  • Equally, everything your opponent does has consequences for both you and your opponent
  • AND…you both know this to be true! This can be thought of as the requirement for common knowledge

SESSION 8 · CHICKEN

Chicken

  • Two drivers head straight at each other. Whoever swerves first loses face, and whoever holds the line wins
  • If both swerve, nobody wins and nobody loses
  • If neither swerves, both crash – the worst outcome for both of them
  • Two equilibria again – one swerves, one goes straight – but each of you wants to be the one who goes straight

Solving games of chicken

  • There are two equilibria here but the incentive to go straight results in a catastrophic outcome if you can't coordinate
  • So how do you win?
  • One way is to convince the other side that under no circumstances will you swerve – we’ll return to commitment and credibility later when we talk about strategic moves
  • Another way is to alternate swerve/straight strategies if you play a repeated game (knowing if a game repeats is crucial)

SESSION 8 · STRATEGIC MOVES

What is a strategic move?

A strategic move changes the game to your advantage
By acting before the game begins, and specifically by moving in a way the other side can see, you can change what they will rationally do
Observable
Otherwise they won’t know what changed
Irreversible
Or at least it has to look that way
THREE KINDS
Commitment
“I'll do X, whatever you do”
The catch: you can’t back out, even when you’d like to
Threat
“Unless you do X, I'll hurt you”
The catch: if it fails, you have to carry it out
Promise
“If you do X, I'll help you”
The catch: if it works, you have to pay up
A COMMITMENT IS UNCONDITIONAL. A THREAT OR A PROMISE IS “IF THIS, THEN THAT”
SESSION 8 · CREDIBILITY

Credibility

  • Strategic moves are only as valuable as they are credible
  • Commitment devices don’t work unless the other side actually believes them to be authentic
  • In August 1981, President Reagan gave the striking air traffic controllers 48 hours to return to work
  • Then he fired more than 11,000 of them
  • The catch with a threat: if it fails, you have to carry it out

SESSION 8 · EXAMPLES

Pfizer’s final offer for AstraZeneca

  • In 2014, Pfizer offered £55 a share for AstraZeneca, about £69 billion, and called it final
  • To the UK’s Takeover Panel, “when you say it’s final it’s supposed to be final”, so Pfizer could not raise it
  • AstraZeneca held out for at least £58.85 a share, so Pfizer had to drop its bid, and wait six months before it could try again
  • The rules made Pfizer’s commitment impossible to undo: it was credible, and when it failed there was no deal

Commitment and brinkmanship

  • If neither side is able to back down, we run into the problem of escalation of commitment
  • Parties may remain committed to their positions even though it’s no longer in their self-interest to do so

SESSION 8 · CREDIBILITY

Credibility and reputation

Strategic moves are only as good as they are credible!
  • Baseball’s owners locked out the players, and on 1 March 2022 the players rejected the owners’ “best and final offer”
  • Opening day was cancelled, for the first time in 27 years
  • Nine days later, the players accepted a later offer, so the “best and final offer” was not final after all
Can be strategically advantageous to develop a certain reputation. But you can also get a reputation for not being credible!
Contracts are designed to ensure credibility, but reputation can work both ways
