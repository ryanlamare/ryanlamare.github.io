SESSION 6

Introduction to non-zero sum games

Recall that zero-sum games require that, in order for one side to win, the other must lose
Zero-sum
A fixed pie where value must be claimed
Non-zero-sum
An unfixed pie where value can be created
These are sometimes called “variable sum” games because each player’s payoffs can vary – unlike “constant sum” games
The variance in each side’s payoffs depends on the negotiation strategies each utilizes
This lesson teaches the most famous non-zero-sum game of them all and applies it to negotiation strategies

What the urgent memo changed in months 7 and 8

  • If trust broke down, both countries would sink to $10, worse for both than holding at $30
  • The urgent memo quadrupled the prize for undercutting alone, from $m to $m
  • The most clever play: take turns, one country undercuts in month 7 and the other in month 8 ($m each, against $m each for holding at $30)
  • But month 8 rewards betrayal (the country that undercut in month 7 gets $m by cutting to $, instead of $m by holding), so the trade is full of risk!

The solution: Axelrod’s tournament

1,000The maximum Defecting every round against a rival who never does.
600Mutual cooperation Two hundred rounds of restraint on both sides.
200Mutual defection Two hundred rounds of price war.
Every submitted program played every other, two hundred rounds each, scoring 3 for mutual restraint, 5 for defecting alone, 1 for a mutual defection and 0 for cooperating alone. Some of the best game theorists and negotiators in the world played each other. Who won?

HOW DOES t4t OVERCOME THE PD FOR NEGOTIATORS?

Nice
It never defects first. No low scorers in the tournament had that property.
Forgiving
It holds no grudges and has no memory of past deceptions.
Retaliatory
It answers every defection, so nobody can profit by exploiting it.
Clear
Simple enough that the other side can read the rules and cooperate.
Keep in mind that no rule is best independent of its environment. Tit for two tats, which absorbs one extra defection before answering, sometimes does better.

SESSION 6 · TAKEAWAYS

a few key takeaways...

What is a PD IN NEGOTIATIONS?
Each side’s dominant strategy leaves both sides worse off
Why do negotiations end up there?
Each side protects itself, and both lose what they could have shared
How do you get out of it?
Make it repeat, bring in an enforcer, change the payoffs
Why is tit for tat the best?
Nice, forgiving, retaliatory, and clear
What about envy of others?
Your own total is the key, not beating the other side
Cooperative solutions to PDs in negotiations aren’t inherently “good”, and defect solutions aren’t inherently “bad”!

SESSION 6 · EXAMPLES

A prisoner’s dilemma in COFFEE SHOP NEGOTIATIONS

  • Larry David opens a coffee shop, Latte Larry’s, right next door to Mocha Joe’s, just to spite its owner
  • Opening right next door means both cafés now fight over exactly the same customers (a classic value claiming problem)
  • Then the price war starts: “A dollar thirty a cup? We’re not going to make any money doing that!” “What choice do I have?”
  • Cutting the price is each café’s dominant negotiation strategy, so both end up worse off -- this is a prisoner’s dilemma

Robert Axelrod on live and let live

  • In the First World War, artillery on both sides deliberately fired between the other side’s first and second trench lines, to show it could be accurate without doing damage
  • Each side did it in the hope that the other would do the same
  • It worked because the same small units faced each other for long periods of time
