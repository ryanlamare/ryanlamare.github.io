## 2. The four questions

Notes, not slide text. Where a note suggests a change it is a question for you.

### Are there too many phone games, and how is the balance?

**The count is fine, and so is where they fall.**

- Phone moments: m1 three, m2 four, m3 three, m4 two, m5 four, m6 three (four with rock paper scissors), m7 four, m8 two. About twenty-five over two days, twelve on day one and thirteen or fourteen on day two, roughly one every half hour. After the plain pass that is a reasonable number for this format.
- **Modules 5 and 6 (corrected 20 Sep).** An earlier draft called day two morning crowded and the formats repetitive. Withdrawn. Module 5's three games share one procedure (vote, a volunteer speaks, vote again) because that is the experiment: the procedure is held constant across stag hunt, battle of the sexes and chicken so the room sees what communication does under each set of payoffs. Changing the format would hide the comparison. Module 6's three games each make a different point (an opponent that learns you, a human opponent, payoffs where the right mix is not fifty-fifty). And apart from Price Wars, which the prisoner's dilemma module needs, every game in the suite can be skipped by pressing next, so none of this constrains the day.
- **Day one is well spread** and varied: a whole-room guess, an auto-answered offer, three different pair games, a static solver, and Price Wars, which is the best thing in the suite for this room because the phone is a card and the game happens between people.
- **Module 8 is the light one:** two phone moments, and Hidden Agenda puts phones in eleven hands while the rest watch.
- The three games now run without the network (Price Wars, the engine matrix, the car market) and the auction run out loud are the pass's best decisions. The evidence so far says: when a game can be played between people, it should be.

**On discussion and "so what for your business" (withdrawn 20 Sep).** An earlier draft said discussion of their world was thin, put it at ten percent, and said every reader independently found no so-what after any game. All three claims are withdrawn. The percentage came from the guessed minutes. The readers were not independent: Claude's brief told each of them to flag any game without a business so-what, so eight reports echoed one instruction. And the finding misreads the design. Each module stages the game first, names the theory after, and then closes on a discussion of their world, which is the point at which the room has the vocabulary to apply it. That closer is the application, once a module, on purpose; it is what the week of 14 to 20 Sep built. The training is in game theory and the debrief after each game is about the game. Nothing needs adding.

- Longest stretches of slides without a room activity, for reference only: m1 slides 5 to 14, m3 slides 8 to 14, m4 slides 7 to 14, m8 slides 9 to 15 and the rules slides 17 to 24.
- **Clips:** about seventeen, all needing YouTube on the venue network (the Krul clip opens a new window and the deck loses the screen). They are overwhelmingly American and old: The Princess Bride, Rebel Without a Cause, Footloose, Mad Men, Moneyball twice, the Super Bowl, Bush in 1988, Clinton in 1992. Footloose and Rebel make the same joke a session apart. Golden Balls and Krul are the two that belong to this room.

### What may not land

In rough order of how much it matters.

1. **Competition law in module 4.** The room rehearses price-setting meetings, and the model answer to "what does cooperating look like?" is a no-poach agreement, with one line on slide 23 that the DOJ punished it. Senior people in a concentrated lessor market will wonder where tacit coordination ends and a cartel begins. One sentence from you early, before someone's general counsel asks, would do.
2. **The examples are American and consumer.** J. Crew and Banana Republic (in dollars; Banana Republic left the UK in 2016), CVS and Rite Aid, Amazon HQ2 on a US map, US television airtime law, Campeau and Macy's, Polaroid, parking meters and the police, Plattsburgh as an airline hub. Module 7 has no example from aviation, leasing or finance at all. Their world appears in the engine game, the inspection game and Boeing, JetBlue, the merged booking system and the hub lease: five of the strongest beats in the suite, and only one is on day one.
3. **Things a UK room will read wrongly.** m3 slide 16, Application to politics: a blue donkey on "entirely Liberal" and a red rhino on "entirely Conservative", the reverse of British colours. "Soccer" in titles in m3 and m6; "overtime"; "football plays" meaning American football; checkers and tic-tac-toe; "résumé"; "gunners in law schools"; "pop quiz".
4. **Classroom residue.** "Learning objectives" on every second slide; m1 slide 17, "the pre-course reading (if you read it, that is!)", which needs a reading to exist; m1 slides 6 and 7, business schools against economics departments and three dictionary definitions; m8 slide 13, the pass/fail grade and the student; m5 slide 13, "Let's play a game where you can win lots of money!"; the phones' "flag the instructor".
5. **People who know the games.** Many in this room will have met the prisoner's dilemma, the ultimatum game and the dollar auction. One person explaining the auction before it starts ends it. Worth deciding in advance what you say to the hand that goes up.
6. **Small exposures.** The auction's two top bidders, by design, though the slide does not say the money is not real. m1's offers board shows each name and amount for a second or two. m2's smoking poll then tells "N of you" the plan will fail. m6's inspection phone says GOT AWAY WITH IT about skipping safety steps, in front of aviation people. None is serious; all are yours to call.
7. **Where someone in the room may know more than the slide.** Boeing and the FAA (m6 slide 20); Campeau bought Federated, Bloomingdale's parent, not Bloomingdale's from Macy's (m7 slide 23); "the war took both close to bankruptcy" (m4 slide 11) looks apocryphal; the Clinton line on m6 slide 3 is, as far as I recall, from a 1995 STRATCOM paper, not his words; m3 slides 9 to 11 cast "the Japanese" as the adversary in a business with many Japanese investors.
8. **Hidden Agenda's watchers, a small point.** (Corrected 20 Sep: an earlier draft ranked this first and called it a risk; that was overstated. Werewolf is proven and Ryan has run it in person and online. "Never run" in section 6 is about the edited code, not the game.) With one table, ten to nineteen people watch. The days are theatre and hold an audience; the nights are silent, about ninety seconds each. Whether the watchers want a job is his to judge.

**What clearly will land:** Price Wars held up across a room; the engine game; the inspection game into Boeing; "do you judge your people on their decisions, or on their outcomes?" (m6 slide 14, the best executive line in the suite); the smoking tree; the ultimatum and centipede with their one-line lessons; Axelrod's ranking and "Always Defect beat it in every game they played, and finished last"; Golden Balls; the Sun Tzu vote; the car market face to face; the eight Takeaways.

### What is usually there and is not

No evaluation, as agreed. Beyond that:

- **An opening ten minutes about them.** Who is in the room and what they want from two days. Every closer depends on their world, and the programme never asks about it until minute 75. Spoken, no slide needed.
- **Ground rules.** One spoken line on what stays in the room, and that nothing anyone taps is kept. It matters more here than in a class because colleagues and their managers are playing against each other by name.
- **Something to leave with.** Nothing is on paper and the decks are unlisted web pages. A one-page summary, a short reading list (the 550 sources), or the slides as a PDF. The 14 Sep audit raised this; still open.
- **A morning look back on day two.** Module 5 opens on the plan for the day. Five minutes of "what stayed with you from yesterday?" costs nothing and tells you what landed.
- **A line for the change of seat.** They bid in the engine game and sit as the airline's committee in Hidden Agenda. Nothing says so (14 Sep finding, still open).
- **A questions slot.** None on day two. The Takeaways slide could hold it if you let it.
- **Module wraps.** m4, m7 and m8 have no What did we learn?; the other five do. m4 has Four lessons and m8 has What was hidden?, so m7 is the one that simply stops.

### What could be better, cheapest first

1. The fixes in section 1.
2. Swap one or two American examples for ones from their trade where you have them to hand, m7 first. Your examples and your words, not Claude's.
3. "In pairs" on the m1, m2 and m3 closers against "In twos or threes" from m4 on: a room of 21 to 30 is often odd.
