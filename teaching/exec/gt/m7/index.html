Applied Game Theory, Module 7 — Credibility, Commitment, and Strategic Moves

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 24: Title slide

Credibility, commitment, and strategic moves. Module 7 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 24: Introduction

One of the most easily challengeable assumptions in strategic thinking is that we are rational. We now consider the value of commitment, credibility, and other strategic instruments or strategic moves that are geared toward overcoming irrationality. We'll then consider another major challengeable assumption in strategic thinking, which is that we always have perfect information.

Slide 3 of 24: Strategic moves

Recall sequential strategies. Smoking — my preferential outcome would be to smoke now, then quit. But Future Ryan is an addict and his preferential outcome is to keep smoking. Therefore, we concluded that I should never smoke in the first place. But what we were really saying was, I lack the willpower to overcome temptation. Instead of being stuck with irrationality, we can manipulate a game to our advantage.

Slide 4 of 24: Strategic moves

We cover three types of strategic moves: commitments, threats, and promises. We will also consider credibility — one must make strategic commitments, threats, and promises credible or else they will not be believed. While much of game theory is science, credibility lends itself more to art. And, there can be very dramatic consequences if some strategic moves are actually played out to their limits.

Slide 5 of 24: Strategic moves

A necessary condition of strategic moves is that they are both observable and irreversible (or give the appearance of irreversibility). These conditions ensure that the entity is a true "first mover" — otherwise, if there is any doubt about either of these conditions, the respondent can become the first mover. There are two broad types of strategic moves: unconditional (commitment strategies) and conditional (threats, promises — efforts to induce compellence or deterrence).

Slide 6 of 24: Unconditional strategic moves: commitment

Player A declares at the outset of a game, "In the game to follow, I will make a particular move, X". The declaration says that Player A's move is unconditional — A will do X irrespective of whatever B does. What are the effects of such a declaration? In sequential move games, it changes the order of the interaction, so that A has now moved first. In simultaneous move games, it limits options and changes Player B's beliefs about the consequences of his/her actions. We've seen this before, but have not explicitly modeled the effects of commitment strategies.

Slide 7 of 24: Changing the rules of the game again

Recall the added value card game from our first session: one white card for every yellow card, and $100 for a pair. Let's play it again under its original rules. Say there's a new stipulation: I'll let the first one of you who raises their hand leave the room and I'll guarantee that person the best deal given to any of the other people in the room. Does the rest of the room accept this deal or not? Think about your own self interest! On the right, a drawn figure: a person stepping out through a door with a red arrow, captioned first hand up gets the best deal.

Slide 8 of 24: Why I became a fierce negotiator

The debrief, after the third game. That deal looked tempting — except it turned me into a fierce negotiator with everyone. On the right, a drawn figure: a deal bar locked at fifty dollars each, with a padlock on the divider, captioned every deal, zero flexibility.

Slide 9 of 24: Strategic moves and chicken

How does Ren induce Chuck to swerve in Footloose? Was this the most effective commitment strategy Ren could have followed? Recall the two conditions of a commitment strategy. What other commitment strategies might Ren have used instead? Throw the steering wheel out or tie the wheel in place. Develop a reputation over the course of several games for never swerving.

Slide 10 of 24: Chicken revisited

The chicken matrix again — Ren against Chuck, swerve or straight, payoffs 0,0; minus 10, plus 10; plus 10, minus 10; minus 100, minus 100. A step adds the annotation: THROWS HIS STEERING WHEEL OUT THE WINDOW — Ren's straight row is now the only row, and Chuck's best response is to swerve.

Slide 11 of 24: Commitment and sleep

The resolute Night Self versus the weak-willed Morning Self. The Morning Self has second-mover advantage. We can express the Night Self's choice to set an alarm or not set an alarm at night against the Morning Self's options to stay in bed or get up.

Slide 12 of 24: Commitment and sleep, the matrix

Night Self (rows: no alarm, set alarm) against Morning Self (columns: stay in bed, get up). No alarm/stay in bed: 0, 10. No alarm/get up: 10, 0. Set alarm/stay in bed: minus 2, minus 5. Set alarm/get up: 8, minus 1.

Slide 13 of 24: Commitment and sleep

How can the Night Self change the game to get first-mover advantage? We've expressed the payoffs here as if the choices are simultaneous, in which case the Night Self should never set an alarm — but in reality, this is a sequential game. In the sequential game, the Night Self can deliberately limit its options so that it sets an alarm, therefore forcing the Morning Self to choose to Get Up.

Slide 14 of 24: Commitment and sleep, committed

The same matrix with a speech bubble: HEY GOOGLE, SET AN ALARM FOR 7AM. The set-alarm row is highlighted and the morning self's get-up cell is ringed.

Slide 15 of 24: Conditional strategic moves: threats and promises

Conditional strategic moves require you to fix a response rule or reaction function: "If this, then that". By declaring a conditional move, Player A necessarily assumes the role of second-mover and thereby cedes the first move to the other side. Two conditional moves on which we focus: threats ("Unless you do what I want, I will respond in a way that will hurt you") and promises ("If you do what I want, I will respond in a way that will help you"). Your parents have probably played conditional strategic games with you!

Slide 16 of 24: Deterrence, compellence, threats, and promises

A two-by-two grid: threat and promise rows against goals of compellence and deterrence. Threat aimed at compellence: hostage takers, etc. Threat aimed at deterrence: mutually assured destruction, etc. Promise aimed at compellence: prosecutors offering deals, etc. Promise aimed at deterrence: mob bosses offering deals, etc. Two problems appear beside the rows: PROBLEM: CONSEQUENCES! for threats and PROBLEM: COMPLIANCE! for promises.

Slide 17 of 24: Conditional moves and the prisoner's dilemma

Let's return to the example of J. Crew and Banana Republic in their effort to price a totally normal blue shirt. Recall that we have a PD situation — both BR and J. Crew are incentivized to cut their prices even though doing so is not optimal for either company. One solution is to introduce a "match or beat the competition" clause. Many argue that these clauses actually hurt customers because they are an implicit conditional move communicated by one company to another. "If you lower your prices, I'll match whatever price you set." And therefore, prices remain high and the incentive to defect is removed.

Slide 18 of 24: Credibility

Strategic moves are only as valuable as they are credible. Commitment devices don't work unless the other side actually believes them to be authentic. Think about strategic moves and sleep — my Night Self can set the alarm, which will force my Morning Self to wake up. Except…

Slide 19 of 24: Commitment and sleep, except

The same matrix with two speech bubbles: the crisp HEY GOOGLE, SET AN ALARM FOR 7AM — and a trailing-off HEY GOOGLE, SET AN ALARM LIKE 9:35AM OR WHATE… The commitment device fails if it isn't credible.

Slide 20 of 24: Credibility and reputation

Much more useful in repeated games than in single-play situations. Can be strategically advantageous to develop a certain reputation. But you can also get a reputation for not being credible! Contracts are designed to ensure credibility, but reputation can work both ways.

Slide 21 of 24: Is commitment always a good thing?

Sometimes commitment strategies can be very beneficial — limiting options ensures that you will follow through with whatever position you've attached yourself to. But sometimes commitment can be detrimental. In the game of chicken — what if both Ren and Chuck threw the wheels out of the car simultaneously? Escalation of commitment can easily lead to brinkmanship.

Slide 22 of 24: Commitment and brinkmanship

If neither side is able to back down, we run into the problem of escalation of commitment. Parties may remain committed to their positions even though it's no longer in their self-interest to do so. "Throwing good money after bad."

Slide 23 of 24: Credibility and irreversibility

Forcing yourself into a strategic move that you cannot undo is an extreme form of credibility. Cortes scuttles his ships. Polaroid's refusal to invest in anything other than instant cameras. Of course, the consequences of all of these actions are, deliberately, severe.
