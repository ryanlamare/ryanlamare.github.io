Module 7: Strategic moves. Credibility, commitment, and strategic moves

The full text of every slide, in order, for screen readers and for anyone who wants it as prose.

Slide 1 of 28: Title slide

Strategic moves. Credibility, commitment, and strategic moves. Module 7 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme, prepared for RRPF.

Slide 2 of 28: Learning objectives

One: see how strategic moves fundamentally change game outcomes. Two: return to our earlier games and reshape them using commitments, threats, and promises. Three: strategic moves are only as valuable as they are credible. Four: cautionary tales with strategic moves.

Slide 3 of 28: What is a strategic move?

A strategic move changes the game to your advantage. Two conditions: it has to be observable, otherwise they won't know the game has changed, and irreversible, or at least it has to look that way. Three kinds: a commitment, "I will do X, whatever you do"; a threat, "unless you do what I want, I will hurt you"; and a promise, "if you do what I want, I will help you", drawn as a raised hand. A commitment is unconditional; threats and promises are conditional.

Slide 4 of 28: Strategic moves in sequential games

Recall sequential strategies: in some games it pays to move first, in others it pays to move second. A commitment is a way of taking the first move, whatever the order of play says. A threat or a promise fixes your response in advance; that makes you the second mover, and hands the first move to the other side. Strategic moves allow you to deliberately claim first or second mover advantage. On the right, two rows of cards joined by red arrows: commitment, "I will do X, whatever you do", leads to first mover, you move, they respond; threat or promise, "if this, then that", leads to second mover, they move, you respond by your rule.

Slide 5 of 28: Strategic moves and smoking

Recall sequential strategies, and smoking. Future Ryan is an addict, so we concluded that I should never smoke in the first place. But what we were really saying was, I lack the willpower to overcome temptation. So we made a strategic move to commit to not smoke, claiming FMA. On the right, the smoking game tree from the second session: Today's Ryan decides whether to smoke; if he does, Future Ryan decides whether to keep smoking. The plan to smoke then quit lights up first, then the quit branch is struck out because Future Ryan keeps smoking, and the red path moves to never starting.

Slide 6 of 28: Changing the rules of the game again

Recall the added value card game from our first session: one white card for every yellow card, and $100 for a pair. Let's make a strategic move to change the rules of the game. I'll let the first one of you who raises their hand leave the room, and I'll guarantee that person the best deal given to any of the other people in the room. Does the rest of the room accept this deal or not? Think about your own self interest! On the right, a drawn figure: a person stepping out through a door with a red arrow.

Slide 8 of 28: Why I became a fierce negotiator

That deal looked tempting, except it turned me into a fierce negotiator with everyone. The first hand up was a strategic move, and it was made against me: everyone saw it, and I could not take it back. On the right, a drawn figure: a deal bar locked at fifty dollars each, with a padlock on the divider, captioned every deal, zero flexibility.

Slide 9 of 28: Strategic moves have unintended consequences

In the United States, television stations must sell candidates airtime at the lowest rate they give any advertiser. Congress made a strategic move on the candidates' behalf: every candidate gets the best deal in the room. So the stations became fierce negotiators with everyone; the discounts disappeared, and the lowest rate went up. The candidates ended up paying more, not less. On the right, a drawn television showing a candidate at a podium, captioned lowest rate, guaranteed, and beside it a price list, peak, early, late and special, where every price below peak is struck through in red on the step.

Slide 10 of 28: Let's play chicken

Pair up. You are two drivers heading straight at each other, and you play six rounds. Each round, at the same time, you swerve or you go straight, for the payoffs on the right. Whoever is ahead in the pair after six rounds takes five points. A QR code takes phones to the game, with a count of pairs in.

Slide 11 of 28: Who swerved?

The live board for the chicken game. Two rows of three tiles. Rounds one to three, no wheel to throw: how often drivers swerved, how many rounds ended in a crash, how many rounds both swerved. Rounds four to six, when a driver could throw the steering wheel out of the window before the round: how many drivers threw it, how often a driver facing a thrown wheel swerved, and how many rounds both drivers threw the wheel, every one of them a crash.

Slide 13 of 28: Commitment and sleep

The resolute Night Self versus the weak-willed Morning Self. The Morning Self has second-mover advantage. In a standard game, the Morning Self should stay in bed, and the Night Self shouldn't set an alarm. But this is actually a sequential game, where the Night Self can deliberately limit its options so that it sets an alarm, forcing the Morning Self to choose to get up. On the right, the matrix: Night Self rows, no alarm and set alarm, against Morning Self columns, stay in bed and get up: 0, 10; 10, 0; minus 2, minus 5; 8, minus 1. The no-alarm, stay-in-bed cell is outlined first; then a speech bubble says "Hey Google, set an alarm for 7am", the no-alarm row fades, and the set-alarm, get-up cell is boxed.

Slide 14 of 28: Credibility

Strategic moves are only as valuable as they are credible. Commitment devices don't work unless the other side actually believes them to be authentic. Think about strategic moves and sleep: my Night Self can set the alarm, which will force my Morning Self to wake up. Introducing Clocky, the alarm that rolls off the nightstand and makes the Morning Self chase it. On the right, two speech bubbles: the crisp "Hey Google, set an alarm for 7am", and a trailing "Hey Google, set an alarm for like 9:35am or whatever"; beneath them the Clocky clip, click to play.

Slide 15 of 28: Credibility and irreversibility

Forcing yourself into a strategic move that you cannot undo is an extreme form of credibility. Three pictogram tiles. Cortés, 1519: lands in Mexico and scuttles his own ships; with no way home, his men have one option left. Polaroid: committed everything to one device, the instant camera; when digital arrived there was no way back. Dr. Strangelove: a doomsday device that fires on its own if attacked, and cannot be switched off. Of course, the consequences of all of these actions are, deliberately, severe.

Slide 16 of 28: Let's play split or steal

Pair up. A £1,000 pot, and each of you holds two golden balls: split, or steal. Talk it over first; say whatever you like. Then choose, at the same time, on your phones. Both split, £500 each. One steals, they take the lot. Both steal, nothing. A QR code and the payoff matrix are on the right.

Slide 17 of 28: Split or steal?

The live board. Round one, talk then choose: how often people split, how many pairs both split, how many pots were stolen, how many pairs both stole. Round two, when one of each pair had Nick's move: how many used it, how the partners facing it chose, how many who said it then split anyway, and how many who stole then handed over half.

Slide 18 of 28: Nick and Ibrahim

Golden Balls, 2012: Nick and Ibrahim, and a pot of £13,600. Nick tells Ibrahim he is going to steal, no matter what, and promises to split the money with him after the show. Nick grabs first-mover advantage and declares an unconditional strategic move. Was Ibrahim going to split without Nick's strategic move? On the right, the clip, click to play.

Slide 19 of 28: Conditional moves and the prisoner's dilemma

Let's return to the example of J. Crew and Banana Republic in their effort to price a totally normal blue shirt. Recall that we have a PD situation: both BR and J. Crew are incentivized to cut their prices even though doing so is not optimal for either company. One solution is to introduce a "match or beat the competition" clause. Many argue that these clauses actually hurt customers because they are an implicit conditional move communicated by one company to another: "If you lower your prices, I'll match whatever price you set". And therefore, prices remain high and the incentive to defect is removed. On the right, the shirt matrix from Module 4 with the $70, $70 price war boxed in red and, on the last step, the $80, $80 cell turning green.

Slide 20 of 28: Credibility and reputation

Much more useful in repeated games than in single-play situations. A drawn debate stage: two candidates at podiums, Bush and Dukakis, with a red 1988 marker between them. Beneath it two clips side by side, click to play: George Bush in 1988, "Read my lips: no new taxes", and Bill Clinton's 1992 campaign ad attacking the broken pledge. Two lines follow on the steps: but you can also get a reputation for not being credible; contracts are designed to ensure credibility, but reputation can work both ways.

Slide 21 of 28: Let's have an auction

I am auctioning ten points. On your phones, whenever you like: bid, at least one point above the standing bid, and every bid goes up on the screen with your name on it. When the bidding stops, it is sold: the highest bidder pays their bid and takes the ten. The second-highest bidder also pays their bid, and gets nothing. A QR code takes phones to the auction, beside a box: the pot, ten points.

Slide 22 of 28: Going once

The live auction board. On the left, the standing high bid, ticking up as bids land, with the bidder's name and the second bid with its name beneath it, a count of bids in, and the sold button. On the right, every bidder in rank order by their standing bid: rank, name, bid, and a tag, takes the pot and pays their bid for the leader, pays their bid and gets nothing for second, safe for now for everyone else. On a keypress the ranks give way to a chart of every bid in the order it arrived against a dashed line at ten points, the pot's worth, the region above it labelled paying more than it is worth and a red line tracing the high bid. A note beneath says when the high bid has passed what the pot is worth, and who took the pot once it is sold.

Slide 23 of 28: Is commitment always a good thing?

Sometimes commitment strategies can be very beneficial: limiting options ensures that you will follow through with whatever position you've attached yourself to. But sometimes commitment can be detrimental. Escalation of commitment can easily lead to brinkmanship. On the right, the chicken matrix: on the step, both swerve options fade and only the minus 100, minus 100 crash is left.

Slide 24 of 28: Commitment and brinkmanship

If neither side is able to back down, we run into the problem of escalation of commitment. Parties may remain committed to their positions even though it's no longer in their self-interest to do so. "Throwing good money after bad". Robert Campeau versus Macy's for the purchase of Bloomingdale's. It often pays to give the other side an escape route in order to avoid escalation of commitment. On the right, the quote as a display line above a drawn bidding war: two bidders, Campeau in teal and Macy's in terracotta, either side of a stack of bids for Bloomingdale's, 1988, that climbs on the step until the top bar is red.

Slide 25 of 28: Surround them, or leave a way out?

A vote from the phones. You are the general; the enemy army is in front of you, and you have the numbers to surround them on every side, or you can leave them one way out. Which do you order? The two answers are revealed as rows of small person figures with a count, on a keypress. A QR code and a responses counter sit on the left; if the poll server is unreachable the board shows marked demo data.

Slide 26 of 28: Leave an outlet free

Two animated panels of toy numbers, played on the steps. Surrounded: an army of dots enclosed by a ring of attackers on every side; the ring closes in and the dots on both sides turn to losses while two counters climb, defenders lost and attackers lost. An outlet left free: the same army with a gap in the ring; the army streams out through the gap and both counters stay low. Beneath them the quote: "When you surround an army, leave an outlet free. Do not press a desperate foe too hard", Sun Tzu, The Art of War.

Slide 27 of 28: Cautionary tales in strategic moves

Can you think of a situation where a commitment would hurt you, not help you? Recall mixed strategies, and the penalty kick: in a zero-sum game, anything they know about your move is used against you. Ten kicks against the keeper from our penalty session, played by the instructor on the big screen; this time I have committed, every kick goes left, and the keeper knows it. The penalty scene from Module 6 with the right half of the goal shaded off, a shoot left button, a start again button, and three counters: kick of ten, goals, and how often the keeper went left. When the ten are done a note compares the goals with what the room scored in Module 6 when it could choose.
