MODULE 7 · GUESS TWO-THIRDS

Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

  • How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and successive elimination of inferior strategies
  • Let’s say everyone in the room guessed 100…what should you guess?
  • 100 × (2/3) = 67, so we can eliminate numbers 68 or above
  • But if nobody will play above 67, your winning strategy should be 2/3 of those choices
  • 67 × (2/3) = 45, so we can now eliminate numbers 46 or above
  • And so on (30, 20, 13, 9…)…all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium
  • So, all of those who answered 0 got it right…or did they??

MODULE 7 · STRATEGIC MOVES

Strategic moves in sequential games

  • Recall sequential strategies: in some games it pays to move first, in others it pays to move second
  • A commitment is a way of taking the first move, whatever the order of play says
  • A threat or a promise fixes a response in advance. It makes you the second mover, handing the first move to the other side
  • Strategic moves let you deliberately claim first or second mover advantage

MODULE 7 · ESCALATION OF COMMITMENT

Commitment and brinkmanship

  • If neither side is able to back down, we run into the problem of escalation of commitment
  • Parties may remain committed to their positions even though it’s no longer in their self-interest to do so
  • It often pays to give the other side an escape route in order to avoid escalation of commitment

MODULE 7 · ADDED VALUE, REVISITED

Strategic moves have unintended consequences

  • In the weeks before an election, US television stations must sell candidates airtime at the lowest rate they give any advertiser
  • Congress made a strategic move on the candidates’ behalf: every candidate gets the best deal in the room
  • The stations became fierce negotiators with the candidates: they dropped their price lists and steered campaigns to pricier airtime
  • The candidates ended up paying more than commercial advertisers, not less, as an unintended result of the strategic move

The badminton players who tried to lose

  • London 2012: the women’s doubles opened with a round-robin group stage, not a knockout
  • Losing a group match could get you an easier draw, so four pairs played to lose, and all eight players were disqualified
