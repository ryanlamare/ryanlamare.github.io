Slide 3 of 33: Introduction

One of the most easily challengeable assumptions in strategic thinking is that we are rational. We now consider the value of commitment, credibility, and other strategic instruments or strategic moves that are geared toward overcoming irrationality. We'll then consider another major challengeable assumption in strategic thinking, which is that we always have perfect information.

Slide 4 of 33: What is a strategic move?

A strategic move changes the game to your advantage. It has to be observable, the other side has to see it, and irreversible, they have to believe it cannot be undone; otherwise the respondent can become the first mover. Three kinds: a commitment, "I will do X, whatever you do"; a threat, "unless you do what I want, I will hurt you"; and a promise, "if you do what I want, I will help you". A commitment is unconditional; threats and promises are conditional.

Slide 5 of 33: Strategic moves in sequential games

Recall sequential strategies: in some games it pays to move first, in others it pays to move second. A commitment is a way of taking the first move, whatever the order of play says. A threat or a promise fixes your response in advance; that makes you the second mover, and hands the first move to the other side. Strategic moves are how you decide who gets first-mover advantage, and who gets second-mover advantage. On the right, two rows of cards joined by red arrows: commitment, "I will do X, whatever you do", leads to first mover, you move, they respond; threat or promise, "if this, then that", leads to second mover, they move, you respond by your rule.

Slide 6 of 33: Strategic moves and smoking

Recall sequential strategies, and smoking. Future Ryan is an addict, so we concluded that I should never smoke in the first place. But what we were really saying was, I lack the willpower to overcome temptation. Instead of being stuck with irrationality, we can manipulate a game to our advantage. On the right, the smoking game tree from the second session: Today's Ryan decides whether to smoke; if he does, Future Ryan decides whether to keep smoking. The plan to smoke then quit lights up first, then the quit branch is struck out because Future Ryan keeps smoking, and the red path moves to never starting.

Slide 7 of 33: Commitment and sleep

The resolute Night Self versus the weak-willed Morning Self. The Morning Self has second-mover advantage. In the sequential game, the Night Self can deliberately limit its options so that it sets an alarm, therefore forcing the Morning Self to choose to get up. On the right, the matrix: Night Self rows, no alarm and set alarm, against Morning Self columns, stay in bed and get up: 0, 10; 10, 0; minus 2, minus 5; 8, minus 1. On the step a speech bubble says "Hey Google, set an alarm for 7am", the no-alarm row fades and the set-alarm, get-up cell is boxed.

Slide 8 of 33: Credibility

Strategic moves are only as valuable as they are credible. Commitment devices don't work unless the other side actually believes them to be authentic. Think about strategic moves and sleep: my Night Self can set the alarm, which will force my Morning Self to wake up. Except… Introducing Clocky, the alarm that rolls off the nightstand and makes the Morning Self chase it. On the right, two speech bubbles: the crisp "Hey Google, set an alarm for 7am", and a trailing-off "Hey Google, set an alarm like 9:35am or whate…"; beneath them the Clocky clip, click to play.

Slide 9 of 33: Credibility and irreversibility

Forcing yourself into a strategic move that you cannot undo is an extreme form of credibility. Three pictogram tiles: scuttle the ships, Cortes scuttles his ships; only instant cameras, Polaroid's refusal to invest in anything other than instant cameras; the doomsday device, Dr. Strangelove and a deterrent that cannot be switched off. Of course, the consequences of all of these actions are, deliberately, severe.

Slide 13 of 33: Conditional strategic moves: threats and promises

A commitment on its own cannot fix a prisoner's dilemma: whatever you commit to, the other side still gains by defecting. It takes a conditional move, and this is where credibility is hardest, because keeping your word costs you. Conditional strategic moves require you to fix a response rule: "if this, then that". By declaring a conditional move, Player A becomes the second mover and cedes the first move to the other side. Two cards on the right. Threats: "Unless you do what I want, I will respond in a way that will hurt you". Promises: "If you do what I want, I will respond in a way that will help you". Your parents have probably played conditional strategic games with you!

Slide 14 of 33: Let's play split or steal

Pair up. A £1,000 pot, and each of you holds two golden balls: split, or steal. Talk it over first; say whatever you like. Then choose, at the same time, on your phones. Both split, £500 each. One steals, they take the lot. Both steal, nothing. This one is for points. A QR code and the payoff matrix are on the right.

Slide 16 of 33: Nick and Ibrahim

Golden Balls, 2012: Nick and Ibrahim, and a pot of £13,600. Nick tells Ibrahim he is going to steal, no matter what, and promises to split the money with him after the show. Ibrahim's choice is now between nothing for certain and a promise. He splits. Nick splits too. On the right, the clip, click to play.

Slide 17 of 33: Conditional moves and the prisoner's dilemma

Let's return to the example of J. Crew and Banana Republic in their effort to price a totally normal blue shirt. Recall that we have a PD situation: both BR and J. Crew are incentivized to cut their prices even though doing so is not optimal for either company. One solution is to introduce a "match or beat the competition" clause. Many argue that these clauses actually hurt customers because they are an implicit conditional move communicated by one company to another: "If you lower your prices, I'll match whatever price you set". And therefore, prices remain high and the incentive to defect is removed. On the right, the shirt matrix from Module 4 with the $70, $70 price war boxed in red and, on the last step, the $80, $80 cell turning green.

Slide 18 of 33: Strategic moves and chicken

In a coordination game a strategic move is at its most powerful: once the other side believes it, going along with it is their best move too. The danger is both sides making one. How does Ren induce Chuck to swerve in Footloose? Was this the most effective commitment strategy Ren could have followed? Recall the two conditions of a commitment strategy. What other commitment strategies might Ren have used instead? Throw the steering wheel out or tie the wheel in place. Develop a reputation over the course of several games for never swerving. On the right, the Footloose clip, click to play.

Slide 20 of 33: Let's play chicken

Pair up. You are two drivers heading straight at each other, and you play six rounds. Each round, at the same time, you swerve or you go straight, for the payoffs on the right. Whoever is ahead in the pair after six rounds takes five points; a dead heat pays nobody. A QR code takes phones to the game, with a count of pairs in.

Slide 21 of 33: Who swerved?

The live board for the chicken game. Two rows of three tiles. Rounds one to three, no wheel to throw: how often drivers swerved, how many rounds ended in a crash, how many rounds both swerved. Rounds four to six, when a driver could throw the steering wheel out of the window before the round: how many drivers threw it, how often a driver facing a thrown wheel swerved, and how many rounds both drivers threw the wheel, every one of them a crash.

Slide 22 of 33: Credibility and reputation

Much more useful in repeated games than in single-play situations. Can be strategically advantageous to develop a certain reputation. But you can also get a reputation for not being credible! Contracts are designed to ensure credibility, but reputation can work both ways. On the right, two clips stacked, click to play: George Bush in 1988, "Read my lips: no new taxes", and Bill Clinton's 1992 campaign ad attacking the broken pledge.

Slide 23 of 33: Is commitment always a good thing?

Sometimes commitment strategies can be very beneficial: limiting options ensures that you will follow through with whatever position you've attached yourself to. But sometimes commitment can be detrimental. In the game of chicken, what if both Ren and Chuck threw the wheels out of the car simultaneously? Escalation of commitment can easily lead to brinkmanship. On the right, the chicken matrix: on the step, both swerve options fade and only the minus 100, minus 100 crash is left.

Slide 24 of 33: Commitment and brinkmanship

If neither side is able to back down, we run into the problem of escalation of commitment. Parties may remain committed to their positions even though it's no longer in their self-interest to do so. "Throwing good money after bad". Robert Campeau versus Macy's for the purchase of Bloomingdale's. It often pays to give the other side an escape route in order to avoid escalation of commitment.

Slide 25 of 33: White walkers, game theorists?

Two stills from Game of Thrones, the army of the dead at Hardhome and the frozen lake beyond the Wall, above the quote: "When you surround an army, leave an outlet free. Do not press a desperate foe too hard", Sun Tzu, The Art of War.

Slide 26 of 33: Let's have an auction (flex)

I am auctioning ten points, the real kind. On your phones, in rounds: every round you bid, anonymously, at least one point above the standing bid, or you pass. When a round closes with no new bid, it is sold: the highest bidder pays their bid and takes the ten. The second-highest bidder also pays their bid, and gets nothing. A QR code takes phones to the auction.

Slide 29 of 33: Did they believe you?

The live board for the Granito game. Row one, the moves, one each before a word was said: the share of the room that chose a commitment, a threat, and a promise, the rest making no move. Row two, five minutes later: how many of the moves made the other side believed, how many pairs shook hands, and how often a believed move ended in a deal against how often a move that was not believed did.

Slide 30 of 33: Cautionary tales in strategic moves

Can you think of a situation where a commitment would hurt you, not help you? Recall mixed strategies, and the penalty kick. In a zero-sum game the other side gains exactly what you lose, so anything they know about your move is used against you. And a commitment is the most predictable thing there is. On the right, the penalty scene from Module 6, a goal seen from the spot with the keeper on the line, and on the last step a red speech bubble: "I'm going left. Every time."

Score as many as you can. On the right, a QR code, a count of responses, and a small preview of the penalty scene.

The live board, two rows of three tiles.
