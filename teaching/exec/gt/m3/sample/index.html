Applied Game Theory, Module 3 — Simultaneous Zero-Sum Games: sample block

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order. This is a short sample block from the Module 3 design pass, for review.

Slide 1 of 7: Title slide

Zero-sum games. Simultaneous zero-sum strategies. A sample block from Module 3 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 7: Michael, Dwight, and Andy in a zero-sum truel game

Three tiles, one per shooter, each with a big hit-rate figure. Dwight Schrute, "Fastest Crossbow in Scranton": 100% hit rate on his shots. Andy Bernard, "Cornell Gun Club President '06": 80% hit rate on his shots. Michael Scott, "I Once Shot Myself by Mistake": 50% hit rate on his shots. Two rules: shooting order is random, and after each person fires, he has to reload. A link plays the Mexican standoff clip.

Slide 3 of 7: Who would you rather be?

A live poll answered on phones at ryanlamare.com/go (QR code shown), with three options: Dwight (hits 100% of his shots), Andy (hits 80% of his shots), Michael (hits 50% of his shots). A response counter rises while the distribution stays hidden; a keypress reveals the answers as a crowd of Isotype figures per option. Two further reveals: What if Michael decides to (or is forced to) shoot first? Then: "Survival of the weakest" — Michael wins 52% of the time; Dwight 30%; Andy 18% — and Michael's option card is ringed as the winner. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 4 of 7: A zero-sum game in your own market

Lead line: a mature market is a fixed pie — every point of share you win comes straight off your rival's total. A two-by-three payoff matrix of your share of the market (your rival gets the rest). You (rows): promote hard, match the market. Your closest rival (columns): promote hard, match the market, pull back spend. Values by row: 50, 60, 70; 40, 50, 60. Steps grey out the rival's pull-back column as an inferior strategy, ring your promote-hard row as dominant, and ring the equilibrium where both sides promote hard: a 50/50 split.

Slide 5 of 7: Combining sequential and simultaneous thinking: Hotelling's game

We've so far considered how to solve zero-sum games with finite choices available (go north/go south; promote hard/match the market). But what strategy should we use if the choices are infinite? Consider Hotelling's game. Two vendors are selling hot dogs to beach goers — identical products and prices. Vendors choose where to place their carts. Beach goers will always choose the closest cart. Where should the vendors set up their carts?

Slide 6 of 7: The beach

An interactive beach strip with two draggable hot-dog carts, A in teal and B in terracotta, each showing its live share of customers; the sand shades toward whichever cart is closer. Two preset buttons replay the lecture beats: "Suppose they set up like this" places A three-quarters of the way east and B in the middle (A gets 37.5 percent, B gets 62.5 percent); "They should always end up like this" places both carts together in the middle, the 50/50 equilibrium. The instructor plays this live with the room.

Slide 7 of 7: Application to politics, the median voter theorem

Lead line: Hotelling proposed this game in 1929 — in 1957, a political scientist named Downs proposed that political representation operates just like vendors on a beach. An interactive political spectrum from 0, entirely Liberal, to 100, entirely Conservative, drawn as a band blending blue to red. Two draggable candidate badges — a blue donkey and a red rhino — each showing its live vote share, with a divider marking where voters switch sides. The same two preset buttons replay the beats: candidates apart (the blue candidate at 15 gets 40 percent, the red candidate at 65 gets 60 percent), then both converging on the median voter for 50/50.
