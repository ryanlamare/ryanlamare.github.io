Slide 3 of 7: A zero-sum game in your own market

Stood as slide 12 of 22. Lead line: a mature market is a fixed pie — every point of share you win comes straight off your rival's total. A three-by-three payoff matrix of your share of the market. Both you (rows) and your closest rival (columns) choose between promote hard, match the market, and pull back spend. Values by row: 50, 60, 70; 40, 50, 60; 30, 40, 50. Steps eliminate one line at a time: your rival will never pull back spend, so that column disappears; with it gone, pulling back can never be your best move either, so that row disappears; your rival is better off promoting hard than matching, so the matching column disappears; that leaves your choice — 50 beats 40 — so the matching row disappears; and the red box marks the equilibrium where both sides promote hard, a 50/50 split.

Slide 4 of 7: The limits of zero-sum thinking, as it stood

Next session: non-zero-sum games, and the most famous of them all — the prisoner's dilemma.

Build your matrix

Outtake Stood as slide 21 of 26 · cut 15 Sep 2026 · replaced by the room’s engines (Ryan’s call: inventing a zero-sum game in pairs asks the room to be the modeller)
In pairs: one zero-sum contest from your world, one prize that only one side can win. One of you brings it; the other plays the rival.
THE PRIZE
You and ___ are both after ___A contract, a customer, a slot, a budget line: real, and something only one of you can have. Then tell your partner who they are playing, in thirty seconds
YOUR MOVES
Two or three things your side could do to win itYour partner writes the rival’s moves at the same time. Neither of you sees the other’s list until both are in
PICK BLIND
Now you can see their moves. Pick the one you would actually make, without knowing theirs, and lock it inThe cell where you meet lights up on both phones
CROSS OUT
Is there a move on your list you would never make, whatever they did? Cross it outIf one cell is left standing, you found the equilibrium without a single number
