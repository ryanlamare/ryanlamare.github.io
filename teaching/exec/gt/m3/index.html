Slide 2 of 25: Introduction to simultaneous zero-sum strategies

Although many strategic interactions involve sequential choices, often a player must make a choice without knowing what their rivals have chosen, and vice versa. Recall that one of the limitations of sequential games is the notion of complete information. In simultaneous games, we have our first application of incomplete information. Two types of simultaneous games: zero-sum (win/lose) and non-zero-sum (win/win). Applications: voting behavior; football plays; employment negotiations; etc.

Slide 3 of 25: Characteristics of zero-sum games

Rational parties. Each player has a choice of two or more strategies. Diametrically opposed interests.

Slide 4 of 25: Some well-known zero-sum examples: soccer players during cup games

Barbados needs to win by two goals. Goals count double if the game goes into overtime. It's 2-0 to Barbados. In the 83rd minute, Grenada scores (2-1). What should Barbados do? What should Grenada do?

Slide 9 of 25: Solving zero-sum games using payoff matrices

In February 1943, General George Churchill Kenney, Commander of the Allied Air Forces in the Southwest Pacific, was faced with a problem. The Japanese were about to reinforce their army in New Guinea and had a choice of two routes. They could sail either north of New Britain, where the weather was rainy, or south of New Britain where the weather was generally fair. In any case, the journey would take three days. General Kenney had to decide where to concentrate the bulk of his reconnaissance aircraft. The Japanese wanted their ships to have the least possible exposure to enemy bombers, and of course, General Kenney wanted the reverse.

Slide 10 of 25: The allies' choice

It would be best for the Allied forces to choose the same route as the Japanese, but the Allies and the Japanese need to make a simultaneous choice. So the Allies — when they make their decision — don't know which route the Japanese will choose. All the Allies know is that the weather is rainy north of the island and fair south of the island, and they have a maximum of three possible days to bomb the Japanese. What should the Allies do? They must pick their best responses to what they think the Japanese will do, and vice versa!

Slide 11 of 25: The payoff matrix, days of bombing

A two-by-two payoff matrix. Allied choice (rows: northern route, southern route) against Japanese choice (columns: northern route, southern route). Northern/northern: 2 days. Northern/southern: 2 days. Southern/northern: 1 day. Southern/southern: 3 days. Steps highlight the northern row (2 days either way), dim the southern row (1 day of risk against 3), and ring the equilibrium at northern/northern: 2 days.

Slide 12 of 25: The Japanese choice

For the Japanese, the northern route minimizes their exposure — whatever the Allies choose to do. When General Kenney realizes this, his choice is clear: go north. The game has an "equilibrium point". Recall, an equilibrium point is a stable outcome, because a player unilaterally picking a new strategy is not helped by the change.

Slide 13 of 25: A workplace example

Two workers, Casey and Pat, are vying to become the chief negotiator at a unionized company. There is a dispute over whether the union should employ hard or soft tactics in its upcoming negotiation with the employer. Each candidate must decide whether to favor hard or soft tactics, or evade the issue. The candidates can calculate how their decision will influence the votes they will receive in the upcoming election to decide who will be chief negotiator. They must pick their best responses to what they think each other will do.

Slide 14 of 25: The workplace example matrix

A three-by-three matrix of Casey's vote share. Casey (rows: favor hard tactics, favor soft tactics, dodge the issue) against Pat (columns: the same three). Values by row: 45, 50, 40; 60, 55, 50; 45, 55, 40. Steps highlight Casey's soft-tactics row as dominant, dim the other rows, and ring the outcome at soft tactics versus dodge the issue: 50 percent.

Slide 15 of 25: Seeking the dominant strategy

Is there a dominant strategy for Casey? Yes: favor soft tactics. How about for Pat? Yes: if Pat realizes what Casey's strategy is certain to be, then clearly Pat should dodge the issue. What is the outcome? The outcome is 50% (conveniently an even split). Moral: always look for the dominant strategy, if there is one.

Slide 16 of 25: A slightly different workplace example

A three-by-three matrix with a payoff pair in each cell (Casey's share, Pat's share). Row favor hard tactics: 35,65; 10,90; 60,40. Row favor soft tactics: 45,55; 55,45; 50,50. Row dodge the issue: 40,60; 10,90; 65,35. Steps scratch out Pat's dodge-the-issue column (inferior for Pat), highlight Casey's soft-tactics row, dim Casey's other rows, and ring the equilibrium at soft tactics versus hard tactics: 45, 55.

Slide 17 of 25: Eliminating the inferior strategy

Here it is clear Pat should never play the "Dodge the Issue" strategy. It is an inferior strategy for Pat (scratch it out). Effectively that means we have a 2 by 3 matrix. Casey's dominant strategy in the reduced matrix is clearly to favor soft tactics (this is their best response no matter what the other side does). If Casey always chooses to favor soft tactics, then clearly Pat's best response is to favor hard tactics.

Slide 18 of 25: Guidelines for solving zero-sum games

Identify dominant (or superior) strategies. A dominant strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always greater than, or at least equal to, the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If a dominant strategy exists, choose it! Eliminate dominated (or inferior) strategies. An inferior strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always less than the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If an inferior strategy exists, never choose it!

Slide 19 of 25: Combining sequential and simultaneous thinking: Hotelling's game

We've so far considered how to solve zero-sum games with finite choices available (go north/go south; favor hard tactics/soft tactics/dodge the issue). But what strategy should we use if the choices are infinite? Consider Hotelling's game. Two vendors are selling goods to beach goers — identical products and prices. Vendors choose where to place their carts. Beach goers will always choose the closest cart. Where should the vendors set up their carts?

Slide 20 of 25: The beach, interactive

An interactive beach strip with two draggable vendor carts, A and B. The beach shades toward whichever cart is closer, and each cart shows its live share of customers. Two preset buttons replay the lecture beats: "Suppose they set up like this" places A three-quarters of the way east and B in the middle (A gets 37.5 percent, B gets 62.5 percent); "They should always end up like this" places both carts together in the middle (50/50 — the equilibrium).

Slide 21 of 25: Hotelling's game, worked

Each vendor has a choice of anywhere along the spectrum of the beach. The vendors will get all the customers more extreme to their location, and a portion of the customers less extreme to their location. Say Vendor A sets up 3/4ths of the way along the eastern side of the beach and Vendor B sets up exactly in the middle of the beach. Vendor A will get all customers farther east of the chosen location, and also a portion of customers between his/her location and the location of Vendor B. Vendor B will get all customers farther west of the chosen location, and also a portion of the customers between his/her location and the location of Vendor A. Who gets more customers? Vendor A gets 25% plus half of 25%, which is 37.5% of customers. Vendor B gets 50% plus half of 25%, which is 62.5% of customers. Is this game in equilibrium? No — Vendor A can improve by moving closer to Vendor B's position!

Slide 22 of 25: Applications of Hotelling's game to business strategies

So, Vendors A and B will always try to locate themselves as close to each other as possible in order to receive the maximum amount of profit — this is the equilibrium. Does this game matter in actual business strategies? You bet! Examples: think about where gas stations locate; in Champaign — look at Home Depot and Lowe's; in Idaho — cupcake wars; in New York City — jewelry stores. This is also one reason why so many products are marketed and sold with such similar characteristics. Even differentiators like the airline JetBlue eventually look a lot more like other airlines. BUT — is this clustering socially optimal?

Slide 23 of 25: Application to politics: the median voter theorem

Hotelling proposed this game in 1929 — in 1957, a political scientist named Downs proposed that political representation operates just like vendors on a beach. Assume that ideologies of voters run from 0-100, with 0 being entirely Liberal and 100 being entirely Conservative. If Candidate A positions herself at 15 and Candidate B positions herself at 65, what will each vote share be? Candidate A gets all votes to the left of 15 and half the votes between 15 and 65: 15% plus 25% equals 40%. Candidate B gets all the votes to the right of 65 and half the votes between 65 and 15: 35% plus 25% equals 60%. Is this game at equilibrium? No, for the same reason as the hot dog vendor game was not at equilibrium. So, the successful Republican and Democrat candidates in presidential elections always look very similar. But do we see this in reality? What are some of the challenges that you can make to this theorem?

Slide 24 of 25: Challenges to the median voter theorem

Not all voters are evenly distributed across the political spectrum. There are always more than just two options for voters (third candidates/not voting). Voters may care about more than just unidimensional political issues. What if the candidates are lying? Can a voter really believe the announcement of a candidate's position on an issue? Can candidates actually commit themselves to their positions?
