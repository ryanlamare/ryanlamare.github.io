Zero-sum games. Simultaneous zero-sum strategies. Module 3 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 21: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, recognize zero-sum games, where one side's win is the other side's loss. Two, make strategic choices without knowing what your rival has chosen. Three, solve simultaneous games with payoff matrices, dominant strategies, and inferior strategies. Four, apply Hotelling's game to business and politics.

Slide 3 of 21: Introduction to simultaneous zero-sum strategies

Although many strategic interactions involve sequential choices, often a player must make a choice without knowing what their rivals have chosen, and vice versa. Recall that one of the limitations of sequential games is the notion of complete information. In simultaneous games, we have our first application of incomplete information. Two types of simultaneous games: zero-sum (win/lose) and non-zero-sum (win/win). Applications: voting behavior; football plays; pricing moves; etc.

Slide 4 of 21: Characteristics of zero-sum games

Rational parties. Each player has a choice of two or more strategies. Diametrically opposed interests.

Slide 10 of 21: Solving zero-sum games using payoff matrices

In February 1943, General George Churchill Kenney, Commander of the Allied Air Forces in the Southwest Pacific, was faced with a problem. The Japanese were about to reinforce their army in New Guinea and had a choice of two routes. They could sail either north of New Britain, where the weather was rainy, or south of New Britain where the weather was generally fair. In any case, the journey would take three days. General Kenney had to decide where to concentrate the bulk of his reconnaissance aircraft. The Japanese wanted their ships to have the least possible exposure to enemy bombers, and of course, General Kenney wanted the reverse.

Slide 11 of 21: The allies' choice

It would be best for the Allied forces to choose the same route as the Japanese, but the Allies and the Japanese need to make a simultaneous choice. So the Allies — when they make their decision — don't know which route the Japanese will choose. All the Allies know is that the weather is rainy north of the island and fair south of the island, and they have a maximum of three possible days to bomb the Japanese. What should the Allies do? They must pick their best responses to what they think the Japanese will do, and vice versa.

Slide 12 of 21: Days of bombing

A two-by-two payoff matrix. Allied choice (rows: northern route, southern route) against Japanese choice (columns: northern route, southern route). Northern/northern: 2 days. Northern/southern: 2 days. Southern/northern: 1 day. Southern/southern: 3 days. Steps circle the two days figures in the northern row as the dominant strategy, make the southern row disappear as the dominated one, and mark the equilibrium at northern/northern with a red box: 2 days.

Slide 13 of 21: The Japanese choice

For the Japanese, the northern route minimizes their exposure — whatever the Allies choose to do. When General Kenney realizes this, his choice is clear: go north. The game has an "equilibrium point". Recall, an equilibrium point is a stable outcome, because a player unilaterally picking a new strategy is not helped by the change.

Slide 14 of 21: A zero-sum game in your own market

Lead line: a mature market is a fixed pie — every point of share you win comes straight off your rival's total. A three-by-three payoff matrix of your share of the market (your rival gets the rest). Both you (rows) and your closest rival (columns) choose between promote hard, match the market, and pull back spend. Values by row: 50, 60, 70; 40, 50, 60; 30, 40, 50. Steps make both pull-back lines disappear as inferior strategies, circle the numbers in your promote-hard row as the dominant strategy while the matching row disappears, and mark the equilibrium with a red box where both sides promote hard: a 50/50 split.

Slide 15 of 21: Guidelines for solving zero-sum games

Identify dominant (or superior) strategies. A dominant strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always greater than, or at least equal to, the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If a dominant strategy exists, choose it. Eliminate dominated (or inferior) strategies. An inferior strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always less than the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If an inferior strategy exists, never choose it.

Slide 16 of 21: Combining sequential and simultaneous thinking, Hotelling's game

We've so far considered how to solve zero-sum games with finite choices available (go north/go south; promote hard/match the market). But what strategy should we use if the choices are infinite? Consider Hotelling's game. Two vendors are selling hot dogs to beach goers — identical products and prices. Vendors choose where to place their carts. Beach goers will always choose the closest cart. Where should the vendors set up their carts?

Slide 17 of 21: The beach

An interactive beach strip with two draggable hot-dog carts, A in teal and B in terracotta, each showing its live share of customers; the sand shades toward whichever cart is closer. Two preset buttons replay the lecture beats: "Suppose they set up like this" places A three-quarters of the way east and B in the middle (A gets 37.5 percent, B gets 62.5 percent); "They should always end up like this" places both carts together in the middle, the 50/50 equilibrium. The instructor plays this live with the room.

Slide 18 of 21: Applications of Hotelling's game to business strategies

So, Vendors A and B will always try to locate themselves as close to each other as possible in order to receive the maximum amount of profit — this is the equilibrium. Does this game matter in actual business strategies? You bet! Examples: think about where gas stations locate; in London — look at the curry houses on Brick Lane; in Idaho — cupcake wars; in New York City — jewelry stores. This is also one reason why so many products are marketed and sold with such similar characteristics. Even differentiators like the airline JetBlue eventually look a lot more like other airlines. But is this clustering socially optimal?

Slide 19 of 21: Application to politics, the median voter theorem

Lead line: Hotelling proposed this game in 1929 — in 1957, a political scientist named Downs proposed that political representation operates just like vendors on a beach. An interactive political spectrum from 0, entirely Liberal, to 100, entirely Conservative, drawn as a band blending blue to red. Two draggable candidate badges — a blue donkey and a red rhino — each showing its live vote share, with a divider marking where voters switch sides. The same two preset buttons replay the beats: candidates apart (the blue candidate at 15 gets 40 percent, the red candidate at 65 gets 60 percent), then both converging on the median voter for 50/50.

Slide 20 of 21: Challenges to the median voter theorem

Not all voters are evenly distributed across the political spectrum. There are always more than just two options for voters (third candidates/not voting). Voters may care about more than just unidimensional political issues. What if the candidates are lying? Can a voter really believe the announcement of a candidate's position on an issue? Can candidates actually commit themselves to their positions?
