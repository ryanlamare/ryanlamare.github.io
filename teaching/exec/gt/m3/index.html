Slide 1 of 26: Title slide

Zero-sum games. Simultaneous zero-sum strategies. Module 3 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 26: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, recognize zero-sum games, where one side's win is the other side's loss. Two, make strategic choices without knowing what your rival has chosen. Three, solve simultaneous games with payoff matrices, dominant strategies, and inferior strategies. Four, apply Hotelling's game to business and politics.

Slide 3 of 26: Introduction to simultaneous zero-sum strategies

Lead line: often a player must make a choice without knowing what their rivals have chosen, and vice versa. Two pictogram columns under the line "Two types of simultaneous games": zero-sum (win/lose), drawn as a fixed pie, and non-zero-sum (win/win), drawn as a pie with a growing arrow. Reveals: recall one of the limitations of sequential games, the notion of complete information; in simultaneous games, we have our first application of incomplete information; applications: voting behavior, football plays, pricing moves, etc.

Slide 4 of 26: Characteristics of zero-sum games

Three pictogram tiles: rational parties (a figure with a check mark); each player has a choice of two or more strategies (a node with two branching arrows); diametrically opposed interests (two figures pulling in opposite directions).

Slide 5 of 26: Zero-sum examples, soccer players during cup games

Bullets on the left, with Barbados shown in blue and Grenada in terracotta: Barbados needs to win by two goals. Goals count double if the game goes into overtime. It's 2-0 to Barbados. In the 83rd minute, Grenada scores (2-1). What should Barbados do? What should Grenada do? The Barbados vs. Grenada clip is embedded large on the right of the slide behind a click-to-play cover.

Slide 9 of 26: Solving zero-sum games

Bullets on the left: February 1943, the Japanese are about to reinforce their army in New Guinea. They can sail north of New Britain in the rain, or south in fair weather — three days either way. General Kenney must decide where to concentrate his reconnaissance aircraft. The Japanese want the least possible exposure to his bombers, and of course, Kenney wants the reverse. On the right, a small mid-century wargame-style map: New Guinea at the left, New Britain in the middle, the Japanese convoy in terracotta at the right, Kenney's bombers in teal at the upper left, and two dashed routes — the northern under rain clouds, the southern over suns.

Slide 10 of 26: What should each side do?

The map fills the slide, with no other text. The convoy drags as one group of ships and the bombers as one group of planes, so the class can war-game the choices live: send the convoy along the northern or southern route, and position the patrols on either side.

Slide 11 of 26: The game expressed as a payoff matrix

A two-by-two payoff matrix of days of bombing. Allied choice (rows: northern route, southern route) against Japanese choice (columns: northern route, southern route). Northern/northern: 2 days. Northern/southern: 2 days. Southern/northern: 1 day. Southern/southern: 3 days. Steps: the Japanese should never sail south — the northern route means less exposure whatever the Allies do — so the southern column disappears. When Kenney realizes this, his choice is clear — go north — and the southern row disappears. The red box marks the equilibrium at northern/northern, and the game is said to have an "equilibrium point": a stable outcome, because a player unilaterally picking a new strategy is not helped by the change.

Slide 12 of 26: Guidelines for solving zero-sum games

Two definition cards. Identify dominant (or superior) strategies: a dominant strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always greater than, or at least equal to, the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If a dominant strategy exists, choose it. Eliminate dominated (or inferior) strategies: an inferior strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always less than the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If an inferior strategy exists, never choose it.

Slide 13 of 26: Combining sequential and simultaneous thinking

We've so far considered how to solve zero-sum games with finite choices available (go north/go south). But what strategy should we use if the choices are infinite? Consider Hotelling's game. Beneath the bullets, two hot-dog carts, A in teal and B in terracotta, can be dragged around while the instructor sets up the game aloud.

Slide 16 of 26: Let's do some city planning

Subtitle: the hospitals and grocery stores of Champaign-Urbana, Illinois. A near full-screen map of Champaign-Urbana's real main roads (from OpenStreetMap, credited on the map), drawn in a spare Mini Metro manner, the interstates heavier than the streets, with about three hundred homes as small grey dots along the street fabric. Two services, two of each: hospitals (round white stations with a red cross, teal and terracotta discs) and grocery stores (square stations with a green basket, green and ochre discs); a disc is a short drive on the map's scale, and homes inside a disc take its colour while the rest stay grey. Three view buttons: Hospitals, Grocery stores, Both; in the Both view a home is coloured when it can reach one of each, amber when only one, grey when neither. The markers start at random spots and the instructor drags them to where they really are: the hospitals side by side, the grocers both in the south, nothing downtown. "Where they should be" (also the first arrow press) glides the shown service's two apart; "Make it a 15-minute city" (also the second press) switches to Both, adds a third and fourth of each service drawn with a dashed edge, and places them all so that no home is grey, a plan worked out at load by a greedy cover of the homes; "Start again" scatters them. No numbers. The last press: a 15-minute city puts every service within a fifteen-minute walk of every home; it is the anti-Hotelling, and it has to be engineered, because it will not happen on its own.

Slide 17 of 26: Application to politics, the median voter theorem

Lead line: Hotelling proposed this game in 1929 — in 1957, a political scientist named Downs proposed that political representation operates just like vendors on a beach. An interactive political spectrum from 0, entirely Liberal, to 100, entirely Conservative, drawn as a band blending blue to red. Two draggable candidate badges — a blue donkey and a red rhino — each showing its live vote share, with a divider marking where voters switch sides. Preset buttons replay the beats: candidates apart, then both converging on the median voter for 50/50.

Slide 18 of 26: Challenges to the median voter theorem

Four numbered points: one, not all voters are evenly distributed across the political spectrum. Two, there are always more than just two options for voters (third candidates/not voting). Three, voters may care about more than just unidimensional political issues. Four, what if the candidates are lying?

Slide 19 of 26: Bidding for the airline's engine business

The rules of the engine game. Bullets: an airline needs several engines financed and supported over the coming years, and it will spread that business across two rival bidders. You are an individual contractor pitching for one slice of that business, for Team Aurora (teal) or Team Borealis (terracotta). Four rounds. Each round, choose the one thing your pitch pushes hardest: cut the price (offer the airline the same deal for less money), promise better terms (keep your price, make the deal easier to live with), solve a longstanding concern (fix a problem the airline has lived with for years), or charm the board (goodwill, hospitality, warm words, no substance).

Slide 20 of 26: The engine game expressed as a payoff matrix

A four-by-four payoff matrix labelled PRICE, TERMS, SOLVE, CHARM on both axes gives Aurora's and Borealis's percentage split of the contested slice for every pair of pitches; every cell sums to 100. Row values, Aurora's share first: price row 50/50, 55/45, 40/60, 70/30; terms row 45/55, 50/50, 45/55, 65/35; solve row 60/40, 55/45, 50/50, 60/40; charm row 30/70, 35/65, 40/60, 50/50. A reveal adds the points: five to whoever is ahead in their pair over the four rounds; a dead heat pays nobody. Beside the matrix, a QR code links to the phone page at ryanlamare.com/teaching/exec/gt/m3/game/.

Slide 21 of 26: The engine is the pie

The play board for the engine game. On the left, a head-on turbofan engine drawn as a pictogram: the fan disc is a pie split between Team Aurora (teal) and Team Borealis (terracotta), with each team's overall percentage of the airline's business shown beneath and the caption "overall share of the airline's business, all rounds so far". Pairs of rivals play on their phones, one pitch per round; each pair's phones spin their own small engine the moment both have played. On the big screen, a counter shows how many pairs have locked the current round; on the instructor's reveal, every pair's contest appears as a card — both pitches and the resulting split — and the big engine spins to the new cumulative book. A verdict line gives the round's counting: how many slices split unevenly, how many dead even, and where the whole book stands. When every pair makes the same pitch, every slice splits 50/50 and the engine spins without moving. If the poll server is unreachable the board runs on marked demo data.

Slide 22 of 26: Solving the engine game

The debrief. The same four-by-four matrix, solved in front of the room the way the module solves every matrix: one line at a time, alternating sides, no circles, the red box for the equilibrium only. Steps: first the question, which pitch would you never make, whatever your rival did? Then: charming the board, because whatever your rival pitches you do better solving their concern, so the charm row disappears; and your rival will never charm either, so the charm column disappears. With charm gone, solving a longstanding concern is never worse for you than price or terms, whatever they pitch, so the price and terms rows disappear; and the same is true for your rival, so the price and terms columns disappear. The red box marks the equilibrium: both sides solve the concern, and every slice splits 50/50. A final red chip asks: did your pair get there on its own?

Slide 23 of 26: A zero-sum game from your world

The closing exercise, in groups of three on one phone. Lead line: a situation where you and another party had to divide something and could not collaborate. Four rows, revealed one by one: THE PIE, what was being divided? (one contract, one budget, one slot, one engine: something fixed, that both of you wanted). THE OTHER PARTY, who was on the other side? YOUR STRATEGY, did either side have a dominant or inferior strategy? (tap dominant, inferior, or neither, and say what it was). THE OUTCOME, how was the pie divided in the end? Set it as a percentage split between you and the other side (100% = you won everything; 0% = you lost everything; the fan on your phone follows the slider). On the right, a QR code for ryanlamare.com/go and a running count of engines in.

Slide 24 of 26: The room's engines

Full screen: every group's situation as one engine, two to an aircraft seen head on (nose, wingspan, tail, and the two fans looking straight at the room; an odd group's aircraft waits on its second engine), each fan the pie, split teal to the group and terracotta to the other party at the percentage they set, numbered in the order they arrived, with a count of engines in. Each arrow press brings the next engine forward: the fan winds up from a standstill to a steady spin on the left (the small engines idle slowly) with the two shares and a word for the outcome (they won everything, they lost everything, an even split), and on the right the three notes, the pie, the other party, and the dominant or inferior strategy if either side had one, under the question for the instructor to ask: was it really zero-sum? Clicking an engine does the same, and clicking the panel puts it back. After the last engine, one more press flies the fleet off, each aircraft climbing away in turn. Marked demo data when there is no room.

Slide 25 of 26: What did we learn?

Five pictogram tiles, revealed one by one. A fixed pie: one side's win is the other side's loss. Choose without knowing: you move without knowing what your rival has chosen, and so do they. Never play an inferior strategy: if a move is worse whatever they do, cross it out. If a dominant strategy exists, choose it: if a move is at least as good whatever they do, it's yours. Back to back in the middle: Hotelling, rivals end up in the middle of the beach, and so do candidates.

The module scoreboard, computed live from the poll rooms: the top five names, revealed fifth place first, one keypress at a time, with each player's engine game points (five to whoever came out ahead in their pair over the four rounds; a dead heat pays nobody) and running total. Top five only, never a full ranking; each participant can check their own score privately at ryanlamare.com/go/points. If the score server is unreachable the board shows marked demo data.
