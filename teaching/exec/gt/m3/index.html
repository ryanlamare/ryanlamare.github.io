Slide 1 of 20: Title slide

Zero-sum games. Simultaneous zero-sum strategies. Module 3 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 20: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, recognize zero-sum games, where one side's win is the other side's loss. Two, make strategic choices without knowing what your rival has chosen. Three, solve simultaneous games with payoff matrices, dominant strategies, and inferior strategies. Four, apply Hotelling's game to business and politics.

Slide 3 of 20: Introduction to simultaneous zero-sum strategies

Lead line: often a player must make a choice without knowing what their rivals have chosen, and vice versa. Two pictogram columns under the line "Two types of simultaneous games": zero-sum (win/lose), drawn as a fixed pie, and non-zero-sum (win/win), drawn as a pie with a growing arrow. Reveals: recall that one of the limitations of sequential games is the notion of complete information; in simultaneous games, we have our first application of incomplete information; applications: voting behavior, football plays, pricing moves, etc.

Slide 4 of 20: Characteristics of zero-sum games

Three pictogram tiles: rational parties (a figure with a check mark); each player has a choice of two or more strategies (a node with two branching arrows); diametrically opposed interests (two figures pulling in opposite directions).

Slide 5 of 20: Some well-known zero-sum examples, soccer players during cup games

Bullets on the left: Barbados needs to win by two goals. Goals count double if the game goes into overtime. It's 2-0 to Barbados. In the 83rd minute, Grenada scores (2-1). The Barbados vs. Grenada clip is embedded on the right of the slide; the instructor pauses it to run the next slide's poll.

Slide 8 of 20: Michael, Dwight, and Andy in a zero-sum truel game

Three tiles, one per shooter, each with a big hit-rate figure. Dwight Schrute, "Fastest Crossbow in Scranton": 100% hit rate on his shots. Andy Bernard, "Cornell Gun Club President '06": 80% hit rate on his shots. Michael Scott, "I Once Shot Myself by Mistake": 50% hit rate on his shots. One rule: shooting order is random, and after each person fires, he has to reload.

Slide 9 of 20: Who would you rather be?

A live poll with three options: Dwight (hits 100% of his shots), Andy (hits 80% of his shots), Michael (hits 50% of his shots). A keypress reveals the crowd of answers. Two further reveals: What if Michael decides to (or is forced to) shoot first? Then: "Survival of the weakest" — Michael wins 52% of the time; Dwight 30%; Andy 18% — and Michael's option card is ringed as the winner. A final reveal shows the link to the Mexican standoff clip.

Slide 10 of 20: Solving zero-sum games using payoff matrices

Lead line: in February 1943, General George Churchill Kenney, Commander of the Allied Air Forces in the Southwest Pacific, was faced with a problem. A mid-century wargame-style map: New Guinea at the left with the port of Lae, the island of New Britain in the middle, the Japanese convoy at Rabaul on the right in terracotta, and Kenney's bombers in teal to the northwest. Two dashed routes run from Rabaul to Lae: the northern route, under a rain cloud, and the southern route, under a sun. Reveals: the Japanese were about to reinforce their army in New Guinea — sail north of New Britain in the rain, or south in fair weather, three days either way. General Kenney had to decide where to concentrate the bulk of his reconnaissance aircraft. The Japanese wanted their ships to have the least possible exposure to enemy bombers, and of course, General Kenney wanted the reverse.

Slide 11 of 20: What should each side do?

The same map, now with a red question by the convoy — north, or south? — and a red question by the bombers — patrol north, or south? Caption: each side must pick its best response to what it thinks the other side will do.

Slide 12 of 20: Days of bombing

A two-by-two payoff matrix. Allied choice (rows: northern route, southern route) against Japanese choice (columns: northern route, southern route). Northern/northern: 2 days. Northern/southern: 2 days. Southern/northern: 1 day. Southern/southern: 3 days. Steps: the Japanese should never sail south — the northern route means less exposure whatever the Allies do — so the southern column disappears. When Kenney realizes this, his choice is clear: pick the biggest number left, and the 2 days at northern/northern is circled. The red box then marks the equilibrium there, and the game is said to have an "equilibrium point": a stable outcome, because a player unilaterally picking a new strategy is not helped by the change.

Slide 13 of 20: A zero-sum game in your own market

Lead line: a mature market is a fixed pie — every point of share you win comes straight off your rival's total. A three-by-three payoff matrix of your share of the market (your rival gets the rest). Both you (rows) and your closest rival (columns) choose between promote hard, match the market, and pull back spend. Values by row: 50, 60, 70; 40, 50, 60; 30, 40, 50. Steps eliminate one line at a time: your rival will never pull back spend, so that column disappears; with it gone, pulling back can never be your best move either, so that row disappears; your rival is better off promoting hard than matching, so the matching column disappears; that leaves your choice — 50 beats 40 — so your 50 is circled; and the red box marks the equilibrium where both sides promote hard, a 50/50 split.

Slide 14 of 20: Guidelines for solving zero-sum games

Two definition cards. Identify dominant (or superior) strategies: a dominant strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always greater than, or at least equal to, the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If a dominant strategy exists, choose it. Eliminate dominated (or inferior) strategies: an inferior strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always less than the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If an inferior strategy exists, never choose it.

Slide 15 of 20: Combining sequential and simultaneous thinking, Hotelling's game

We've so far considered how to solve zero-sum games with finite choices available (go north/go south; promote hard/match the market). But what strategy should we use if the choices are infinite? Consider Hotelling's game. Two vendors are selling hot dogs to beach goers — identical products and prices. Vendors choose where to place their carts. Beach goers will always choose the closest cart. Where should the vendors set up their carts?

Slide 16 of 20: The beach

An interactive beach strip with two draggable hot-dog carts, A in teal and B in terracotta, each showing its live share of customers; the sand shades toward whichever cart is closer. Two preset buttons replay the lecture beats: "Suppose they set up like this" places A three-quarters of the way east and B in the middle (A gets 37.5 percent, B gets 62.5 percent); "They should always end up like this" places both carts together in the middle, the 50/50 equilibrium. The instructor plays this live with the room.

Slide 17 of 20: Where else do we see Hotelling's game?

A paired game: in pairs, come up with as many real-world examples of Hotelling's game as you can. Think of the curry houses on Brick Lane, then find your own. Two minutes, with a large on-screen countdown timer. Debrief reveals: this is also one reason why so many products are marketed and sold with such similar characteristics. But is this clustering socially optimal?

Slide 18 of 20: Application to politics, the median voter theorem

Lead line: Hotelling proposed this game in 1929 — in 1957, a political scientist named Downs proposed that political representation operates just like vendors on a beach. An interactive political spectrum from 0, entirely Liberal, to 100, entirely Conservative, drawn as a band blending blue to red. Two draggable candidate badges — a blue donkey and a red rhino — each showing its live vote share, with a divider marking where voters switch sides. The same two preset buttons replay the beats: candidates apart (the blue candidate at 15 gets 40 percent, the red candidate at 65 gets 60 percent), then both converging on the median voter for 50/50.

Slide 19 of 20: Challenges to the median voter theorem

Four numbered points: one, not all voters are evenly distributed across the political spectrum. Two, there are always more than just two options for voters (third candidates/not voting). Three, voters may care about more than just unidimensional political issues. Four, what if the candidates are lying? Can a voter really believe the announcement of a candidate's position on an issue? Can candidates actually commit themselves to their positions?
