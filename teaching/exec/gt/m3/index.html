Slide 1 of 26: Title slide

Zero-sum games. Simultaneous zero-sum strategies. Module 3 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 26: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, recognize zero-sum games, where one side's win is the other side's loss. Two, make strategic choices without knowing what your rival has chosen. Three, solve simultaneous games with payoff matrices, dominant strategies, and inferior strategies. Four, apply Hotelling's game to business and politics.

Slide 3 of 26: Introduction to simultaneous zero-sum strategies

Lead line: often a player must make a choice without knowing what their rivals have chosen, and vice versa. Two pictogram columns under the line "Two types of simultaneous games": zero-sum (win/lose), drawn as a fixed pie, and non-zero-sum (win/win), drawn as a pie with a growing arrow. Reveals: recall one of the limitations of sequential games, the notion of complete information; in simultaneous games, we have our first application of incomplete information; applications: voting behavior, football plays, pricing moves, etc.

Slide 4 of 26: Characteristics of zero-sum games

Three pictogram tiles: rational parties (a figure with a check mark); each player has a choice of two or more strategies (a node with two branching arrows); diametrically opposed interests (two figures pulling in opposite directions).

Slide 9 of 26: Solving zero-sum games

Bullets on the left: February 1943, the Japanese are about to reinforce their army in New Guinea. They can sail north of New Britain in the rain, or south in fair weather — three days either way. General Kenney must decide where to concentrate his reconnaissance aircraft. The Japanese want the least possible exposure to his bombers, and of course, Kenney wants the reverse. On the right, a small mid-century wargame-style map: New Guinea at the left, New Britain in the middle, the Japanese convoy in terracotta at the right, Kenney's bombers in teal at the upper left, and two dashed routes — the northern under rain clouds, the southern over suns.

Slide 10 of 26: What should each side do?

The map fills the slide, with no other text. The convoy drags as one group of ships and the bombers as one group of planes, so the class can war-game the choices live: send the convoy along the northern or southern route, and position the patrols on either side.

Slide 11 of 26: The game expressed as a payoff matrix

A two-by-two payoff matrix of days of bombing. Allied choice (rows: northern route, southern route) against Japanese choice (columns: northern route, southern route). Northern/northern: 2 days. Northern/southern: 2 days. Southern/northern: 1 day. Southern/southern: 3 days. Steps: the Japanese should never sail south — the northern route means less exposure whatever the Allies do — so the southern column disappears. When Kenney realizes this, his choice is clear — go north — and the southern row disappears. The red box marks the equilibrium at northern/northern, and the game is said to have an "equilibrium point": a stable outcome, because a player unilaterally picking a new strategy is not helped by the change.

Slide 12 of 26: Guidelines for solving zero-sum games

Two definition cards. Identify dominant (or superior) strategies: a dominant strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always greater than, or at least equal to, the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If a dominant strategy exists, choose it. Eliminate dominated (or inferior) strategies: an inferior strategy exists if a player's payoff from choosing this strategy is always less than the payoff from choosing any other available strategy. If an inferior strategy exists, never choose it.

Slide 13 of 26: Combining sequential and simultaneous thinking

We've so far considered how to solve zero-sum games with finite choices available (go north/go south). But what strategy should we use if the choices are infinite? Consider Hotelling's game. Beneath the bullets, two hot-dog carts, A in teal and B in terracotta, can be dragged around while the instructor sets up the game aloud.

Slide 16 of 26: Application to politics, the median voter theorem

Lead line: Hotelling proposed this game in 1929 — in 1957, a political scientist named Downs proposed that political representation operates just like vendors on a beach. An interactive political spectrum from 0, entirely Liberal, to 100, entirely Conservative, drawn as a band blending blue to red. Two draggable candidate badges — a blue donkey and a red rhino — each showing its live vote share, with a divider marking where voters switch sides. Preset buttons replay the beats: candidates apart, then both converging on the median voter for 50/50.

Slide 17 of 26: Challenges to the median voter theorem

Four numbered points: one, not all voters are evenly distributed across the political spectrum. Two, there are always more than just two options for voters (third candidates/not voting). Three, voters may care about more than just unidimensional political issues. Four, what if the candidates are lying?

Slide 18 of 26: Bidding for Tapas's engine business

The rules of the engine game, which borrows the name of one of the two cases the client sent over (spoken here, once: the names are borrowed for a couple of the games and for the tables on the last afternoon, and the cases are not otherwise taught). Bullets: Tapas needs several engines financed and supported over the coming years, and it will spread that business across two rival bidders. Everyone is an individual contractor pitching for one slice of that business, on behalf of Team Aurora (teal) or Team Borealis (terracotta). Four rounds. Each round, choose the one thing your pitch pushes hardest — cut the price (offer Tapas the same deal for less money), promise better terms (keep your price, make the deal easier to live with), solve a longstanding concern (fix a problem Tapas has lived with for years), or charm the board (goodwill, hospitality, warm words — no substance). Five points to whoever is ahead in their pair over the four rounds; a dead heat pays nobody. On the right, a four-by-four payoff matrix labelled PRICE, TERMS, SOLVE, CHARM on both axes gives Aurora's and Borealis's percentage split of the contested slice for every pair of pitches — every cell sums to 100. Row values, Aurora's share first: price row 50/50, 55/45, 40/60, 70/30; terms row 45/55, 50/50, 45/55, 65/35; solve row 60/40, 55/45, 50/50, 60/40; charm row 30/70, 35/65, 40/60, 50/50. A QR code below the matrix links to the phone page at ryanlamare.com/teaching/exec/gt/m3/game/.

Slide 19 of 26: The engine is the pie

The play board for the engine game. On the left, a head-on turbofan engine drawn as a pictogram: the fan disc is a pie split between Team Aurora (teal) and Team Borealis (terracotta), with each team's overall percentage of Tapas's business shown beneath and the caption "overall share of Tapas's business, all rounds so far". Pairs of rivals play on their phones, one pitch per round; each pair's phones spin their own small engine the moment both have played. On the big screen, a counter shows how many pairs have locked the current round; on the instructor's reveal, every pair's contest appears as a card — both pitches and the resulting split — and the big engine spins to the new cumulative book. A verdict line gives the round's counting: how many slices split unevenly, how many dead even, and where the whole book stands. When every pair makes the same pitch, every slice splits 50/50 and the engine spins without moving. If the poll server is unreachable the board runs on marked demo data.

Slide 20 of 26: Solving the engine game

The debrief. The same four-by-four matrix, solved in front of the room the way the module solves every matrix: one line at a time, alternating sides, no circles, the red box for the equilibrium only. Steps: first the question, which pitch would you never make, whatever your rival did? Then: charming the board, because whatever your rival pitches you do better solving their concern, so the charm row disappears; and your rival will never charm either, so the charm column disappears. With charm gone, solving a longstanding concern is never worse for you than price or terms, whatever they pitch, so the price and terms rows disappear; and the same is true for your rival, so the price and terms columns disappear. The red box marks the equilibrium: both sides solve the concern, and every slice splits 50/50. A final red chip asks: did your pair get there on its own?

Slide 21 of 26: Build your matrix

The closing exercise, in pairs on two phones. Lead line: one zero-sum contest from your world, one prize that only one side can win; one of you brings it, the other plays the rival. Four rows, revealed one by one: THE PRIZE, you and blank are both after blank (a contract, a customer, a slot, a budget line: real, and something only one of you can have; then tell your partner who they are playing, in thirty seconds). YOUR MOVES, two or three things your side could do to win it (your partner writes the rival's moves at the same time, and neither sees the other's list until both are in). PICK BLIND, now you can see their moves; pick the one you would actually make, without knowing theirs, and lock it in (the cell where you meet lights up on both phones). CROSS OUT, is there a move on your list you would never make, whatever they did? Cross it out (if one cell is left standing, you found the equilibrium without a single number). On the right, a QR code for ryanlamare.com/go and a running count of matrices in.

Slide 24 of 26: The limits of zero-sum thinking

Lead line: zero-sum games require that, in order for one side to win, the other must lose. The two pie pictograms from the opening slide return: zero-sum, today's games, a fixed pie; and non-zero-sum, now highlighted, where both sides can lose, one side can win, or both sides can win. Reveal: most of your decisions are not restricted to win/lose, which is where we go next.

Slide 25 of 26: What did we learn?

Five pictogram tiles, revealed one by one. A fixed pie: one side's win is the other side's loss. Choose without knowing: you move without knowing what your rival has chosen, and so do they. Never play an inferior strategy: if a move is worse whatever they do, cross it out. If a dominant strategy exists, choose it: if a move is at least as good whatever they do, it's yours. Back to back in the middle: Hotelling, rivals end up in the middle of the beach, and so do candidates.
