Beyond the prisoner's dilemma. Module 6 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 39: Introduction

So far, we've covered strategic thinking as it applies to sequential choices, simultaneous zero-sum (win/lose) choices, and a specific type of simultaneous non-zero-sum strategic thinking (the prisoner's dilemma). But there are many other important types of strategic thinking that come into play both in business decision-making and in everyday life. Although the prisoner's dilemma gets a lot of attention, other theories are equally important in explaining strategic thinking!

Slide 3 of 39: Best response and the Nash equilibrium

First, we need to figure out a comprehensive way to identify parties' ideal strategies in all games, not just prisoner's dilemmas or zero-sum games. We know already that we should (1) identify dominant strategies, and (2) reject inferior strategies. But what if there aren't any clear answers even after considering dominant and inferior strategies? Can we still find an equilibrium? The answer is yes — thanks to John Nash! Best response analysis.

Slide 4 of 39: Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of two conditions: each player is choosing a best response to what he believes the other players will do in the game; and each player's beliefs are correct — the other players are doing just what everyone else thinks they are doing.

Slide 5 of 39: Best response and the Nash equilibrium

Best response analysis finds all the possible Nash equilibria in a game. That is, it's a way to find all strategies for each player whereby no player can get a better payoff by switching to some other strategy. Like the other games we've studied, best response relies on considering one side's strategy as a function of others' moves. The Nash equilibrium, and using best response analysis to identify Nash equilibria, helps to square potentially circular strategies and provides clarity when there is no dominant response.

Slide 6 of 39: An example of using best response analysis to find the Nash equilibrium

A four-by-three matrix. Player 1 rows: top, high, low, bottom. Player 2 columns: left, middle, right. Payoffs (player 1, player 2) by row — top: 3,1; 2,3; 10,2. High: 4,5; 3,0; 6,4. Low: 2,2; 5,4; 12,3. Bottom: 5,6; 4,5; 9,7. Steps mark player 1's best response in each column (bottom-left, low-middle, low-right), then player 2's best response in each row (top-middle, high-left, low-middle, bottom-right), then ring the one cell marked twice — low/middle, 5, 4 — the Nash equilibrium.

Slide 7 of 39: Multiple Nash equilibria

So far, we have considered only games where one unique solution is present — i.e., a single pure strategy Nash equilibrium. But there are often instances where individuals can conceive of many strategies that are capable of generating similar outcomes for players. Games with multiple equilibria often require some sort of coordination by the players in order to reach a useful resolution.

Slide 8 of 39: You need to find some food!

Oops, we blew most of the planet up, but there's good news! Three things survived the nuclear fallout, and are edible by humans: two-headed cows, three-headed pigs, and cockroaches. Each type of animal has a known area in which it likes to roam. Two hunters set off to find food — but, since there are no phones left, they can't communicate with each other before they get to the roaming areas. Both hunters would prefer cow or pig over cockroach, but they also both must eat something in order to survive.

Slide 9 of 39: The hunting matrix

Hunter 1 against Hunter 2, choices: two-headed cow, two-headed pig, cockroach. Cow/cow: 3, 3. Pig/pig: 3, 3. Cockroach/cockroach: 1, 1. Mixed choices score 0 for a hunter who finds no partner, 1 for a hunter eating cockroach alone. Steps ring the three equilibria on the diagonal.

Slide 10 of 39: You need to find some food, resolved

Here there are three Nash equilibria — cow/cow, pig/pig, and cockroach/cockroach. If Hunter 1 heads to the area where the cows (or pigs, or cockroaches) are, Hunter 2 should also go there. But there is no way for Hunter 2 to know where Hunter 1 is going to go — and although Hunters 1 and 2 both dislike cockroaches, how are they meant to determine who prefers two-headed pork and who prefers two-headed beef? This can be thought of as a coordination game, because both sides need to coordinate in order to be successful. The name of this game is commonly known as stag hunt.

Slide 11 of 39: You need to go on a date!

Oops, we blew most of the planet up, but don't worry, there are still two of life's staples that survived: coffee and dating. Slide 13 of 39: You need to go on a date, resolved

How will you coordinate in order to repopulate the earth together? We need to identify a focal point where our expectations can converge. How might we find a focal point in this game? We have to consider common knowledge — do you know that your date knows to go to a certain café? Does your date know that you know to go to a certain café? Are you both 100 percent sure this is true?

Slide 14 of 39: Examples of coordination games

Coordination games are very common in society. Examples of coordination problems: Sleepless in Seattle (where should Meg Ryan and Tom Hanks find each other?). Television shows (if everyone else watches Game of Thrones, I should too otherwise I won't have anything to talk about with people, even if I hate it). Bad fashion ideas (normcore? Hush puppies? The Tipping Point and coordination games). Political bandwagons (it's best to present a unified — even if incorrect — front). Bank runs (we all keep our money in the banks only as long as we're sure it's safe).

Slide 15 of 39: Overcoming coordination problems

If only we could communicate, we could assure the other side by persuading them that we're better off at a certain equilibrium (or that we'll coordinate at the right place). This is why key figureheads give speeches in order to restore confidence. This is also one reason why leadership matters! Finally, this is also a crucial difference between these types of games and the prisoner's dilemma — would strategic communication matter under a PD?

Slide 20 of 39: You need to go to the movies, resolved

Here, unlike in stag hunt, you have a problem. There are two equilibria, but players have different preferences for each equilibrium point. You'd rather see Mad Max than Zombieland, and vice versa for your date, but you'd both rather be on a date together than to watch the movie separately (unless the movie is Waterworld). Slide 24 of 39: Solving games of chicken

There are two equilibrium points in this game (swerve/straight). So how do you win? Well, one way is to convince the other side that under no circumstances will you swerve. We'll return to commitment and credibility later. Another way is to alternate swerve/straight strategies if you play a repeated game.

Slide 25 of 39: Mixing strategies

Remember one of the paradoxes of strategic thinking, from way back in the first week — sometimes it pays to be inconsistent or unpredictable. Chomsky on Clinton's nuclear strategy in the 1990s: "It is 'beneficial' for our strategic posture if some elements may appear to be potentially 'out of control'". Nixon's madman theory in the 1960s: "I want them to believe I've reached the point where I might do anything". But let's go back even further to Machiavelli: "A very wise thing to simulate madness". Why might unpredictability be, in some cases, a preferable strategy? Think about a zero-sum game, where coordination is not preferable or possible.

Slide 26 of 39: The penalty kick in soccer

Penalty kicks involve two players — a kicker and a goalie. They face a simultaneous choice (in this case let's limit the choice to left or right). If the kicker shoots left, the goalie would always be best off by diving left. Conversely, if the kicker shoots left the kicker would always be best off by having the goalie dive right. Therefore, neither player is incentivized to coordinate strategies. How should a penalty kicker behave?

Slide 27 of 39: The psychology of penalties

Penalties are often thought of as a psychological game. Neither side wants to reveal to the other which way he or she will go. The psychological trickery can result in situations like the linked clips. "I told the players I knew where they were going and it made them a bit nervous." And, most famously, the last clip. But can you play the penalty kick game ideally, ignoring psychology, when no coordination is possible and no strategy dominates another?

Slide 28 of 39: The penalty kick matrix

The kicker's success percentage. Kicker left, goalie left: 58. Kicker left, goalie right: 95. Kicker right, goalie left: 93. Kicker right, goalie right: 70.

Slide 29 of 39: No pure strategy works

In this case, there is no (pure strategy) Nash equilibrium. If the kicker always chooses left, the goalie can choose left and drop the kicker's percentage to 58. If the kicker always chooses right, the goalie can choose right and drop the kicker's percentage to 70. Of the two, the kicker would prefer right-right to left-left, but can the kicker do better? Yes — by employing a mixed strategy and making it so that it doesn't matter which direction the goalie guesses!

Slide 30 of 39: The 50/50 coin flip

What if the kicker alternated left/right strategies at a 50/50 rate (say the kicker decides to flip a coin just before the run-up to decide where to shoot)? If the goalie goes left, the kicker gets 75.5 percent odds of success. If the goalie goes right, the kicker gets 82.5 percent. So the worst option from playing a completely random strategy (50/50 split) is better than even the best pure strategy option (70 percent if you choose to purely kick right). But can the kicker do even better than this? Yes — by making it so that the kicker's odds are the same irrespective of whatever the goalie does. What if the kicker decided, "I'm going left with 40% likelihood and I'm going right with 60% likelihood?" Then the kicker's success rate is 79% if the goalie picks left, and 80% if the goalie picks right.

Slide 31 of 39: Mix the kicker's strategy, interactive

An interactive slider sets p, the share of kicks going left. Two readouts show the kicker's expected success if the goalie dives left (58p plus 93 times one minus p) and if the goalie dives right (95p plus 70 times one minus p), plotted as two crossing lines. A button jumps to the indifference point at p equals 38.3 percent, where both readouts equal 79.6.

Slide 32 of 39: The kicker's optimal mixture

Is there a mixture that the kicker can use to make the goalie completely indifferent about going left or right? Think about the goalie's expected success rate for each choice the kicker has, left (p) or right (1-p). If the goalie goes left, the expected utility is 58p plus 93(1-p), which is 93 minus 35p. If the goalie goes right, it is 95p plus 70(1-p), which is 70 plus 25p. Setting them equal: 93 minus 35p equals 70 plus 25p, so 23 equals 60p, and p equals 23/60, which is 38.3 percent. So the kicker should go left 38.3 percent of the time (and thus right 61.7 percent of the time). With this mixture, the expected payoff would be 79.6 against any strategy the goalie chooses.

Slide 33 of 39: The goalie's mixture

Okay, but what about the goalie? Think about the kicker's expected success rate for each choice the goalie has, left (p) or right (1-p). If the kicker goes left, the expected utility is 95 minus 37p. If the kicker goes right, it is 70 plus 23p. Setting them equal gives 25 equals 60p, so p equals 41.7 percent. So the goalie should go left 41.7 percent of the time, and right 58.3 percent of the time. At a kicker mixture of 38.3% left and a goalie mixture of 41.7% left, neither side can be made better off by changing its strategy — we have found our Nash equilibrium!

Slide 34 of 39: Real-life penalty kick strategies

In real life, the probability of scoring a penalty kick goal is around 75-80%. In real life, kickers go left about 40% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 38.3%). In real life, goalies guess left about 42.4% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 41.7%). So, soccer players are game theorists even though they may not realize it!

Slide 35 of 39: Rock-paper-scissors and strategy

Can strategic thinking be applied to a game like rock-paper-scissors? If so, can the strategic thinking in a game like this tell us anything about business strategies? Yes and yes! Let's first design a payoff matrix and solve out RPS.

Slide 36 of 39: The RPS matrix

Player 1 against Player 2, choices rock, paper, scissors. Ties on the diagonal: 0, 0. Each off-diagonal cell: plus 1 for the winner, minus 1 for the loser (paper beats rock, scissors beat paper, rock beats scissors).

Slide 37 of 39: Rock-paper-scissors, solved

The best strategy is to mix with 1/3 probability of each option, for an average payoff of 0, which is better than the minimax outcome of minus 1 if each side plays a pure strategy. Since the game is symmetrical, both sides have identical strategies (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) which is the Nash equilibrium. And yet — nobody is really random! We fall into subconscious patterns even if we don't realize it, which makes the (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) strategy impossible.

Slide 38 of 39: Mixed strategies in business

The most obvious application of mixed strategies to the business world comes from organizational efforts to motivate compliance. Organizations and institutions want people to behave, but can't always monitor them. So, they introduce selective enforcement — aka a mixed strategy of enforcement (somewhere between 0% and 100% enforcement). Think about parking meters, speed limits, stop lights, etc. — the police employ "random" mixed enforcement strategies combined with excessive penalties to deter many criminal actions without actually monitoring those actions.

The penalty kick in soccer

  • Is there a mixture that the kicker can use to make the goalie completely indifferent about going left or right – i.e., so that it makes no difference to the expected outcome whatever side the goalie picks?
  • Think about the goalie’s expected success rate (or expected utility) for each choice the kicker has, left (p) or right (1-p)
  • If the goalie goes left, the expected utility is 58(p) + 93(1-p) = 93 – 35p

The penalty kick in soccer

  • Okay, but what about the goalie? What mixture would work best?
  • Think about the kicker’s expected success rate (or expected utility) for each choice the goalie has, left (p) or right (1-p)
  • If the kicker goes left, the expected utility is 58(p) + 95(1-p) = 95 – 37p
  • If the kicker goes right, the expected utility is 93p + 70(1-p) = 70 + 23p
  • Setting the success rates equal to each other (95 – 37p = 70 + 23p): 25 = 60p, so p = 25/60, which equals 41.7 percent
  • So the goalie should go left 41.7 percent of the time, and right 58.3 percent of the time
  • At a kicker mixture of 38.3% left and 61.7% right, and a goalie mixture of 41.7% left and 58.3% right, neither side can be made better off by changing its strategy – we have found our Nash equilibrium!
MODULE 6

Real-life penalty kick strategies

  • In real life, the probability of scoring a penalty kick goal is around 75-80%
  • In real life, kickers go left about 40% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 38.3%)
  • In real life, goalies guess left about 42.4% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 41.7%)
  • So, soccer players are game theorists even though they may not realize it!

MODULE 6

Rock-paper-scissors and strategy

  • The best strategy is to mix with 1/3 probability of each option, for an average payoff of 0, which is better than the minimax outcome of -1 if each side plays a pure strategy
  • Since the game is symmetrical, both sides have identical strategies (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) which is the Nash equilibrium
  • And yet – nobody is really random!
  • We fall into subconscious patterns even if we don’t realize it, which makes the (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) strategy impossible
MODULE 6

Mixed strategies in business

  • The most obvious application of mixed strategies to the business world comes from organizational efforts to motivate compliance
  • Organizations and institutions want people to behave, but can’t always monitor them
  • So, they introduce selective enforcement – aka a mixed strategy of enforcement (somewhere between 0% and 100% enforcement)
  • Think about parking meters, speed limits, stop lights, etc. – the police employ “random” mixed enforcement strategies combined with excessive penalties to deter many criminal actions without actually monitoring those actions
