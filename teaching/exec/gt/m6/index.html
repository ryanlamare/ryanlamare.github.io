Applied Game Theory, Module 6 — Mixed Strategies

Slide 1 of 16: Title slide

Mixed strategies. Sometimes it pays to be inconsistent or unpredictable. Module 6 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 16: Mixing strategies

Remember one of the paradoxes of strategic thinking, from way back in the first week — sometimes it pays to be inconsistent or unpredictable. Chomsky on Clinton's nuclear strategy in the 1990s: "It is 'beneficial' for our strategic posture if some elements may appear to be potentially 'out of control'". Nixon's madman theory in the 1960s: "I want them to believe I've reached the point where I might do anything". But let's go back even further to Machiavelli: "A very wise thing to simulate madness". Why might unpredictability be, in some cases, a preferable strategy? Think about a zero-sum game, where coordination is not preferable or possible.

Slide 3 of 16: The penalty kick in soccer

Penalty kicks involve two players — a kicker and a goalie. They face a simultaneous choice (in this case let's limit the choice to left or right). If the kicker shoots left, the goalie would always be best off by diving left. Conversely, if the kicker shoots left the kicker would always be best off by having the goalie dive right. Therefore, neither player is incentivized to coordinate strategies. How should a penalty kicker behave?

Slide 4 of 16: The psychology of penalties

Penalties are often thought of as a psychological game. Neither side wants to reveal to the other which way he or she will go. The psychological trickery can result in situations like the linked clips. "I told the players I knew where they were going and it made them a bit nervous." And, most famously, the last clip. But can you play the penalty kick game ideally, ignoring psychology, when no coordination is possible and no strategy dominates another?

Slide 5 of 16: The penalty kick matrix

The kicker's success percentage. Kicker left, goalie left: 58. Kicker left, goalie right: 95. Kicker right, goalie left: 93. Kicker right, goalie right: 70.

Slide 6 of 16: No pure strategy works

In this case, there is no (pure strategy) Nash equilibrium. If the kicker always chooses left, the goalie can choose left and drop the kicker's percentage to 58. If the kicker always chooses right, the goalie can choose right and drop the kicker's percentage to 70. Of the two, the kicker would prefer right-right to left-left, but can the kicker do better? Yes — by employing a mixed strategy and making it so that it doesn't matter which direction the goalie guesses!

Slide 7 of 16: The 50/50 coin flip

What if the kicker alternated left/right strategies at a 50/50 rate (say the kicker decides to flip a coin just before the run-up to decide where to shoot)? If the goalie goes left, the kicker gets 75.5 percent odds of success. If the goalie goes right, the kicker gets 82.5 percent. So the worst option from playing a completely random strategy (50/50 split) is better than even the best pure strategy option (70 percent if you choose to purely kick right). But can the kicker do even better than this? Yes — by making it so that the kicker's odds are the same irrespective of whatever the goalie does. What if the kicker decided, "I'm going left with 40% likelihood and I'm going right with 60% likelihood?" Then the kicker's success rate is 79% if the goalie picks left, and 80% if the goalie picks right.

Slide 8 of 16: Mix the kicker's strategy, interactive

An interactive slider sets p, the share of kicks going left. Two readouts show the kicker's expected success if the goalie dives left (58p plus 93 times one minus p) and if the goalie dives right (95p plus 70 times one minus p), plotted as two crossing lines. A button jumps to the indifference point at p equals 38.3 percent, where both readouts equal 79.6.

Slide 9 of 16: The kicker's optimal mixture

Is there a mixture that the kicker can use to make the goalie completely indifferent about going left or right? Think about the goalie's expected success rate for each choice the kicker has, left (p) or right (1-p). If the goalie goes left, the expected utility is 58p plus 93(1-p), which is 93 minus 35p. If the goalie goes right, it is 95p plus 70(1-p), which is 70 plus 25p. Setting them equal: 93 minus 35p equals 70 plus 25p, so 23 equals 60p, and p equals 23/60, which is 38.3 percent. So the kicker should go left 38.3 percent of the time (and thus right 61.7 percent of the time). With this mixture, the expected payoff would be 79.6 against any strategy the goalie chooses.

Slide 10 of 16: The goalie's mixture

Okay, but what about the goalie? Think about the kicker's expected success rate for each choice the goalie has, left (p) or right (1-p). If the kicker goes left, the expected utility is 95 minus 37p. If the kicker goes right, it is 70 plus 23p. Setting them equal gives 25 equals 60p, so p equals 41.7 percent. So the goalie should go left 41.7 percent of the time, and right 58.3 percent of the time. At a kicker mixture of 38.3% left and a goalie mixture of 41.7% left, neither side can be made better off by changing its strategy — we have found our Nash equilibrium!

Slide 11 of 16: Real-life penalty kick strategies

In real life, the probability of scoring a penalty kick goal is around 75-80%. In real life, kickers go left about 40% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 38.3%). In real life, goalies guess left about 42.4% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 41.7%). So, soccer players are game theorists even though they may not realize it!

Slide 12 of 16: Rock-paper-scissors and strategy

Can strategic thinking be applied to a game like rock-paper-scissors? If so, can the strategic thinking in a game like this tell us anything about business strategies? Yes and yes! Let's first design a payoff matrix and solve out RPS.

Slide 13 of 16: The RPS matrix

Player 1 against Player 2, choices rock, paper, scissors. Ties on the diagonal: 0, 0. Each off-diagonal cell: plus 1 for the winner, minus 1 for the loser (paper beats rock, scissors beat paper, rock beats scissors).

Slide 14 of 16: Rock-paper-scissors, solved

The best strategy is to mix with 1/3 probability of each option, for an average payoff of 0, which is better than the minimax outcome of minus 1 if each side plays a pure strategy. Since the game is symmetrical, both sides have identical strategies (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) which is the Nash equilibrium. And yet — nobody is really random! We fall into subconscious patterns even if we don't realize it, which makes the (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) strategy impossible.

Slide 15 of 16: Mixed strategies in business

The most obvious application of mixed strategies to the business world comes from organizational efforts to motivate compliance. Organizations and institutions want people to behave, but can't always monitor them. So, they introduce selective enforcement — aka a mixed strategy of enforcement (somewhere between 0% and 100% enforcement). Think about parking meters, speed limits, stop lights, etc. — the police employ "random" mixed enforcement strategies combined with excessive penalties to deter many criminal actions without actually monitoring those actions.

The penalty kick in soccer

  • Is there a mixture that the kicker can use to make the goalie completely indifferent about going left or right – i.e., so that it makes no difference to the expected outcome whatever side the goalie picks?
  • Think about the goalie’s expected success rate (or expected utility) for each choice the kicker has, left (p) or right (1-p)
  • If the goalie goes left, the expected utility is 58(p) + 93(1-p) = 93 – 35p
  • If the goalie goes right, the expected utility is 95(p) + 70(1-p) = 70 + 25p
  • We can make the goalie indifferent to going left or right by setting the success rates (or expected utilities) equal to each other (93 – 35p = 70 + 25p)
  • We can then solve for p: 23 = 60p, so p = 23/60, which equals 38.3 percent
  • So the kicker should go left 38.3 percent of the time (and thus right 61.7 percent of the time)
  • With this mixture, the expected payoff would be 79.6 against any strategy the goalie chooses
