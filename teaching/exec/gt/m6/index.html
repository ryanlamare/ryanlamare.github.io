Applied Game Theory, Module 6 — Mixed Strategies

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order. Payoff matrices are described cell by cell.

Slide 1 of 22: Title slide

Mixed strategies. Sometimes it pays to be inconsistent or unpredictable. Module 6 of the Applied Game Theory programme for RRPF.

Slide 2 of 22: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, recognize games with no stable move, where the right strategy is to be unpredictable; two, play a penalty shootout and find out how the room really kicks; three, find the mix that leaves your rival nothing to exploit, without the algebra; four, spot where mixing pays in business, and why a good mixed strategy can look like a bad decision. Four chips name the games: beat the keeper, the penalty shootout, shoot against me, and rock, paper, scissors.

Slide 3 of 22: Mixing strategies

Remember one of the paradoxes of strategic thinking, from way back in the first session — sometimes it pays to be inconsistent or unpredictable. Chomsky on Clinton's nuclear strategy in the 1990s: "It is 'beneficial' for our strategic posture if some elements may appear to be potentially 'out of control'". Nixon's madman theory in the 1960s: "I want them to believe I've reached the point where I might do anything". But let's go back even further to Machiavelli: "A very wise thing to simulate madness". Why might unpredictability be, in some cases, a preferable strategy? Think about a zero-sum game, where coordination is not preferable or possible.

Slide 4 of 22: The penalty kick in soccer

Penalty kicks involve two players — a kicker and a goalie. They face a simultaneous choice (in this case let's limit the choice to left or right). How should a penalty kicker behave? On the right, a drawn goal with a goalkeeper in the middle, a kicker with the ball at the spot, and two dashed arrows to the left and right corners.

Slide 5 of 22: The psychology of penalties

Penalties are often thought of as a psychological game. Neither side wants to reveal to the other which way he or she will go. The psychological trickery can result in situations like the first two clips. "I told the players I knew where they were going and it made them a bit nervous." And, most famously, the third clip. But can you play the penalty kick game ideally, ignoring psychology, when no coordination is possible and no strategy dominates another? Three click-to-play clip chips sit on the right.

Slide 6 of 22: Let's play: beat the keeper

The rules, one at a time: on your own, on your phone — pick a team, and your player wears the colours. Ten kicks against the keeper; on every kick you choose left or right. Score as many as you can. A QR code opens the game page beside a count of responses, and under it a still of the scene: a goal seen from the penalty spot, the keeper on the line, the kicker at the ball. On the phone every kick is animated: the run-up, the keeper's dive, the ball into the corner, into the gloves, or off the post. What the slide does not say: the keeper remembers every kick and leans the way the kicker leans — the room finds that out on the next slide.

Slide 8 of 22: Let's play: the penalty shootout

The rules, one at a time: pair up with the person next to you, on your phones. Five kicks each, taken in turns — you shoot, then you go in goal. On every kick you both choose left or right at the same time, without seeing the other's choice. Whether the ball goes in is decided by the real odds in the table, the same ones professional kickers and keepers face. A point for every goal you score from your five. A QR code opens the game page. The odds table: kicker's success rate — kicker left and goalie left 58 percent; kicker left and goalie right 95; kicker right and goalie left 93; kicker right and goalie right 70. Each kick is animated on both phones, with each player in their chosen team's colours.

Slide 9 of 22: How the room kicked

The live board, computed from every pair's kicks: counters for pairs in and kicks taken, then four stat tiles revealed one keypress at a time. Goalies dived left: the room's share, against 42 percent. Predictable? The share of kicks that went the same way as the kicker's previous kick, against the coin's 50 percent, and the longest run to one side. Slide 10 of 22: So how should a penalty kicker behave?

In this case, there is no (pure strategy) Nash equilibrium. If the kicker always chooses left, the goalie can choose left and drop the kicker's percentage to 58. If the kicker always chooses right, the goalie can choose right and drop the kicker's percentage to 70. Of the two, the kicker would prefer right-right to left-left, but can the kicker do better? Yes — by employing a mixed strategy and making it so that it doesn't matter which direction the goalie guesses! The odds table sits small on the right.

Slide 11 of 22: Mix the kicker's strategy, interactive

An interactive slider sets p, the share of kicks going left. Two readouts show the kicker's expected success if the goalie dives left (58p plus 93 times one minus p) and if the goalie dives right (95p plus 70 times one minus p), plotted as two crossing lines. A button jumps to the indifference point at p equals 38.3 percent, where both readouts equal 79.6.

Slide 12 of 22: Real-life penalty kick strategies

In real life, the probability of scoring a penalty kick goal is around 75-80%. In real life, kickers go left about 40% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 38.3%). In real life, goalies guess left about 42.4% of the time (recall the optimal strategy would be 41.7%). So, soccer players are game theorists even though they may not realize it! A final reveal reads the room's own numbers from the shootout: how often this room's kickers and goalies went left.

Slide 14 of 22: Pete Carroll, game theorist?

Super Bowl XLIX, February 2015. Seattle trail by four with 26 seconds left, second down on New England's one-yard line. Marshawn Lynch, the best short-yardage runner in football, is standing in the backfield, and everyone in the stadium expects the run. Seattle throw a quick slant. Malcolm Butler intercepts it, and the game is over. It was called the worst play call in Super Bowl history. Was it? A click-to-play clip of the play sits on the right.

Slide 15 of 22: The worst call, or the worst draw?

If Seattle run every time from the one, the defense only has to stop the run. A team that sometimes passes is harder to defend on every play, including the runs. The call was part of a mix, and the interception was the worst draw of it. So: do you judge your people on their decisions, or on their outcomes? On the right, a drawn goal line: seven defenders stacked against three attackers, a solid arrow for the run into the stack and a dashed red arrow for the pass over the top.

Slide 16 of 22: Let's play rock, paper, scissors

Can strategic thinking be applied to a game like rock-paper-scissors? Let's find out. The rules: same partner, on your phones — ten throws. Both of you throw at the same time; rock beats scissors, scissors beat paper, paper beats rock. No points for this one — we are playing it for the pattern it leaves behind. A QR code opens the game page.

Slide 17 of 22: Nobody is really random

The live board from the room's throws: three bars for the share of rock, paper and scissors against the one-third line, then three stat tiles revealed one keypress at a time — the room's opening throw, how often a player switched after losing a throw (a random player switches two times in three), and how often a player repeated after winning. If the poll server is unreachable the board shows marked demo data.

Slide 18 of 22: Rock, paper, scissors and strategy

The best strategy is to mix with 1/3 probability of each option, for an average payoff of 0, which is better than the minimax outcome of minus 1 if each side plays a pure strategy. Since the game is symmetrical, both sides have identical strategies (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) which is the Nash equilibrium. And yet — nobody is really random! We fall into subconscious patterns even if we don't realize it, which makes the (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) strategy impossible. The three-by-three matrix sits on the right: ties on the diagonal, plus one for the winner and minus one for the loser elsewhere.

Slide 19 of 22: Mixed strategies in business

The most obvious application of mixed strategies to the business world comes from organizational efforts to motivate compliance. Organizations and institutions want people to behave, but can't always monitor them. So, they introduce selective enforcement — aka a mixed strategy of enforcement (somewhere between 0% and 100% enforcement). Think about parking meters, speed limits, stop lights, etc. — the police employ "random" mixed enforcement strategies combined with excessive penalties to deter many criminal actions without actually monitoring those actions. On the right, a drawn row of ten cars with two picked out in red, labelled random checks.

Slide 20 of 22: Unpredictability in your world

A discussion slide. Where does it pay to be unpredictable in your business, and where does it cost you? Two prompts: the moves you would rather the other side could not predict; and the moves you need them to be able to predict.

Slide 21 of 22: When there is no stable move

The wrap-up. Today: when there is no stable move, mix, and don't let anyone read the pattern. A reveal asks: remember Ren's boot in Footloose — he won by committing, by accident. What does it take to make a commitment, a threat, or a promise believable on purpose? Closing chip: next session covers credibility, commitment, and strategic moves.
