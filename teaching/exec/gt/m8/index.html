Applied Game Theory, Module 8 — Information Asymmetries

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 25: Title slide

Uncertainty and information. Module 8 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 25: Information asymmetries

Recall two assumptions: rationality and complete information. We've already shown that rationality is problematic. We'll now discuss complete information. Can we always assume that both we and the other side are providing us with perfect information? Of course not!

Slide 3 of 25: Introduction

Why do people lie? Because sometimes telling the truth is against their own strategic interests. "If the Archbishop of Canterbury says he believes in God, that's all in the way of business, but if he says he doesn't, one can take it he means what he says." How do we deal with information asymmetries?

Slide 4 of 25: Manipulating information

You know something that the other side does not — what should you do? Conceal information or reveal misleading information; reveal selected information truthfully. You don't know something and want to find out from the other side — what should you do? Elicit information or filter truth from falsehood; deliberately remain ignorant. But how can you do any of these if you and the other side both know that talk is cheap?

Slide 5 of 25: Signaling, signal jamming, and screening

Three devices can be used to manipulate information. Signaling: favorable leakage of information, or actions taken to have the other side believe your information is good. Signal jamming: acting to reduce or eliminate the leakage of unfavorable information. Screening: forcing the other side to reveal that side's true information.

Slide 6 of 25: The market for lemons

Studies find that buyers in the market for a used car are far more likely to pick the one with a warranty. Why? It's not just because the warranty protects against future problems (though this is comforting). It's because the car seller must believe that the car is of sufficient quality to not lose money by offering a warranty.

Slide 7 of 25: Lemons versus peaches

A famous study tried to answer the seemingly simple question: why does a car immediately lose value as soon as it's driven off the lot? Say there are two types of cars for sale: bad quality (lemon) and good quality (peach). Also suppose that exactly half the market is made up of lemons and half peaches. Lemon owners will sell their bad cars for a minimum of $1000, while peach owners will sell their good cars for a minimum of $3000. If buyers can identify a lemon, they will pay up to $1500 for it; if they can identify a peach, they will pay up to $4000 for it. Under perfect information, the market should work — lemons sell for $1000-$1500 and peaches sell for $3000-$4000.

Slide 8 of 25: Lemons versus peaches, the asymmetry

But, what about information asymmetry? Only the sellers know whether the car is truly a lemon or a peach. If this is the case, and buyers know that half the cars are lemons, in any interaction the buyers should only ever be willing to pay a maximum of half of $1500 plus $4000, which is $2750. But if buyers are only willing to pay $2750 because of their uncertainty about whether they're getting a lemon or a peach, the only sellers in the market will be those with lemons (recall that peach owners will not sell below $3000). And, anyone who buys a car in the market is therefore overpaying for a bad quality car — the market will break down.

Slide 9 of 25: Lemons versus peaches, drawn

A price line from $0 to $4000. A lemon band sits at $1000 to $1500 and a peach band at $3000 to $4000. A dashed red line marks the buyer's maximum of $2750 under uncertainty. A step then greys out the peach band — peach owners leave the market, and only lemons remain below the line.

Slide 10 of 25: Example: screening and insurance

People know their own propensity for risk far better than insurance companies do. Companies are aware of adverse selection — people will select themselves into groups based on their private information, and by doing so will reveal the information to companies (this is true of the market for lemons as well). So companies offer customers several choices as a screening mechanism. A risky driver, when given the choice of low premiums for low coverage or high premiums for high coverage, is likely to select into the second option.

Slide 11 of 25: A few more examples of signaling and screening

Price discrimination and screening by self-selection (airlines offering business class seats; coffee shops offering "frequent buyer" cards; hardcover books). Product design and advertising: is there value in an expensive, useless, and uninformative product launch? You bet! Do we want our banks to have marble countertops and Nespresso machines? Of course! Taxis and customers (will I be robbed by my customer? Less likely if they call by phone or reveal a pickup location). What about drivers who see a customer wearing a suit? What about drivers who see a customer wearing a suit leaving an office building?

Slide 12 of 25: The dog that didn't bark: signaling by not signaling

Signaling can get messy — sometimes one side will expect a signal, and the other will not provide it. "The curious incident of the dog in the night time" or "the dog that didn't bark". The "danger" of taking a pass/fail grade in college — what might a student signal by not signaling in this case? A friend of mine once told me, "I'm not political. I don't vote." I responded, "You are political, because you don't vote." What did I mean in terms of signaling by not signaling? Keep this in mind when you're sending out your job applications, too!

Slide 13 of 25: So, should you always signal?

Not necessarily! It's important to be aware of countersignals. Conspicuous consumption. Political grandstanding. "Gunners" in law schools. "Ryan" or "Dr. Lamare?"

Slide 14 of 25: Pooling and separating equilibria

Cleanliness is a potentially useful signal of the quality of a house. But all sellers know they need to clean their houses, so even those houses with terrible underlying problems are clean. This is an example of a pooling equilibrium — where all different types (both good and bad) end up taking the same action, rendering that action useless. If I want to find out the true quality of a house, I need to find a separating equilibrium, where the good types will signal their goodness to me. How might I find a separating equilibrium when I'm in the market for a house?

Slide 15 of 25: The Tapas committee

The setup of the capstone game at the Tapas table: Tapas Airways is choosing who will finance its spare engine and the players are the committee recommending a bidder to the board. Everyone at the table plays, ten to fifteen people. Roles are Committee Member, Backer (two, or three at a table of twelve or more), Compliance Officer and Chair. Two phases each round, day and night; the moderator runs the table and the clock on the big screen runs the room. Phones deal the roles. Beside the bullets, a drawn phone shows the join screen: which table are you at, Tapas or Granito.

Slide 16 of 25: Committee Member

An honest member here in good faith to recommend the best offer. The job is to spot colleagues with a hidden loyalty and get them recused before they take the committee over. No special power, just the discussion and a read of people. If the phone gives no special role, you are a Committee Member. The drawn phone shows the role card.

Slide 17 of 25: Backer

By day an ordinary member, but secretly backing the bank whatever the offers say. The phone shows the fellow Backers. Each night the Backers together file an anonymous complaint against one honest member, who is removed; if they cannot agree, nobody is removed. Backers win by becoming half the committee and lose if caught. The drawn phone shows a night-one pick list with the fellow Backer's current pick.

Slide 18 of 25: Compliance Officer

An honest member who can request a confidential conflicts check: each night, name one person and the phone reveals their true role. The read is accurate but cannot be proved, and a Backer can claim to be the Compliance Officer. The drawn phone shows a check result and last night's check.

Slide 19 of 25: Chair

The one member who can vouch for a colleague so that any complaint against them that night is dismissed. Not the same person two nights running, but the Chair may vouch for themself. Keeping the Compliance Officer in the room is often the most important job. The drawn phone shows the vouch list with last night's name struck out.

Slide 20 of 25: The day

Introductions before the first night. Each day after that the committee debates which offer is best and who is not acting in good faith. An accusation must be seconded, the accused gets thirty seconds, then everyone votes on their phone to recuse or keep, votes shown by name on the big screen, majority wins. Anyone recused leaves but keeps their phone. Five minutes a day on the clock. The drawn phone shows a recuse-or-keep vote.

Slide 21 of 25: The night

Phones only, no talking. Backers file the complaint, the Compliance Officer checks, the Chair vouches. Everyone else writes notes for the morning: up to three suspects and a guess at the Compliance Officer and the Chair, which come back to them each day. A vouched-for member is not removed; otherwise the moderator announces the removal in the morning. The drawn phone shows the notes page.

Slide 22 of 25: Endgame

Rounds continue until one side wins: the committee by recusing every Backer, the Backers by becoming half the committee. Then everyone reveals who they were and the phones' record is shown. Ten points to everyone on the winning side.

Slide 23 of 25: The Granito management team

The second table: Granito Air is choosing who buys its five stored engines and the players are the management team recommending a buyer. Same game, roles and clock; the Backers here secretly back AOG-247, the rival trader.

Slide 24 of 25: The two committees

The live board that stays on screen during the game. A clock across the top reads the phase and the time left (demo: day 2, 4:37); D starts a day, N a night. Below, two panels, Tapas committee and Granito management, each listing every seat by name with removed seats struck through and tagged (night 1, recused day 2), and a log of each night's removal or dismissal and each day's vote. Demo data.

Slide 25 of 25: What the phones recorded

For each table, a spectrum from never suspected to suspected every night, with every player placed by how often they appeared in colleagues' nightly notes. On a keypress the true roles show: Backers turn red, the Compliance Officer and Chair are labelled. Then three honours per table appear in turn: Nose for it, for the player who had a Backer in their top three most often; Hidden in plain sight, for the least-suspected Backer; and Saint, for the honest member suspected least. Demo data.
