Introduction

MODULE 8

Manipulating information

  • You know something that the other side does not – what should you do?
  • Conceal information or reveal misleading information
  • Reveal selected information truthfully
  • You don’t know something and want to find out from the other side – what should you do?
  • Elicit information or filter truth from falsehood
  • Deliberately remain ignorant
  • But how can you do any of these if you and the other side both know that talk is cheap?
MODULE 8

Signaling, signal jamming, and screening

  • Three devices can be used to manipulate information:
  • Signaling = favorable leakage of information, or actions taken to have the other side believe your information is good
  • Signal jamming = acting to reduce or eliminate the leakage of unfavorable information
  • Screening = forcing the other side to reveal that side’s true information
MODULE 8

The market for lemons

  • Studies find that buyers in the market for a used car are far more likely to pick the one with a warranty
  • Why? It’s not just because the warranty protects against future problems (though this is comforting)
  • It’s because the car seller must believe that the car is of sufficient quality to not lose money by offering a warranty
MODULE 8

Lemons versus peaches

  • A famous study tried to answer the seemingly simple question: why does a car immediately lose value as soon as it’s driven off the lot?
  • Say there are two types of cars for sale: bad quality (lemon) and good quality (peach)
  • Also suppose that exactly half the market is made up of lemons and half peaches
  • Lemon owners will sell their bad cars for a minimum of $1000, while peach owners will sell their good cars for a minimum of $3000
  • If buyers can identify a lemon, they will pay up to $1500 for it; if they can identify a peach, they will pay up to $4000 for it
  • Under perfect information, the market should work -- lemons sell for $1000-$1500 and peaches sell for $3000-$4000
MODULE 8

Lemons versus peaches

  • But, what about information asymmetry? Only the sellers know whether the car is truly a lemon or a peach
  • If this is the case, and buyers know that half the cars are lemons, in any interaction the buyers should only ever be willing to pay a maximum of ½×($1500+$4000) = $2750
  • But if buyers are only willing to pay $2750 because of their uncertainty about whether they’re getting a lemon or a peach, the only sellers in the market will be those with lemons (recall that peach owners will not sell below $3000)
  • And, anyone who buys a car in the market is therefore overpaying for a bad quality car – the market will break down
MODULE 8

Lemons versus peaches

So, what can buyers do to resolve this game under incomplete information? Ask the sellers to produce signals of quality!
MODULE 8

Example: screening and insurance

  • People know their own propensity for risk far better than insurance companies do
  • Companies are aware of adverse selection – people will select themselves into groups based on their private information, and by doing so will reveal the information to companies (this is true of the market for lemons as well)
  • So companies offer customers several choices as a screening mechanism
  • A risky driver, when given the choice of low premiums for low coverage or high premiums for high coverage, is likely to select into the second option
MODULE 8

A few more examples of signaling and screening

  • Price discrimination and screening by self-selection (airlines offering business class seats; coffee shops offering “frequent buyer” cards; hardcover books)
  • Product design and advertising
  • Is there value in an expensive, useless, and uninformative product launch? You bet!
  • Do we want our banks to have marble countertops and Nespresso machines? Of course!
  • Taxis and customers (Will I be robbed by my customer? Less likely if they call by phone or reveal a pickup location)
  • What about drivers who see a customer wearing a suit?
  • What about drivers who see a customer wearing a suit leaving an office building?
MODULE 8

The dog that didn’t bark: signaling by not signaling

  • Signaling can get messy – sometimes one side will expect a signal, and the other will not provide it
  • “The curious incident of the dog in the night time” or “the dog that didn’t bark”
  • The “danger” of taking a pass/fail grade in college – what might a student signal by not signaling in this case?
  • A friend of mine once told me, “I’m not political. I don’t vote.” I responded, “You are political, because you don’t vote.” What did I mean in terms of signaling by not signaling?
  • Keep this in mind when you’re sending out your job applications, too!
MODULE 8

So, should you always signal?

  • Not necessarily!
  • It’s important to be aware of countersignals
  • Conspicuous consumption
  • Political grandstanding
  • “Gunners” in law schools
  • “Ryan” or “Dr. Lamare?”
MODULE 8

Pooling and separating equilibria

  • Cleanliness is a potentially useful signal of the quality of a house
  • But all sellers know they need to clean their houses, so even those houses with terrible underlying problems are clean
  • This is an example of a pooling equilibrium – where all different types (both good and bad) end up taking the same action, rendering that action useless
  • If I want to find out the true quality of a house, I need to find a separating equilibrium, where the good types will signal their goodness to me
  • How might I find a separating equilibrium when I’m in the market for a house?

MODULE 8 · ROLE

Backer

  • By day you look like any other member, but you secretly back one bidder, whatever the offers say
  • Your phone shows you which bidder, and who your fellow Backers are
  • Each night, together, you file an anonymous complaint against one honest member, and the moderator has to remove them from the committee. If you can’t agree on a name, nobody is removed that night
  • You win if Backers are half the committee, but you lose if you get caught, so never reveal your true loyalty!

The day

  • Everyone introduces themselves before the first night
  • Each day after that, the committee debates: which offer is best for the airline? And most importantly, who isn’t acting in good faith?
  • If you accuse someone, it must be seconded. The accused gets thirty seconds to defend themselves
  • Then everyone votes on their phone to recuse them, and the votes land on the big screen by name. Majority wins
  • Anyone recused leaves the committee, but keeps their phone
  • Five minutes a day, on the clock at the front
