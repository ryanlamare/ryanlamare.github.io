Applied Game Theory, Module 8 — Information Asymmetries

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 30: Title slide

Uncertainty and information. Module 8 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 30: Learning objectives

Four objectives revealed in turn: drop the assumption of complete information and play a market where it fails; the three devices, signaling, signal jamming and screening; lemons and peaches, pooling and separating; and Hidden Agenda, a hidden loyalty at the table.

Slide 3 of 30: Why talk is cheap

Why do people lie? Because sometimes telling the truth is against their own strategic interests. Two figures: a self-interested claim, with the Archbishop of Canterbury saying he believes, which is all in the way of business; and a costly admission, if he says he does not, one can take it he means what he says. Then the question: how do we deal with information asymmetries?

Slide 4 of 30: Manipulating information (flex)

Two figures. A person with a locked box: you know something the other side does not, so you can conceal it or reveal misleading information, or reveal selected information truthfully. A person with a magnifying glass: you want to find out what the other side knows, so you elicit information and filter truth from falsehood, or deliberately remain ignorant. Then the question: but how can you do any of these if you and the other side both know that talk is cheap? A pre-agreed cut if the session runs long.

Slide 5 of 30: Signaling, signal jamming, and screening

Three devices can be used to manipulate information, shown as three tiles with the instructor's definitions: signaling, favorable leakage or actions taken to have the other side believe your information is good; signal jamming, acting to reduce or eliminate the leakage of unfavorable information; screening, forcing the other side to reveal its true information.

Slide 6 of 30: A car market game

The first of three rounds of the car market, played face to face around the room. Seven lines revealed in turn: half of you have been allocated as car buyers and half are car sellers; there are two types of cars, lemons (bad quality cars) and peaches (good quality cars); buyers care about quality and will pay a premium for good quality; assume no further information is available to any side other than what is written on your phones; sellers want to get as much as possible irrespective of whether the car is a lemon or a peach; sellers should show buyers what is written on their phones when buyers approach them; try to make a deal! A QR code opens the page that stands in for the card: a buyer's phone says what a lemon and a peach are worth to them, and a seller's phone shows this round's car and the least the seller would take for it.

Slide 7 of 30: A car market game

The second round. Let's relax one informational assumption: sellers should not reveal to buyers what is written on their phones no matter how persistent the buyer is. All other assumptions hold: there is no further information available to any parties other than what is written on your phones.

Slide 8 of 30: A car market game

The third round. Let's relax one more informational assumption: sellers should still not reveal what is written on their phones no matter how persistent the buyer is, but buyers are now able to ask sellers for additional information beyond what is written on the phones.

Slide 9 of 30: Lemons versus peaches, drawn

A price line from 0 to 4000 with the lemon band at 1000 to 1500 and the peach band at 3000 to 4000; a dashed line marks the buyer's maximum of 2750 under uncertainty; the peach band is struck as peach owners leave the market; a final red chip: so what can buyers do? Ask the sellers to produce signals of quality!

Slide 10 of 30: Adverse selection

People know their own propensity for risk far better than insurance companies do. A before-and-after figure: the pool priced at the average, then only the bad risks stay. Then: people select themselves into groups based on private information and by doing so reveal it, so companies offer several choices as a screening mechanism; the risky driver picks the high-coverage option.

Slide 11 of 30: Credible signals

Three tiles from the instructor's own examples: the warranty, which the seller only offers if the car is good enough not to lose money on; the expensive, useless, uninformative product launch, which has value; the bank's marble countertops and Nespresso machines. Then: a signal works only if it is too costly for the wrong type to fake.

Slide 12 of 30: When the signal breaks

A costly signal separates one figure from three; when it is cheap to fake, all nine look alike. Generative AI made the polished résumé nearly free; the signal drowns in noise, firms retreat to referrals, and well-qualified outsiders get screened out.

Slide 13 of 30: The dog that didn't bark (flex)

Signaling can get messy: sometimes one side expects a signal and the other does not provide it. A cartoon dog face on, brown with a white muzzle and a red collar, mouth shut, with a struck-out speech bubble, the curious incident of the dog in the night-time, signaling by not signaling. Three chips in turn: the danger of a pass/fail grade, what does a student signal by not signaling; I am not political, I do not vote, you are political because you do not vote; and keep this in mind when hiring job candidates. A pre-agreed cut if the session runs long.

Slide 14 of 30: So, should you always signal?

A bar figure: signalling is low at the bottom, who cannot, peaks in the middle, who signal hardest, and is low at the top, who need not. Not necessarily! Be aware of countersignals: conspicuous consumption, political grandstanding, and gunners in law schools.

Slide 15 of 30: Pooling and separating equilibria

Cleanliness signals a house's quality, but every seller cleans, so even houses with terrible problems are clean. Two figures: pooling, where all types take the same action and it tells you nothing; separating, where the good types signal their goodness. Then: how might I find a separating equilibrium when I am in the market for a house?

Slide 16 of 30: The Tapas committee

The setup of Hidden Agenda: Tapas Airways is choosing who finances its spare engine and the players are the committee recommending a bidder to the board. Eleven people play, at one table, and run the whole committee; everyone else watches, and tries to work out who the Manipulators are. Roles are Committee Member, Manipulator (two), Auditor and General Counsel. Two phases each round, day and night; the moderator runs the table, and the clock is on the big screen. Phones deal the roles. A QR code joins, with a live count of those seated.

Slide 17 of 30: The Tapas shortlist

Four bidder cards, each with an archetype, a name, two strengths marked with green ticks and two watch-outs marked with red crosses: the bank, the lessor, the manufacturer, the fund. The shortlist is also on every phone. No obviously correct choice, on purpose.

Slide 18 of 30: Committee Member

An honest member here in good faith to recommend the best offer. The job is to spot committee members with a hidden agenda and get them removed before they take the committee over. No special power, just the discussion and a read of people. The default role. A drawn detective with a magnifying glass and a thought bubble, who are the Manipulators?

Slide 19 of 30: Manipulator

By day an ordinary member, but secretly backing one bidder whatever the offers say. The phone shows which bidder and the fellow Manipulators. Each night the Manipulators together make an anonymous allegation against one honest member, who is pulled off the committee while it is investigated; if they cannot agree, nobody is removed. Manipulators win by becoming half the committee and lose if caught. The same detective with two small red horns and the bubble, but I'm a committee member!

Slide 20 of 30: Auditor

An honest member who can check one committee member each night for a hidden agenda: the phone says whether they are a Manipulator. The read is accurate but cannot be proved, and a Manipulator can claim to be the Auditor. A magnifying glass revealing a small red horned figure inside: one check a night, and it never lies.

Slide 21 of 30: General Counsel

The one member who can shield a committee member so that any allegation against them that night is dismissed. Not the same person two nights running, but General Counsel may shield themself. Keeping the Auditor in the room is often the most important job. A person behind a green shield with a tick: one shield a night.

Slide 22 of 30: The day

Introductions before the first night. Each day after that the committee debates which offer is best and who is not acting in good faith. An accusation must be seconded, the accused gets thirty seconds, then everyone votes on their phone to remove or keep, the count lands on the big screen, majority wins. Anyone voted off leaves but keeps their phone. Five minutes a day on the clock. The drawn phone shows a remove-or-keep vote.

Slide 23 of 30: The night

Phones only, no talking. The Manipulators agree on one name for the allegation, the Auditor checks, General Counsel shields. Everyone else answers one question, who do you suspect, up to three names, which comes back to them in the morning. A shielded person is not removed; otherwise the moderator announces the removal in the morning. The drawn phone shows the suspects page.

Slide 24 of 30: Endgame

Rounds continue until one side wins. Two figures: the committee wins, nine people with the Manipulators outlined as removed, every Manipulator removed and a clean recommendation on the merits; the Manipulators win, nine people of whom half are red, and they steer the airline to their bidder. Then everyone reveals who they were and the phones' record is shown.

Slide 25 of 30: The committee

The live board that stays on screen during the game. A clock across the top reads the phase and the time left; D starts a day, N a night, P pauses. Below, the table shows every name in large type. When someone is removed overnight or voted off, their name is struck through with a red line that draws across it, and a banner announces it: pulled off the committee overnight, voted off, or stays. During an open vote the board shows the count to remove and to keep. Demo data until the table starts.

Slide 26 of 30: The recommendation

Bars for the four bidders showing how many at the table recommended each once the game was over, and a line naming the table's choice. One press reveals which bidder the Manipulators were backing and whether they got their way.

Slide 27 of 30: What the phones recorded

One full-width spectrum from never suspected to suspected every night, every player placed by how often colleagues named them in the nightly suspects question. One press reveals the roles: Manipulators turn red, the Auditor and General Counsel are labelled.

Slide 28 of 30: What was hidden?

The lesson as four tiles: a speech bubble, you saw words, never loyalty; a padlock, the loyalty was hidden, not just the choice; nine identical people, honest members and Manipulators sounded alike, a pooling equilibrium; a struck megaphone, a perfect read is useless if it cannot be credibly shared, talk is cheap.

Slide 29 of 30: Information asymmetries in your world

One plain question, talked through in twos or threes: where in your own deals does the other side know something you don't, and what do you do about it? On a press: then we sort them, which are you signaling, which are they jamming, and which do you screen for?

Slide 30 of 30: Takeaways from the two days

Eight lines on a numbered rail, revealed in turn, one for each module: always put yourselves in the others' shoes; always think about the future; always find dominant strategies; always try to be nice, but retaliate; always seek out focal points; always consider whether inconsistency is more beneficial than consistency; always know how strategic moves will affect you; always use information to your advantage.

MODULE 8 · ROLE

Committee Member

  • You’re an honest member of the committee, here in good faith to recommend the best offer for the airline
  • Your main job: spot the committee members with a hidden agenda and get them removed before they take the committee over
  • You have no special power, just the discussion and your read of people. Who is steering the committee toward a bidder they have already chosen?
  • If your phone doesn’t give you a special role, you’re a Committee Member
Who are the Manipulators?
MODULE 8 · ROLE

Manipulator

  • By day you look like any other member, but you secretly back one bidder, whatever the offers say
  • Your phone shows you which bidder, and who your fellow Manipulators are
  • Each night, together, you make an anonymous allegation against one honest member, and the airline pulls them off the committee while it’s investigated. If you can’t agree on a name, nobody is removed that night
  • You win if Manipulators are half the committee, but you lose if you get caught, so never reveal your agenda!
But I’m a committee member!
MODULE 8 · ROLE

Auditor

  • You are an honest committee member who can check one committee member each night for a hidden agenda
  • Name one person on your phone, and it tells you whether they are a Manipulator
  • Your read is always accurate, but you can’t prove it, and a Manipulator can claim to be you
  • Say who you are, and you risk being the next name in an anonymous allegation
One check a night, and it never lies
MODULE 8 · ROLE

General Counsel

  • The one member who can shield a committee member, so that any allegation against them that night is dismissed
  • Each night, name one person on your phone. Your shield dismisses any allegation made against them
  • You can’t shield the same person two nights in a row, but you may shield yourself
  • Keeping the Auditor in the room is often your most important job, if you can spot who they are!
One shield a night
