Applied Game Theory, Module 8 — Information Asymmetries

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 31: Title slide

Uncertainty and information. Module 8 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 31: Learning objectives

Four objectives revealed in turn: drop the assumption of complete information and play a market where it fails; the three devices, signaling, signal jamming and screening; lemons and peaches, pooling and separating; and Closed Session, a hidden loyalty at the table.

Slide 3 of 31: Why talk is cheap

Why do people lie? Because sometimes telling the truth is against their own strategic interests. Two figures: a self-interested claim, with the Archbishop of Canterbury saying he believes, which is all in the way of business; and a costly admission, if he says he does not, one can take it he means what he says. Then the question: how do we deal with information asymmetries?

Slide 4 of 31: Manipulating information (flex)

You know something the other side does not: conceal or mislead, or reveal selected information truthfully. You want to find something out: elicit and filter, or deliberately remain ignorant. But how can you do any of these if both sides know that talk is cheap? A pre-agreed cut if the session runs long.

Slide 5 of 31: Signaling, signal jamming, and screening

Three devices can be used to manipulate information, shown as three tiles with the instructor's definitions: signaling, favorable leakage or actions taken to have the other side believe your information is good; signal jamming, acting to reduce or eliminate the leakage of unfavorable information; screening, forcing the other side to reveal its true information.

Slide 6 of 31: The car market

The rules: half the cars on the lot are lemons and half peaches and nobody can tell which; a lemon is worth 1500 to you and a peach 4000; every round bid what you would pay for a car off the lot, and the owner sells at or above their own price; your phone tells you what you bought and what it was worth. A QR code joins, with a live count of buyers.

Slide 7 of 31: The lot

A placeholder for the live market board, built next: a grid of cars, sold cars driving off right in their true colour, under-offered peaches driving off left, then the warranty rule change.

Slide 8 of 31: Lemons versus peaches, drawn

A price line from 0 to 4000 with the lemon band at 1000 to 1500 and the peach band at 3000 to 4000; a dashed line marks the buyer's maximum of 2750 under uncertainty; the peach band is struck as peach owners leave the market; a final red chip: so what can buyers do? Ask the sellers to produce signals of quality!

Slide 9 of 31: Adverse selection

People know their own propensity for risk far better than insurance companies do. A before-and-after figure: the pool priced at the average, then only the bad risks stay. Then: people select themselves into groups based on private information and by doing so reveal it, so companies offer several choices as a screening mechanism; the risky driver picks the high-coverage option.

Slide 10 of 31: Credible signals

Three tiles from the instructor's own examples: the warranty, which the seller only offers if the car is good enough not to lose money on; the expensive, useless, uninformative product launch, which has value; the bank's marble countertops and Nespresso machines. Then: a signal works only if it is too costly for the wrong type to fake.

Slide 11 of 31: When the signal breaks

A costly signal separates one figure from three; when it is cheap to fake, all nine look alike. Generative AI made the polished résumé nearly free; the signal drowns in noise, firms retreat to referrals, and well-qualified outsiders get screened out.

Slide 12 of 31: The dog that didn't bark (flex)

Signaling by not signaling: the curious incident of the dog in the night-time, pass/fail grades, the friend who does not vote, job applications. A pre-agreed cut if the session runs long.

Slide 13 of 31: So, should you always signal?

A bar figure: signalling is low at the bottom, who cannot, peaks in the middle, who signal hardest, and is low at the top, who need not. Not necessarily! Be aware of countersignals: conspicuous consumption, political grandstanding, gunners in law schools, and Ryan or Dr. Lamare.

Slide 14 of 31: Pooling and separating equilibria

Cleanliness signals a house's quality, but every seller cleans, so even houses with terrible problems are clean. Two figures: pooling, where all types take the same action and it tells you nothing; separating, where the good types signal their goodness. Then: how might I find a separating equilibrium when I am in the market for a house?

Slide 15 of 31: What you said you'd want to know about the other players

The wall of questions the room wrote in module one about Tapas Airways, shown again as coloured chips. Two chips then appear: which of these can you simply ask, which will the airline jam, which need a screen; and, now you are the airline, which do you dread being asked?

Slide 16 of 31: The Tapas committee

The setup of Closed Session at the Tapas table: the airline is choosing who finances its spare engine, the players are the committee, ten to fifteen play, roles are Committee Member, Backer, Auditor and General Counsel, day and night phases, one clock for the room, phones deal the roles. Slide 17 of 31: The Tapas shortlist

Four bidder cards from the shared configuration, each with an archetype, a name, strengths and watch-outs: the bank, the lessor, the manufacturer's finance arm, the fund.

Slide 18 of 31: Committee Member

An honest member in good faith; spot the hidden loyalty and get it recused; no special power; the default role. A drawn phone shows the role card.

Slide 19 of 31: Backer

Secretly backs one bidder; the phone shows which and the fellow Backers; each night the Backers agree on one honest member to complain about, or nobody is removed; they win at half the committee, lose if caught. A drawn phone shows the pick list with the fellow Backer's pick.

Slide 20 of 31: Auditor

Runs one confidential conflicts check a night and learns a colleague's true loyalty; cannot prove it; a Backer can claim to be the Auditor. A drawn phone shows a check result.

Slide 21 of 31: General Counsel

Clears one colleague a night so a complaint against them is dismissed; not the same person two nights running; may clear themself; keeping the Auditor in the room matters. A drawn phone shows the clear list.

Slide 22 of 31: The day

Introductions first; then debate on the offers and on good faith; an accusation needs a second, thirty seconds to defend, then a phone vote shown by name, majority wins; the recused keep their phones; five minutes a day.

Slide 23 of 31: The night

Phones only, no talking; Backers file; the Auditor checks; General Counsel clears; everyone else writes notes: suspects and guesses at the Auditor and Counsel; a cleared person is not removed.

Slide 24 of 31: Endgame

The committee wins by recusing every Backer; the Backers win at half the table; then the reveal and the phones' record; ten points to the winning side.

Slide 28 of 31: What the phones recorded

For each table a spectrum from never suspected to suspected every night, every player placed by how often they appeared in colleagues' nightly notes. Slide 29 of 31: The recommendation

For each table, bars for the four bidders showing how many recommended each once the game was over, and a line naming the table's choice. One press reveals which bidder the Backers were backing and whether they got their way.

Slide 30 of 31: What was hidden?

The lesson: you saw words, never loyalty; the loyalty was hidden, not just the choice; honest members and Backers sounded alike, a pooling equilibrium; a perfect read is useless if it cannot be credibly shared.

MODULE 8

The dog that didn’t bark: signaling by not signaling

  • Signaling can get messy – sometimes one side will expect a signal, and the other will not provide it
  • “The curious incident of the dog in the night time” or “the dog that didn’t bark”
  • The “danger” of taking a pass/fail grade in college – what might a student signal by not signaling in this case?
  • A friend of mine once told me, “I’m not political. I don’t vote.” I responded, “You are political, because you don’t vote.” What did I mean in terms of signaling by not signaling?
  • Keep this in mind when you’re sending out your job applications, too!

MODULE 8 · ROLE

Backer

  • By day you look like any other member, but you secretly back one bidder, whatever the offers say
  • Your phone shows you which bidder, and who your fellow Backers are
  • Each night, together, you file an anonymous complaint against one honest member, and the moderator has to remove them from the committee. If you can’t agree on a name, nobody is removed that night
  • You win if Backers are half the committee, but you lose if you get caught, so never reveal your true loyalty!

MODULE 8 · CLOSED SESSION

The day

  • Everyone introduces themselves before the first night
  • Each day after that, the committee debates: which offer is best for the airline? And most importantly, who isn’t acting in good faith?
  • If you accuse someone, it must be seconded. The accused gets thirty seconds to defend themselves
  • Then everyone votes on their phone to recuse them, and the votes land on the big screen by name. Majority wins
  • Anyone recused leaves the committee, but keeps their phone
  • Five minutes a day, on the clock at the front
