Slide 3 of 9: Southwest Airlines

The cut slide, with its dates corrected; it stood as slide 13 of 28. Bullets on the left: Southwest does not allocate seats, so boarding position decides seating. EarlyBird check-in: ten dollars to be checked in automatically, twenty-four hours ahead. Paying is individually rational, so almost everyone pays. If everyone pays, the advantage is gone, and Southwest can no longer guarantee an A group seat. Ninety-eight million dollars of revenue in year one, now around four hundred million a year, and customers are suing. On the right, a clickable drawn front page for the Mind Your Decisions article on Southwest boarding and game theory.

Slide 4 of 9: Inside Tapas

The cut slide, verbatim; it stood as slide 25 of 28. The rules of the Tapas growth game. Three bullets: you and another Tapas executive need to decide on a growth strategy over several months; nobody is sure how long the board will ask for your input; it could shift focus next month, or keep debating growth options in perpetuity. Each month, you both decide simultaneously: fast or responsible growth. Your brief is on your phone. On the right, a QR code for the phone page with a live count of responses, and beneath it the two-by-two payoff matrix carrying all the numbers, sized like the ferry-scene matrix, you (rows) against your colleague (columns), grow responsibly or fast growth: both responsible 3, 3; you responsible while they push 0, 5; you push while they hold 5, 0; both push 1, 1. What the slide does not say: every phone carries the same setup, and the brief differs by one line: some are told that each month they and the same colleague decide simultaneously; the rest that each month they and a different colleague, someone they will never interact with again, decide simultaneously, and they are re-paired every month so that is true. The number of months is never shown to anyone, pairs move on to the next month the moment both have played, and each player sees only their own results. The matrix and the buttons are identical on every phone.

Slide 5 of 9: What Tapas chose

The cut slide, verbatim; it stood as slide 26 of 28. The play board for the Tapas growth game. A control row shows the state (waiting for the room, in play, revealed), a count of how many people have finished all their months, and two buttons: start the game (which freezes the list of who has joined and deals out the two briefs at random) and reveal the game. Nothing else shows while the room plays. On reveal, a line chart appears in one go: the percentage of the room that chose responsible growth, month by month, as two lines, the same-colleague part of the room in teal and the different-colleague part in terracotta, each labelled with its brief and with every point carrying its own percentage (the axis itself is unnumbered). A verdict line gives the two halves' overall rates. If the poll server is unreachable the board runs on marked demo data.

Slide 6 of 9: Create your own prisoner's dilemma

The cut slide, verbatim; it stood as slide 17 of 28. A paired write-in exercise. In pairs, think of strategic interactions in your own world where the pursuit of self-interest leaves everyone worse off, and send them from your phones — a QR code links to the write-in page at ryanlamare.com/go, and a counter shows how many examples are in. When the instructor calls time, a keypress fades the setup and fills the screen with everyone's answers as a wall of coloured cards, identical answers grouped with a times-N marker. If the poll server is unreachable the wall shows marked demo data.

Slide 7 of 9: Run the tournament

The cut slide, verbatim; it stood as slide 23 of 29. An interactive simulator: two strategies are chosen from menus and played against each other for twenty moves of the same matrix, each player's moves as a row of C and D squares with running totals, and a verdict line naming the winner of the pair.

Slide 8 of 9: The Evolution of Trust

The cut slide, verbatim; it stood as slide 27 of 28. A pointer for participants' own time: The Evolution of Trust at ncase.me/trust, an interactive guide to the game theory of trust that takes about twenty minutes, with the game's own closing advice as a caption.

Slide 9 of 9: Beyond the prisoner's dilemma

The cut slide, verbatim; it stood as slide 28 of 28. Lead line: today was the prisoner's dilemma, the temptation to defect, and how repeated play can overcome it; then the question of finding the same equilibrium together, and a chip: next session, coordination games.
