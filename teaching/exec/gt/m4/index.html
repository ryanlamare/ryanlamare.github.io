Applied Game Theory, Module 4 — Prisoner's Dilemmas and How to Overcome Them

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order. Payoff matrices are described cell by cell.

Slide 1 of 26: Title slide

Prisoner's dilemmas, and how to overcome them. Module 4 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 26: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, play a six-week price setting game against another team. Two, recognize prisoner's dilemmas and learn how to overcome them. Three, spot prisoner's dilemmas in business and society. Four, create your own prisoner's dilemma.

Slide 3 of 26: Introduction to non-zero sum games

Lead line: recall that zero-sum games require that, in order for one side to win, the other must lose. Two pictogram columns: zero-sum, last session's games, a fixed pie; and non-zero-sum, highlighted, where both sides can lose, one side can win, or both sides can win. Reveals: these are sometimes called "variable sum" games because each player's payoffs can vary, unlike "constant sum" games; the variance in each side's payoffs depends on the strategies each utilizes; and a closing chip: today, the most famous non-zero-sum game of them all.

Slide 4 of 26: Two stations, one junction

The game setup. Aura Fuels and Buco's Service Station face each other across the same junction on a busy A road. Aura has been there thirty years, but there is family pressure to hang up the spurs. Buco's is the newest upstart, ready to make a mark, but its investors want returns. You sell identical fuel to identical drivers, and price is the only thing that separates you. Every Monday morning you post your price on the roadside sign, without seeing theirs. Once both signs are up, every driver can read both prices all week. Six trading weeks. A pinned link opens the game brief — the full rules, the payoffs, the team ledger, and the price lock-in — on participants' phones or laptops.

Slide 5 of 26: The board

The running scoreboard, filled in live during the game. Eight team rows — Aura and Buco's in each of four matches — a column for each of the six weeks, and a running total; the weeks are deliberately unmarked on the board, though weeks three and six pay double as the game brief announces. Teams lock their weekly price in privately from their phones; a small dot marks a locked price that has not yet been revealed, and the instructor taps the cell to reveal it. Weekly profits and totals compute themselves once both stations in a match are revealed. If a team has not locked in, tapping the cell cycles the price by hand. When the week five shock has been announced from the Junction Gazette page, the instructor taps the W5 column header to switch the final two weeks to the shock ruleset; tapping it again switches back.

Slide 6 of 26: Debriefing the outcomes

A near full-screen chart with Aura's six-week total on the horizontal axis and Buco's six-week total on the vertical axis, both from zero to 240 thousand pounds. Reveals place the benchmark outcomes one at a time, computed under the full ruleset including the shock: both hold at one pound fifty every week, 84 thousand each; both cut every week, 63 thousand each; and the two corners where one station undercuts throughout, 234 thousand against 14 thousand. A final reveal plots where each of the four matches actually landed, live from the board.

Slide 7 of 26: The prisoner's dilemma

Lead line: the most famous, and perhaps most useful, of all non-zero-sum games. A drawn scene: two interrogation rooms with a red dividing wall between them, Suspect A in teal on the left, Suspect B in terracotta on the right, and a badge of two crossed-out speech bubbles above the wall. Caption: arrested and interrogated apart, with no way to coordinate. Confess, or stay silent?

Slide 8 of 26: The PD in payoff matrix form

Same lead line. Suspect A rows, Suspect B columns; each chooses stay silent or confess. Both silent: one year each. A silent, B confesses: twenty-five years for A, B goes free. A confesses, B silent: A goes free, twenty-five years for B. Both confess: three years each. Steps mark the matrix: teal circles around A's payoffs in the confess row, because confessing is A's dominant strategy; terracotta circles around B's payoffs in the confess column; a red box on both-confess, the Nash equilibrium, which holds both circles; and a green box on both-silent, the Pareto-optimal outcome, its payoffs turning green, with a green arrow drawn from the equilibrium to it. The green cell is unreachable if everyone follows their dominant strategies and individual self-interest.

Slide 9 of 26: Strip out the story

The generalized prisoner's dilemma, marked in the same scheme. Side A and Side B each choose between the collective action and the individual one. Both collective pays A to each; both individual pays D to each; if one defects while the other cooperates the defector gets B and the cooperator gets C. Steps: whenever B beats A and D beats C, the individual action is dominant for Side A, circled in teal; and for Side B too, circled in terracotta; so both sides land on D, D, boxed in red; even though A, A, boxed in green with its letters turning green, pays both more. B greater than A greater than D greater than C: any situation whose payoffs fit that ordering is a prisoner's dilemma, whatever it happens to involve.

Slide 10 of 26: Banana Republic and J. Crew

Both price a near-identical blue dress shirt without knowing what the other will charge, marked in the same scheme. At eighty dollars each they make seventy-two thousand dollars each. If one drops to seventy while the other holds, the cheaper firm makes one hundred and ten thousand and the dearer one twenty-four thousand. If both drop, each makes seventy thousand. Steps: cutting to seventy dollars is dominant for both, circled; the equilibrium is a price war at seventy thousand each, boxed in red; when holding at eighty would have paid both more, boxed in green with the arrow. In real life, J. Crew cut first, Banana Republic followed, and the war took both close to bankruptcy.

Slide 11 of 26: Southwest Airlines

Bullets on the left: Southwest does not allocate seats, so boarding position decides seating. EarlyBird check-in: ten dollars to be checked in automatically, twenty-four hours ahead. Paying is individually rational, so almost everyone pays. If everyone pays, the advantage is gone, and Southwest can no longer guarantee an A group seat. Ninety-eight million dollars of revenue in year one, now around four hundred million a year, and customers are suing. On the right, a clickable drawn front page for the Mind Your Decisions article on Southwest boarding and game theory.

Slide 12 of 26: Big Tech and AI

Bullets on the left: Big Tech is building AI capacity faster than it can prove demand for it. Spending will not peak until 2028, at around one trillion dollars. Davidson Kempner's CIO: "You have to invest in it because your peers are investing in it." A slower build matched to real demand would leave every firm better off. Think Anthropic's call to "pause" AI development. On the right, a clickable drawn front page for the Business Insider article on Big Tech's AI spend as a prisoner's dilemma.

Slide 13 of 26: Cigarette advertising

In the 1960s, the U.S. public was turning against smoking, and the government proposed a ban on TV ads for tobacco. The industry not only accepted the ban, it encouraged it. Had cigarette companies grown a conscience? No, they were game theorists escaping the prisoner's dilemma. A Mad Men clip, Don Draper the game theorist, is embedded on the right behind a click-to-play cover.

Slide 14 of 26: States bid against each other

A drawn map of the United States showing the Amazon HQ2 bidding war: Amazon in Washington State, thick red arrows to New York and Virginia, rows of dollar coins sized to each bid — about 1.5 billion dollars from New York and 573 million from Virginia, with each coin standing for 250 million dollars offered — and the headline figure that 238 cities bid for Amazon's HQ2.

Slide 15 of 26: Create your own prisoner's dilemma

A paired write-in exercise. If the poll server is unreachable the wall shows marked demo data.

Slide 16 of 26: Repetition alone is not enough

A backward-induction chain across the six weeks of the game. Week six has no future game, so undercut; so it pays to undercut in week five; and the same reasoning runs all the way back to week one. Working backward from a known final interaction says cooperation should never appear at all. But in practice it appears constantly. So what does the argument leave out?

Slide 17 of 26: Resolving the prisoner's dilemma

We see voluntary cooperative behavior all the time. In a world full of incentives to defect against each other, why do things often not work this way? Think here about The Dark Knight. The ferry scene as a payoff matrix: the civilians' ferry and the prisoners' ferry each choose whether to push the button. Both refrain: 3, 3. One pushes while the other refrains: 4 for the pusher, 1 for the other. Both push: 2, 2. Discussion reveal: how did the two sides overcome the PD, and what role did communication play?

Slide 18 of 26: The solution, Axelrod's tournament

Robert Axelrod asked under what conditions cooperation will emerge in a world of egoists without central authority. He invited game theorists to submit computer programs, paired every entry against every other, and ran two hundred moves per game, scoring three points each for mutual cooperation, five for defecting alone, one each for mutual defection and nothing for cooperating alone. Three benchmark figures: the theoretical maximum was one thousand; a pair that cooperated throughout scored six hundred each; a pair that defected throughout scored two hundred each. All the best game theorists in the world played each other. So who won?

Slide 19 of 26: Run the tournament

An interactive simulator. Two strategies are chosen from menus (Tit for Tat, Tit for Two Tats, Always Cooperate, Always Defect, Grudger, Random) and played against each other for twenty moves of the same matrix. Each player's moves appear as a row of C and D squares with running totals, so the room can watch niceness, forgiveness, and retaliation play out — and discover that tit for tat, the simplest entry, is the one that wins.

Slide 20 of 26: Nice, forgiving, retaliatory, clean

The four properties of tit for tat. Nice, drawn as a gold smiling face: it never defects first, and none of the low scorers had that property. Forgiving, drawn as a gold smiling face with a halo: it holds no grudges and has no memory of past deceptions. Retaliatory: it answers every defection, so nobody can profit by exploiting it. Clean: simple enough that the other side can read the rules and cooperate. Keep in mind that no rule is best independent of its environment; tit for two tats, which absorbs one extra defection before answering, sometimes does better.

Slide 21 of 26: Four lessons

Axelrod's four lessons. Don't be envious: your own total is the key, not whether you beat the other side. Don't be the first to defect: that decision is rarely recoverable. Reciprocate both ways: answer restraint with restraint, defection with defection. Don't be too clever: you can't cooperate with a strategy you can't work out.

Slide 22 of 26: The Evolution of Trust

A pointer for participants' own time: The Evolution of Trust at ncase.me/trust, an interactive guide to the game theory of trust that takes about twenty minutes to play. The game's own closing advice: evolve trust, build relationships, find win-wins, communicate clearly. If only it were that easy, of course.

Slide 23 of 26: Inside Tapas

The rules of the Tapas growth game. Three bullets: you and another Tapas executive need to decide on a growth strategy over several months; nobody is sure how long the board will ask for your input; it could shift focus next month, or keep debating growth options in perpetuity. Each month, you both decide simultaneously: fast or responsible growth. Your brief is on your phone. On the right, a QR code for the phone page with a live count of responses, and beneath it the two-by-two payoff matrix carrying all the numbers, sized like the ferry-scene matrix, you (rows) against your colleague (columns), grow responsibly or fast growth: both responsible 3, 3; you responsible while they push 0, 5; you push while they hold 5, 0; both push 1, 1. What the slide does not say: every phone carries the same setup, and the brief differs by one line: some are told that each month they and the same colleague decide simultaneously; the rest that each month they and a different colleague, someone they will never interact with again, decide simultaneously, and they are re-paired every month so that is true. The number of months is never shown to anyone, pairs move on to the next month the moment both have played, and each player sees only their own results. The matrix and the buttons are identical on every phone.

Slide 24 of 26: What Tapas chose

The play board for the Tapas growth game. A control row shows the state (waiting for the room, in play, revealed), a count of how many people have finished all their months, and two buttons: start the game (which freezes the list of who has joined and deals out the two briefs at random) and reveal the game. Nothing else shows while the room plays. On reveal, a line chart appears in one go: the percentage of the room that chose responsible growth, month by month, as two lines, the same-colleague part of the room in teal and the different-colleague part in terracotta, each labelled with its brief and with every point carrying its own percentage (the axis itself is unnumbered). A verdict line gives the two halves' overall rates. If the poll server is unreachable the board runs on marked demo data.

Slide 25 of 26: Beyond the prisoner's dilemma

Lead line: although the prisoner's dilemma gets a lot of attention, other theories are equally important in explaining strategic thinking. Closing chip: next session covers coordination games, where players must find the same equilibrium together.
