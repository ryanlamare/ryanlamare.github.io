Slide 3 of 33: Introduction to non-zero sum games

Recall that zero-sum games require that, in order for one side to win, the other must lose. Non-zero-sum games are not restricted to win/lose dichotomies. Both sides can lose, one side can win, or both sides can win in non-zero-sum games. These are sometimes called "variable sum" games because each player's payoffs can vary — unlike "constant sum" games. The variance in each side's payoffs depends on the strategies each utilizes.

Slide 4 of 33: The prisoner's dilemma

The most famous, and perhaps most useful, of all non-zero-sum games. Two suspects in a crime are arrested and interrogated separately. Each suspect may either confess (and betray his/her partner) or stay silent. If one suspect confesses and his partner does not, the one who confesses goes free and the other goes to prison for a very long time (say, 25 years to life). If both suspects confess (and betray each other), they both go to prison for 3 years. If both suspects remain silent (and nobody is betrayed), they both go to prison for 1 year.

Slide 5 of 33: The prisoner's dilemma payoff matrix

Suspect A (rows: do not confess, confess) against Suspect B (columns: the same). Do-not-confess/do-not-confess: 1 year each. Do-not-confess/confess: 25 years for A, B goes free. Confess/do-not-confess: A goes free, 25 years for B. Confess/confess: 3 years each. Steps ring A's best response in each of B's columns (confess both times), then ring the both-confess equilibrium.

Slide 6 of 33: Another version of the prisoner's dilemma

Player A against Player B, choices cooperate or defect. Cooperate/cooperate: 3, 3. Cooperate/defect: 0, 5. Defect/cooperate: 5, 0. Defect/defect: 1, 1.

Slide 7 of 33: Yet another version of the prisoner's dilemma

The same matrix in words. Cooperate/cooperate: good result for both. Cooperate/defect: worst result for the cooperator, best result for the defector. Defect/defect: not good result for either.

Slide 8 of 33: The generalized prisoner's dilemma

Thing A against Thing B, choices collectively beneficial action or individually beneficial action. Both collective: A, A. Collective against individual: C for the collective actor, B for the individual actor. Both individual: D, D. The rule: B is greater than A, which is greater than D, which is greater than C.

Slide 9 of 33: An example from business: pricing a totally normal blue shirt

Banana Republic and J. Crew are setting prices for the spring shopping season. Both companies sell almost identical items and must set prices without knowing what the other's price will be. How much should BR and JC sell a totally normal blue dress shirt for? We can assume that the marginal cost per shirt is the same — say $20 each. We can also assume consumers will discriminate based on price and will move between companies in response to price cuts.

Slide 10 of 33: Pricing a totally normal blue shirt, continued

Both BR and JC estimate that if they charge $80 for the shirt, they will sell 1,200 shirts each. Both BR and JC also estimate that, if either firm cuts their prices by $10 while the other does not, the cheaper firm will sell 100 more shirts (80 customers who shift from the higher to lower priced firm, plus 20 new customers), while the more expensive firm will lose 80 shirt sales. If both BR and JC cut their prices simultaneously by $10, the shirt now costs $70, which gains each firm 20 new customers but does not result in any shifting of customers from one company to another. The expected profits of each firm can be constructed as a payoff matrix.

Slide 11 of 33: Payoff matrix for profits for Banana Republic and J. Crew

Banana Republic (rows: $80, $70) against J. Crew (columns: $80, $70). Both $80: $72k each. BR $80, JC $70: $24k, $110k. BR $70, JC $80: $110k, $24k. Both $70: $70k each. Steps ring each side's incentive to cut and then the both-cut equilibrium at $70k, $70k.

Slide 12 of 33: Pricing a totally normal blue shirt, the outcome

Each side is incentivized to unilaterally price their shirts at $70 — which nets both BR and JC "only" $70k for their ridiculously overpriced blue shirts. Sometimes we see them cooperating, and sometimes we see the PD playing out in between JC and BR in real life — a few years back, JC began undercutting BR on price. BR responded by cutting prices too, which led to near-bankruptcy for both companies.

Slide 13 of 33: Prisoner's dilemmas in the programme game

Think about how PDs might operate when you play the tournament game against each other (Azul in the university course — swappable per programme). Any given game is zero-sum and sequential. But you also play it repeatedly against a single opponent and you both want to enjoy the experience throughout. Therefore the game can be cooperative in the long-run even if it's competitive in the short-run. You have various opportunities throughout the game to either screw the other side over or let them take the tiles they need. Think about under what conditions (if any) you would be incentivized to act with self-interest in mind, but in so doing you hurt the overall game experience?

Slide 14 of 33: The prisoner's dilemma in real life: college recruiting

Think about recruiting in college athletics. College football programs want to sign the best possible players to their teams. By offering a scholarship to a player before other teams do, a college can get a jump on the competition. But, if everyone else has the same idea, what does this create? A fictionalized version of a real-life phenomenon. Recruiting 4th graders is socially inefficient, but teams do it anyway because they're trapped in the prisoner's dilemma.

Slide 15 of 33: The prisoner's dilemma in real life: Southwest Airlines

If you've ever flown on Southwest Airlines, you know that they do not assign seats. Instead, customers pick their own seats — originally, this was based on a strictly "first come, first served" system based on where you stood in line at the gate. In 2007, Southwest introduced boarding groups based on check-in time. In changing the rules of the game, Southwest incentivized the organized and affluent instead of those who did not value their time. To monetize this incentive, Southwest introduced EarlyBird check-in — customers could pay $10 to have Southwest automatically check them in. But, this didn't guarantee a good seat — it just guaranteed automatic check-in. What would happen if all passengers on a Southwest flight paid for EarlyBird check-in?

Slide 16 of 33: The Southwest matrix in words

Traveler A (rows: regular $0, EarlyBird $10) against Traveler B (columns: the same). Both regular: okay seat each. Regular against EarlyBird: bad seat for the regular traveler, good seat for the EarlyBird. Both EarlyBird: okay seat each.

Slide 17 of 33: The Southwest matrix in numbers

The same matrix with payoffs. Both regular: 3, 3. Regular/EarlyBird: 1, 4. EarlyBird/regular: 4, 1. Both EarlyBird: 2, 2. Steps ring the defection incentives and the both-pay equilibrium at 2, 2.

Slide 18 of 33: Southwest, the effects

What has been the effect of Southwest's putting travelers into a PD? $98 million in EarlyBird revenues for Southwest the first year. 44% growth in revenue from EarlyBird. Now up to $400 million in revenue yearly. Initially, few people paid for EarlyBird, so those that did were guaranteed a good seat. Now, so many people buy it that Southwest even says up-front that it can't guarantee EarlyBird travelers an "A" group seat. It now costs $15 and Southwest is getting sued by customers trapped in the PD.

Slide 19 of 33: A post-Valentine's day PD: Tinder

Tinder users make simultaneous choices to swipe right or left on users (right = like, left = reject). The goal is to match people who are genuinely interested in each other. There are more men on Tinder than women. Recall from your reading what happens to added value when there are more of one thing than another thing in a market (any one male's added value = zero!). But is there an alternative way for men to play the Tinder game rather than selectively swiping right on people that genuinely interest you?

Slide 20 of 33: A prisoner's dilemma for Tinder

Tinder Man 1 (rows) against Tinder Man 2 (columns), choices: express only genuine interest, or just swipe right on everyone. Both genuine: 3, 3. Genuine/swipe-everyone: 1, 4. Swipe-everyone/genuine: 4, 1. Both swipe everyone: 2, 2.

Slide 21 of 33: A prisoner's dilemma for Tinder, continued

How can (or does) Tinder overcome the PD and try to spark matches of genuine interest? One option: limited likes (max = 100 likes per 12 hours). But, does Tinder really want to do this? As a profit-maximizing company, Tinder uses the PD to monetize itself by allowing men to buy their way back into equilibrium if they purchase Tinder Plus/Gold/Platinum. Tinder Plus: unlimited likes! Tinder Gold: see people who like you before you have to swipe (FMA!). Tinder Platinum: prioritized unlimited likes and prioritized Super Likes!

Slide 22 of 33: The Tinder matrix, monetized

The same payoffs with new choices: be constrained by limited likes, or buy Tinder Plus and swipe away. Both constrained: 3, 3. Constrained/buy: 1, 4. Buy/constrained: 4, 1. Both buy: 2, 2.

Slide 23 of 33: The prisoner's dilemma in real life: cigarette advertising

In the 1960s, the U.S. public was increasingly concerned with the harmful effects of smoking. The government proposed a ban on television ads for tobacco products. To the surprise of many, the cigarette industry not only accepted the ban, but they actively encouraged it. Had cigarette companies grown a conscience and decided to behave morally in the face of scientific evidence that their products caused cancer? No, they were game theorists attempting to escape the prisoner's dilemma!

Slide 24 of 33: The cigarette advertising matrix

Marlboro (rows: don't advertise, advertise) against Lucky Strike (columns: the same). Both don't advertise: 50% market share each. Don't/advertise: 20%, 80%. Advertise/don't: 80%, 20%. Both advertise: 50%, 50%.

Slide 25 of 33: The prisoner's dilemma in real life: cartels

Many groups of competitors value having as much control over a given market as possible. One way to maintain control is to band together (i.e., collude) and fix prices at a certain level or reduce output within an industry to keep added values high. Groups that engage in this behavior are sometimes called cartels. Examples: OPEC (oil pricing), De Beers (diamonds), crime syndicates. Cartels are hard to keep together because the PD incentivizes defection.

Slide 26 of 33: Business application: corporations and government taxes

Corporations often pressure municipalities to provide economic incentives in order to remain within a city/county/state/country. Corporations receive at least $80 billion each year from cities by way of incentives. Why do cities give (usually very profitable) companies so much free money? Fear of abandonment. Economic reliance. Political necessity. Thinking from a strategic perspective — companies benefit because they place governments into a prisoner's dilemma.

Slide 27 of 33: A prisoner's dilemma for government incentives

City A (rows: do not subsidize companies, subsidize companies) against City B (columns: the same). Both hold: 3, 3. Hold/subsidize: 1, 4. Subsidize/hold: 4, 1. Both subsidize: 2, 2.

Slide 28 of 33: Corporations and government taxes, continued

Incentives only really grew in popularity in the 1980s — before then, firms tended to be locally based and independently profitable. 1985: GM looks to manufacture its new brand (Saturn) and 30 states make offers. 1990s: BMW then moves to SC ($130 million); Mercedes moves to Alabama ($300 million). United Airlines solicits bids — 90 cities apply. Governor of Illinois calls for a truce between cities — but few sign on! "If you've got some states doing it, it's hard for others not to do it." Of course, there is no requirement that the firm stays for the long-term, nor is there any requirement that governments have a say in firm strategy or decisions.

Slide 29 of 33: But is this truly a prisoner's dilemma?

On the one hand, yes — it is only because corporations can move around that they require incentives to stay where they are or heavy subsidies to leave. On the other hand, no — consider a case like Ireland and the Celtic Tiger. Until it solicited FDI from multinationals, Ireland was one of the poorest countries in Europe. By 2008, Ireland was one of the wealthiest countries in Europe — this wealth was almost entirely generated via government incentives. Then again, where is the Irish economy now?

Slide 30 of 33: From the boardroom to the gridiron: sports relocation incentives

The same logic behind corporate welfare can be easily applied to professional sports in the U.S. context. In the U.S., pro sports teams are not tied to a single city or region (with few exceptions). They can move anytime they want, as long as there is another receptive city and the league approves. The Oakland Raiders were considering moving to Las Vegas, which had no professional football team. The cities of Oakland and Las Vegas could compete for public subsidies to attract/retain the Raiders. How might we model this situation as a prisoner's dilemma?

Slide 31 of 33: A prisoner's dilemma for football relocation

City of Oakland (rows: do not subsidize, subsidize) against City of Las Vegas (columns: the same). Both hold: 3, 3. Hold/subsidize: 1, 4. Subsidize/hold: 4, 1. Both subsidize: 2, 2.

Slide 32 of 33: The problem of the sports subsidy PD and possible solutions

As we are all aware, taxpayer money is a finite resource and many governments are broke. Spending money on sports stadiums is difficult to reconcile against crumbling infrastructure and socioeconomic disparities. Some proposed solutions to the PD? City co-ownership of stadia/teams. Laws banning the use of bonds to pay for stadia. "Just say no" solutions are unlikely — (1) emotional pull of sports; (2) public irrationality; (3) political impracticality.

Slide 33 of 33: Iterated prisoner's dilemma

Does it matter if parties play a PD against each other repeatedly over time? If rational players know ahead of time how many interactions there will be, they should always defect. If there are n plays — and cooperation exists on n-1 plays — it pays to defect on the nth (and last) play. But, by the same logic, then it also pays to defect on n-1 — and so on. So it appears that cooperation should never be rational in the iterated case. And yet…
