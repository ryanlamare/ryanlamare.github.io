MODULE 4

Introduction to non-zero sum games

Recall that zero-sum games require that, in order for one side to win, the other must lose
Zero-sum
Last session’s games – a fixed pie
Non-zero-sum
Both can lose, one can win, or both can win
These are sometimes called “variable sum” games because each player’s payoffs can vary – unlike “constant sum” games
The variance in each side’s payoffs depends on the strategies each utilizes
This module: the most famous non-zero-sum game of them all

Two stations, one junction

Identical fuel, identical drivers. Each week you post £1.50 or £1.40. Drivers go wherever is cheaper; match prices, and you share them. Your sole objective: maximize your own station’s revenue over the six weeks.
MODULE 4 · THE RULES

Six weeks at the junction

  • Every week you post your price without seeing theirs. Then both signs go up, simultaneously
  • One phone for your team. Agree your price, lock it in, and hold the phone up when your junction is called. Once it is locked it cannot be changed
  • Then tap what the other station posted. Your phone records your revenue
  • Weeks 3 and 6 pay double. Before each one you may ask for a two-minute meeting, one person from each station. It happens only if both stations ask, and nothing said in it binds anyone
  • Otherwise, no talking to the other station

MODULE 4 · OVERCOMING

Nice, forgiving, retaliatory, clean

Nice
It never defects first. None of the low scorers in Axelrod’s tournament had that property.
Forgiving
It holds no grudges and has no memory of past deceptions.
Retaliatory
It answers every defection, so nobody can profit by exploiting it.
Clean
Simple enough that the other side can read the rules and cooperate.
Keep in mind that no rule is best independent of its environment. Tit for two tats, which absorbs one extra defection before answering, sometimes does better.

MODULE 4 · TAKEAWAYS

a few key takeaways...

The prisoner’s dilemma
Each side’s dominant strategy leaves both sides worse off
The equilibrium
Where correct individual reasoning lands, not where both players do better
Getting out
Make it repeat, bring in an enforcer, or change the payoffs
Tit for tat
Nice, forgiving, retaliatory, and clean
Don’t be envious
Your own total is the key, not whether you beat the other side
Cooperative solutions to PDs aren’t inherently “good”, and defect solutions aren’t inherently “bad”!
