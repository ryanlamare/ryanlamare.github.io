Prisoner's dilemmas, and how to overcome them. Module 4 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 24: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, play a six-week price setting game against another team. Two, recognize prisoner's dilemmas and learn how to overcome them. Three, spot prisoner's dilemmas in business and society. Four, create your own prisoner's dilemma.

Slide 3 of 24: Introduction to non-zero sum games

Lead line: recall that zero-sum games require that, in order for one side to win, the other must lose. Two pictogram columns: zero-sum, last session's games, a fixed pie; and non-zero-sum, highlighted, where both sides can lose, one side can win, or both sides can win. Reveals: these are sometimes called "variable sum" games because each player's payoffs can vary, unlike "constant sum" games; the variance in each side's payoffs depends on the strategies each utilizes; and a closing chip: today, the most famous non-zero-sum game of them all.

Slide 4 of 24: Two stations, one junction

The game setup, told to the room. Aura Fuels and Buco's Petrol face each other across the same junction on a busy A road. Aura has been there thirty years, but there is family pressure to hang up the spurs. Buco's is the newest upstart, ready to make a mark, but its investors want returns. You sell identical fuel to identical drivers. Each week you post £1.50 or £1.40. Drivers go to the cheaper sign; if you match, you share them. Your sole objective: maximize your own station's revenue over the six weeks. The QR code for the phones is on the next slide.

Slide 5 of 24: Six weeks at the junction

The rules of the six weeks, told to the room. A calendar strip of six boxes, weeks 3 and 6 taller and gold and marked times two, with a speech mark labelled meet? before each of those two. Three reveals: every Monday you post your price without seeing theirs, then both signs go up and everybody sees everything; weeks 3 and 6 pay double, and before each one you may ask for a two-minute meeting, one person from each station, which happens only if both stations ask, and nothing said in it binds anyone; one phone for your team: lock in your price, and hold it up when your junction is called. Slide 6 of 24: The junction, week by week

The slide that stays up while the game is played. The teams play on one phone each, with no server: they lock a price, the phone goes dark, and when the instructor calls their junction the two stations reveal and hold their phones up, green for one pound fifty and striped red for one pound forty; each team then taps what the other side posted and its phone keeps the running revenue. The slide shows the week in play and what it pays, one keypress a step, as a two-by-two matrix in thousands of pounds, Aura on the rows and Buco's on the columns. Week 1 and week 2: both at one pound fifty 12 and 12; Aura at one pound fifty and Buco's at one pound forty 2 and 18; the reverse 18 and 2; both at one pound forty 9 and 9. Before week 3, marked Meet?: the doubled matrix, with the rule that a meeting is two minutes, one person from each station, only if both ask, and binds nobody. Week 3, marked pays double: 24 and 24, 4 and 36, 36 and 4, 18 and 18. Week 4: the ordinary matrix. Then the news fills the slide, before week 5: Breaking news: FuelWatch app disrupts pricing at the junction. Undercut alone: 72 thousand pounds for the week, and the other side gets 2 thousand. Match each other: 12 thousand each at one pound fifty, 9 thousand each at one pound forty. No doubling in week 6. One last meeting before week 5, two minutes, only if both stations ask. Week 5 and week 6, marked FuelWatch: 12 and 12, 2 and 72, 72 and 2, 9 and 9, the changed cells highlighted.

Slide 7 of 24: Price Wars as a payoff matrix

The game they just played as a two-by-two matrix, one ordinary week in thousands of pounds. Aura on the rows, Buco's on the columns, each choosing one pound fifty or one pound forty. Both at one pound fifty: 12 and 12. Aura at one pound fifty and Buco's at one pound forty: 2 and 18. Aura at one pound forty and Buco's at one pound fifty: 18 and 2. Both at one pound forty: 9 and 9. Reveals: whatever the other station posts, cutting pays more, the cutting payoffs circled in each station's colour; so both cut and land on 9 each, boxed in red as the equilibrium; when 12 each was there for the taking, boxed in green with an arrow from the equilibrium to it. You have just played the most famous game in game theory.

Slide 8 of 24: The prisoner's dilemma

Lead line: the most famous, and perhaps most useful, of all non-zero-sum games. A drawn scene: two interrogation rooms with a red dividing wall between them, Suspect A in teal on the left, Suspect B in terracotta on the right, and a badge of two crossed-out speech bubbles above the wall. Caption: arrested and interrogated apart, with no way to coordinate. Confess, or stay silent?

Slide 9 of 24: The PD in payoff matrix form

Same lead line. Suspect A rows, Suspect B columns; each chooses stay silent or confess. Both silent: one year each. A silent, B confesses: twenty-five years for A, B goes free. A confesses, B silent: A goes free, twenty-five years for B. Both confess: three years each. Steps mark the matrix: teal circles around A's payoffs in the confess row, because confessing is A's dominant strategy; terracotta circles around B's payoffs in the confess column; a red box on both-confess, the equilibrium, which holds both circles; and a green box on both-silent, the Pareto-optimal outcome, its payoffs turning green, with a green arrow drawn from the equilibrium to it. The green cell is unreachable if everyone follows their dominant strategies and individual self-interest.

Slide 10 of 24: Strip out the story

The generalized prisoner's dilemma, marked in the same scheme. Side A and Side B each choose between the collective action and the individual one. Both collective pays A to each; both individual pays D to each; if one defects while the other cooperates the defector gets B and the cooperator gets C. Steps: whenever B beats A and D beats C, the individual action is dominant for Side A, circled in teal; and for Side B too, circled in terracotta; so both sides land on D, D, boxed in red; even though A, A, boxed in green with its letters turning green, pays both more. B greater than A greater than D greater than C: any situation whose payoffs fit that ordering is a prisoner's dilemma, whatever it happens to involve.

Slide 11 of 24: Banana Republic and J. Crew

Both price a near-identical blue dress shirt without knowing what the other will charge, marked in the same scheme. At eighty dollars each they make seventy-two thousand dollars each. If one drops to seventy while the other holds, the cheaper firm makes one hundred and ten thousand and the dearer one twenty-four thousand. If both drop, each makes seventy thousand. Steps: cutting to seventy dollars is dominant for both, circled; the equilibrium is a price war at seventy thousand each, boxed in red; when holding at eighty would have paid both more, boxed in green with the arrow. In real life, J. Crew cut first, Banana Republic followed, and the war took both close to bankruptcy.

Slide 12 of 24: Big Tech and AI

Bullets on the left: Big Tech is building AI capacity faster than it can prove demand for it. Spending will not peak until 2028, at around one trillion dollars. Davidson Kempner's CIO: "You have to invest in it because your peers are investing in it." If the other labs don't build the more powerful model, you should, to take the market; if they do, you must, to keep it; so every lab races, and where does it end? A slower build matched to real demand would leave every firm better off. Think of the 2023 open letter asking every lab to "pause" giant AI training for six months: thousands signed, and nobody paused. On the right, a clickable drawn front page for the Business Insider article on Big Tech's AI spend as a prisoner's dilemma.

Slide 13 of 24: Cigarette advertising

In the 1960s, the U.S. public was turning against smoking, and the government proposed a ban on TV ads for tobacco. The industry not only accepted the ban, it encouraged it. Had cigarette companies grown a conscience? No, they were game theorists escaping the prisoner's dilemma. A Mad Men clip, Don Draper the game theorist, is embedded on the right behind a click-to-play cover.

Slide 14 of 24: States bid against each other

A drawn map of the United States showing the Amazon HQ2 bidding war: Amazon in Washington State, thick red arrows to New York and Virginia, rows of dollar coins sized to each bid — about 1.5 billion dollars from New York and 573 million from Virginia, with each coin standing for 250 million dollars offered — and the headline figure that 238 cities bid for Amazon's HQ2.

Slide 15 of 24: Create your own prisoner's dilemma

The closing exercise, talked through in twos or threes, with no phones. Lead line: a situation from one of your worlds where each side is tempted to do the thing that leaves you both worse off. Three numbered questions: one, who are the two sides? Two, what does cooperating look like? Three, what does defecting look like? A keypress fills in the model under each question, Ryan's case: Apple, and Google; don't poach the rival's talent; poach talent from the rival.

Slide 16 of 24: Can repeated games solve prisoner's dilemmas?

A backward-induction chain across the six weeks of the game. Week six has no future game, so undercut; so it pays to undercut in week five; and the same reasoning runs all the way back to week one. Working backward from a known final interaction says cooperation should never appear at all. But in practice it appears constantly. So what does the argument leave out?

Slide 17 of 24: Resolving the prisoner's dilemma

We see voluntary cooperative behavior all the time. In a world full of incentives to defect against each other, why do things often not work this way? Think here about The Dark Knight. The ferry scene as a payoff matrix: the civilians' ferry and the prisoners' ferry each choose whether to push the button. Both refrain: 3, 3. One pushes while the other refrains: 4 for the pusher, 1 for the other. Both push: 2, 2. Discussion reveal: how did the two sides overcome the PD, and what role did communication play?

Slide 18 of 24: The solution, Axelrod's tournament

Robert Axelrod asked under what conditions cooperation will emerge in a world of egoists without central authority. He invited game theorists to submit computer programs, paired every entry against every other, and ran two hundred moves per game, scoring three points each for mutual cooperation, five for defecting alone, one each for mutual defection and nothing for cooperating alone. Three benchmark figures: the theoretical maximum was one thousand; a pair that cooperated throughout scored six hundred each; a pair that defected throughout scored two hundred each. All the best game theorists in the world played each other. So who won?

Slide 19 of 24: Rank the strategies

A live ranking before the winner is named. Seven strategies, alphabetical, each with one line: Always Cooperate (cooperates every move, whatever they do); Always Defect (defects every move); Grudger (cooperates until they defect once, then defects for ever); Random (a coin toss every move); Tit for Tat (cooperates first, then copies their last move); Tit for Two Tats (defects only after they defect twice running); and Tester, marked given, fourth (defects first; if you hit back it copies you from then on, if you don't it keeps cheating). On the right, a QR code for ryanlamare.com/go and a count of rankings in. On the phone each person taps the strategy they think finishes first, then the next, with Tester already placed fourth, and locks the order in.

Slide 20 of 24: The room's tournament

One row per strategy, in the room's consensus order, each labelled with where the room put it and how many did. A button runs the tournament: five copies of each of the seven strategies, every entry against every other for two hundred moves on the same matrix (three each for mutual cooperation, five for defecting alone, one each for mutual defection, nothing for cooperating alone), Random on a fixed seed so the result is the same every time. Each bar grows to the strategy's average points per game, out of a thousand, then the rows slide into the true order: Tit for Tat 504, Grudger 481, Tit for Two Tats 464, Tester 457, Random 427, Always Cooperate 423, Always Defect 379, and the winner is boxed. A keypress adds the verdict: in Axelrod's tournament the winner was Tit for Tat; Always Defect beat it in every game they played, and finished last; and how many in the room put Tit for Tat first. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 21 of 24: Nice, forgiving, retaliatory, clean

The four properties of tit for tat. Nice, drawn as a gold smiling face: it never defects first, and none of the low scorers in Axelrod's tournament had that property. Forgiving, drawn as a gold smiling face with a halo: it holds no grudges and has no memory of past deceptions. Retaliatory: it answers every defection, so nobody can profit by exploiting it. Clean: simple enough that the other side can read the rules and cooperate. Keep in mind that no rule is best independent of its environment; tit for two tats, which absorbs one extra defection before answering, sometimes does better.

Slide 22 of 24: Four lessons

Axelrod's four lessons. Don't be envious: your own total is the key, not whether you beat the other side. Don't be the first to defect: that decision is rarely recoverable. Reciprocate both ways: answer restraint with restraint, defection with defection. Don't be too clever: you can't cooperate with a strategy you can't work out.

Slide 23 of 24: How would you get out of yours?

The second touch, after Four lessons, talked through in the same twos or threes, with no phones. Four numbered ways out of the dilemma they named earlier: one, make it repeat. Two, bring in an enforcer. Three, change the payoffs. Four, there is no way out. A keypress adds the model in red: Apple and Google: they formed a cartel, and the DOJ punished them.

Slide 24 of 24: Where we're going on day two

The close of day one. Three questions for day two, revealed one per keypress: what happens when there is more than one equilibrium, or none at all? How does changing the rules affect our strategies? What if one side knows something the other doesn't?
