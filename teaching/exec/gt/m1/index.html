Some core beliefs I will bring into these sessions (and you can agree with or challenge!). Three pictogram tiles: Strategy is everywhere — strategic thinking can be found everywhere, and we all do it, all the time. A two-circle Venn diagram — business schools and economics — with this programme at the red centre. Economics departments teach courses dedicated entirely to mathematical applications of strategic thinking (Nash equilibrium; zero-sum and non-zero-sum games; payoff matrices; etc.). Slide 6 of 23: Defining game theory

Three quoted definitions revealed one at a time, operative phrases in red: a model of optimality taking into consideration not only benefits less costs, but also the interaction between participants; a set of concepts aimed at strategic decision making in situations of competition and conflict (as well as of cooperation and interdependence) under specified rules; a mathematical theory that deals with strategies for maximizing gains and minimizing losses within prescribed constraints, as in the rules of a card game, widely applied in the solution of various decision-making problems, as those of military strategy and business policy.

Slide 7 of 23: Is an act a singular decision or a broader game?

Two columns: a decision involves a unilateral choice made without regard to others' behaviors; a game involves considerations of how the other side will react to the choices made. Below: we care about broader games, and assume — everyone has strategies, everyone has payoffs, everyone is behaving in their own self-interest.

Four opposed pairs: sequential versus simultaneous; zero-sum versus non-zero sum; single play / single opponent versus repeat play / repeat opponent; fixed rules / complete information versus manipulable rules / incomplete information.

Slide 11 of 23: The first rule of game theory: it's not (just) about you!

At its core, the game theoretic way of thinking strategically requires that whatever action one entity does must affect the other side and vice versa. This means that you cannot think one-dimensionally if you want to think strategically. Everything you do has consequences for both you and your opponent. Equally, everything your opponent does has consequences for both you and your opponent. AND, you both know this to be true! This can be thought of as the requirement for common knowledge. A pinned link plays the Common Knowledge video.

Slide 12 of 23: Common knowledge and equilibrium

The ultimate goal in GT is usually to identify an equilibrium point. This is the point at which each player is engaging in a strategy that is the player's best response to the strategies of the other players. Keep in mind, there doesn't have to be only one equilibrium point! Also keep in mind, an equilibrium point won't always be the most optimal solution! But at its core, game theory explores and finds strategies that generate equilibria.

Slide 13 of 23: A typical example of using common knowledge to find equilibrium

A news photograph of traffic lights. I know to stop on red and go on green. You know that I know to stop on red and go on green. So when the light turns green for you, you proceed safely by using common knowledge. And, therefore, we get a nice equilibrium of stop/go traffic.

Slide 14 of 23: Except sometimes GT sucks

The same photograph zoomed close: each light shows red and green lit at the same time. Look a little closer at the traffic lights. You can actually file lawsuits over this equilibrium failure.

Slide 15 of 23: Let's try one more example of common knowledge

I'm thinking of a number between 1 and 100. You get five guesses — and after each guess, I'll tell you if you need to go higher or lower. I'm looking for one volunteer who is willing to play the game with me. The rest of the room should recommend strategies — the volunteer can either accept or reject this guidance!

Slide 16 of 23: How to guess the correct number

Let's work through one series of efficient guesses. A button plays the efficient sequence — 50 too high, 25 too low, 37 too low, 43 too low — narrowing the range. A step then shows: you've narrowed your last guess down to 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, or 49. So what is the answer?

Slide 17 of 23: How to guess the correct number

This is actually a game about common knowledge, not about guessing the right number. Remember — I am strategic too! The game actually started when you were sent the pre-course reading (if you read it, that is!). If I know that you've done the reading, how should I play this game? If you know that I know that you've done the reading, how should you play this game?

Slide 18 of 23: Lessons from the "I'm thinking of a number" game

Of course, you already know to always put yourself in the other person's shoes! But also know that, people are not random in their strategies! And crucially, you might know something, but I know that you know it! And you should know that I know that you know it! And I should know that you know that I know that you kn…! A good strategist must always be thinking multidimensionally! A pinned link plays The Princess Bride battle of wits.

Slide 19 of 23: Added values game

I have a set of white cards corresponding with the total number of people in this session, and each of you has one yellow card. Senior management has agreed to pay anyone who turns in a pair of white and yellow cards $10. You can negotiate with me individually, but you cannot band together (this is key!). Make me an offer for my card.

Slide 20 of 23: How should this game end up?

An interactive figure with no text: two added-value bars, mine at $210 (21 people times $10 a pair) and yours at $10, and below them a deal-split bar reading $5 and $5 whose divider can be dragged within a small shaded negotiable zone — a 50-50 split with a bit of flexibility.

Slide 21 of 23: Changing the rules of the game

Say I "lose" three cards — now we have fewer white cards, but each of you still has 1 yellow card. Added value matters. Because three of you are guaranteed to end up with nothing, I can walk away from any individual interaction whenever I want — so you're lucky if I give you even $1! The key stipulation was that you could not band together — what would the difference have been if you could have collectively interacted with me? The same interactive figure sits below; a button — I "lose" three cards — collapses your added-value bar to $0 and moves the deal split to $9 against $1, if you're lucky.

Slide 22 of 23: Changing the rules of the game again

Let's reset the card game to its original rules. Say there's a new stipulation — I'll let the first one of you who raises their hand leave the room and I'll guarantee that person the best deal given to any of the other people in the room. Does the rest of the room accept this deal or not? Think about your own self interest! This looks tempting — except it will turn me into a fierce negotiator with everyone. The interactive figure's button — one person leaves — locks the deal split at $5 and $5 with zero flexibility: dragging the divider now refuses to move it.
