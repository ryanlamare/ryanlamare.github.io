Applied Game Theory, Module 1 — Introduction to Game Theory and Strategic Thinking

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 30: Title slide

Game Theory. Introduction to game theory and strategic thinking. Module 1 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 30: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, understand core game theory and strategy concepts. Two, explain common knowledge and equilibrium. Three, recognize when a choice is a decision and when it's a game. Four, play three games of strategic thinking, shown as three red chips: A Game of Drawing; I'm Thinking of a Number; Added Value.

Slide 3 of 30: How familiar are you with game theory as a strategic concept?

A live poll answered on phones at ryanlamare.com/go (QR code shown). Before answering, each phone picks its owner's name once — the class points system runs on these names for the rest of the programme. Four options: I have no idea what game theory is and I am a little terrified right now. I've dabbled in game theory but I don't think about it very much. You know, I'm something of a game theorist myself. Call me John Nash, I spend my life working to reach equilibrium. A response counter rises while the distribution stays hidden; a keypress reveals four horizontal rows, one per option, each filling with one small human figure per answer plus the count. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 4 of 30: A few basic ideas

Some core beliefs I will bring into these sessions. Three pictogram tiles: Strategy is everywhere — strategic thinking can be found everywhere, and we all do it, all the time. You must consider others — strategic thinking requires a consideration of others' behaviors as well as our own. Self-interest is rational — we are at least reasonably rational actors (or are aware of the limits of our rationality).

Slide 5 of 30: Different ways to think about strategy

A two-circle Venn diagram — business schools and economics — with this programme at the red centre. Business schools teach courses on strategic management (differentiation; competitive advantage; added value; etc.). Economics departments teach courses dedicated entirely to mathematical applications of strategic thinking (Nash equilibrium; zero-sum and non-zero-sum games; payoff matrices; etc.). This programme can be thought of as a hybrid of these different approaches.

Slide 6 of 30: Defining game theory

Three quoted definitions revealed one at a time, operative phrases in red: a model of optimality taking into consideration not only benefits less costs, but also the interaction between participants; a set of concepts aimed at strategic decision making in situations of competition and conflict (as well as of cooperation and interdependence) under specified rules; a mathematical theory that deals with strategies for maximizing gains and minimizing losses within prescribed constraints, as in the rules of a card game, widely applied in the solution of various decision-making problems, as those of military strategy and business policy.

Slide 7 of 30: Is an act a singular decision or a broader game?

Two columns: a decision involves a unilateral choice made without regard to others' behaviors; a game involves considerations of how the other side will react to the choices made. Below: we care about broader games, and assume — everyone has strategies, everyone has payoffs, everyone is behaving in their own self-interest.

Slide 8 of 30: Types of games we'll cover

Four opposed pairs: sequential versus simultaneous; zero-sum versus non-zero sum; single play / single opponent versus repeat play / repeat opponent; fixed rules / complete info versus dynamic rules / incomplete info.

Slide 9 of 30: A game of drawing

Everyone draws, on your phones — a QR code opens the drawing page and a counter shows dogs arriving. Your task is to draw the median dog (based on size, facing left or right). The winning drawing will be that which best represents the median dog. Dog sizes will be measured from snout to tail to determine the winner. You have 100 seconds to draw the median dog, on an on-screen countdown timer.

Slide 10 of 30: The median dog

A full-screen dog sanctuary with no title or other text: every submitted drawing at true relative size, sorted small to big, each housed in its own cartoon doghouse — an overhanging gable roof with a small bone on the front, a picket fence, and a nameplate reading the artist's name and snout-to-tail span. A full room of 21 makes three rows of seven. The houses appear one after another on a keypress; a second keypress computes the median span and pins medals on the three dogs nearest it — gold, silver and bronze discs on ribbons, ties sharing a medal — and the gold dog's house turns red. The medals are the announcement. If the poll server is unreachable the sanctuary shows marked demo stick-dogs.

Slide 11 of 30: NOT the median dogs

A photograph of two real dogs in bandanas looking up expectantly. The title says: NOT the median dogs.

Slide 12 of 30: The first rule of game theory: it's not (just) about you!

Bullets on the left: whatever action one entity does must affect the other side, and vice versa — you cannot think one-dimensionally. Everything you do has consequences for both you and your opponent. Equally, everything your opponent does has consequences for both you and your opponent. AND, you both know this to be true! This can be thought of as the requirement for common knowledge. On the right, the Common Knowledge video is embedded behind a click-to-play cover.

Slide 13 of 30: Common knowledge and equilibrium

Bullets on the left: the ultimate goal in GT is usually to identify an equilibrium point — where each player's strategy is their best response to the strategies of the other players. There doesn't have to be only one! And an equilibrium point won't always be the most optimal solution! But at its core, game theory explores and finds strategies that generate equilibria. On the right, a first look at a payoff matrix, marked in the same scheme the prisoner's dilemma module uses: you on the rows, others on the columns, each choosing strategy A or strategy B. Both A: 3 each. You A, they B: 1 for you, 4 for them. You B, they A: 4 for you, 1 for them. Both B: 2 each. Strategy B's payoffs are circled in each player's colour as the dominant strategy, and a red box marks both-B, the equilibrium. A small key names the circles and the box.

Slide 14 of 30: A typical example of using common knowledge to find equilibrium

A news photograph of traffic lights. I know to stop on red and go on green. You know that I know to stop on red and go on green. So when the light turns green for you, you proceed safely by using common knowledge. And, therefore, we get a nice equilibrium of stop/go traffic.

Slide 15 of 30: Except sometimes GT sucks

The same photograph zoomed close: each light shows red and green lit at the same time. Look a little closer at the traffic lights. You can actually file lawsuits over this equilibrium failure.

Slide 16 of 30: Let's try one more example of common knowledge

I'm thinking of a number between 1 and 100. Everyone plays, on your phones — the game runs in five rounds, one guess per round. After each guess, your phone tells you if you need to go higher or lower. Between rounds, the board shows the whole room's guesses — and how they move. On the right, a drawn number line from 1 to 100 with a large red question mark over the middle.

Slide 17 of 30: Lock in your guesses

The whole room plays by phone — a QR code opens the guessing page, and the server answers each guess too high, too low, or correct; the secret number never leaves the server. The board shows five strips, one per round, each a 1-to-100 line: a counter rises as guesses arrive in the open round, and a facilitator button, close round and show, reveals that round's guesses as neutral ink dots (verdict colours mid-game would give the number away) and opens the next round on everyone's phones, so the room watches the distribution tighten round by round. When the game ends the dots recolour — too low in teal, too high in terracotta, correct in red — and a keypress counts the solvers and names the first. The secret number is preset on the server — nothing to set on the day. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 18 of 30: How to guess the correct number

Let's work through one series of efficient guesses. An interactive number line from 1 to 100: tapping anywhere makes that guess, the excluded range shades out, and each guess logs as too high or too low. A button plays the efficient sequence — 50 too high, 25 too low, 37 too low, 43 too low — narrowing the range. A step then shows: you've narrowed your last guess down to 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, or 49. So what is the answer?

Slide 19 of 30: How to guess the correct number

This is actually a game about common knowledge, not about guessing the right number. Remember — I am strategic too! The game actually started when you were sent the pre-course reading (if you read it, that is!). If I know that you've done the reading, how should I play this game? If you know that I know that you've done the reading, how should you play this game? Beside the bullets, a tower of four cards builds one by one, each indented deeper: you might know something; but I know that you know it; and you should know that I know that you know it; and I should know that you know that I know that you kn…!

Slide 20 of 30: Lessons from the "I'm thinking of a number" game

Bullets on the left: of course, you already know to always put yourself in the other person's shoes! But also know that, people are not random in their strategies! And crucially, you might know something, but I know that you know it! And you should know that I know that you know it! And I should know that you know that I know that you kn…! A good strategist must always be thinking multidimensionally! On the right, The Princess Bride battle of wits is embedded behind a click-to-play cover.

Slide 21 of 30: Added value game

Bullets on the left: game theory can result in paradoxical or unusual strategic choices that benefit players' self-interest. Let's consider the idea of added value. I have one white card for every person in this session, and each of you has one yellow card. Senior management has agreed to pay anyone who turns in a pair of white and yellow cards $100. You can negotiate with me individually, but you cannot band together (this is key!). Make me an offer for my card. On the right, a drawn figure: a fan of three white cards labeled mine, one per person, a plus sign, one yellow card labeled yours, and underneath, equals $100 a pair.

Slide 22 of 30: Make me an offer

The live offers board, first game: equal cards. Phones send dollar offers for their share of the $100 (QR code shown, with a rising offers counter); each pending offer appears as a card with the sender's name and amount, and the deck decides it automatically against this game's rule — a share between 40 and 60 dollars is accepted, anything else rejected. The phone shows a big ACCEPTED or REJECTED — one offer per player per game, no second tries — and every decided offer lands as a dot on a zero-to-one-hundred-dollar strip — accepted teal, rejected red — with the mean accepted deal and a count of deals and rejections. If the poll server is unreachable the board shows marked demo data.

Slide 23 of 30: What happens if I "lose" three cards?

The rule change only — no analysis yet, so the game isn't given away. Say I "lose" three cards: now we have fewer white cards, but each of you still has 1 yellow card. Let's play again. On the right, a drawn figure: the fan of three white cards each crossed out in red, labeled three of mine, gone, and the yellow card untouched, labeled still one each.

Slide 24 of 30: Make me another offer

The live offers board again, second game: after I lose three. New offers are decided the same way, now against a harder rule — only a share of ten dollars or less is accepted — and land on a second strip labeled after I lose three, shown under the equal-cards strip so the room can compare the two distributions. If the poll server is unreachable the board shows marked demo data.

Slide 25 of 30: Added value matters

The debrief, after the second game has been played. Because three of you are guaranteed to end up with nothing, I can walk away from any individual interaction whenever I want — so you're lucky if I give you even $10! The key stipulation was that you could not band together — what would the difference have been if you could have collectively interacted with me? The crossed-out-cards figure sits on the right.

Slide 26 of 30: Tapas Airways

The running deal for the whole programme, introduced. A drawn top view of a Tapas Airways twin-engine jet — a swept wing on each side with an engine slung beneath it facing forward, and a slanted TA monogram on the tail fin — beside a spare engine sitting on a stand, labeled the spare engine. Bullets revealed one at a time: Tapas Airways flies ten A330neos out of Madrid, and they need a spare engine. A spare Trent 7000 costs millions, and they want the best deal possible. Several funders are competing for this deal, and you are one of them. The airline knows things about its own position that you don't. Get this deal, and you may get their business for the next decade. A closing red chip asks: how can common knowledge help us in this game?

Slide 27 of 30: Granito Air

The second running deal for the programme, introduced right after Tapas. A drawn top view of a Granito Air twin-engine jet with a GA monogram on a blue tail fin, above a row of five small spare engines on stands, labeled five engines in storage. Bullets revealed one at a time: Granito Air flies seven A330ceos out of Parma, and they are retiring them; five Trent 700 engines are sitting in storage. Those engines are worth millions, whole or in parts, and the airline wants the best deal possible. Several traders are competing for them, and you are one of them. The airline knows things about its own engines that you don't. Get this deal, and there are more engines behind it, and maybe the whole fleet. The same closing red chip asks: how can common knowledge help us in this game?

Slide 28 of 30: What do you need to know about the other players?

A group write-in exercise on the Tapas deal. In your groups: before you bid, what would you want to know about the airline, your competitors, or anyone else at the table? Questions are sent from phones — a QR code links to the write-in page at ryanlamare.com/go, and a counter shows how many questions are in. When the instructor calls time, a keypress fades the setup and fills the screen with everyone's questions as a wall of coloured cards, identical questions grouped with a times-N marker. The spoken debrief sorts the wall into what everyone in the deal already knows — common knowledge — and what only the airline knows, which is where the final session will pick up. If the poll server is unreachable the wall shows marked demo data.

Slide 29 of 30: This was just the opening move

The wrap-up. Today was the foundation: strategic thinking, common knowledge, and the search for equilibrium. A two-player pictogram sits centre. A reveal asks: what happens when the game unfolds move by move — and you can reason backward from the end? A closing chip: next session, sequential strategies — thinking forward and reasoning backward.

Slide 30 of 30: Top of the class

The break scoreboard, computed live from the poll rooms: the top five names, revealed fifth place first, one keypress at a time, then left on screen through the break. Columns show each player's points from the median dog game and the number game, plus the total. Top five only, never a full ranking; each participant can check their own running total privately at ryanlamare.com/go/points. If the score server is unreachable the board shows marked demo data.
