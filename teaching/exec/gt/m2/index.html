Slide 1 of 14: Title slide

Sequential Strategies. Module 2 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 14: Introductory concepts

Recall that there are two overarching types of strategic interactions — those that involve sequential choices, and those that involve simultaneous choices. Strategic interactions can be wholly sequential, wholly simultaneous, or have elements of both. This lesson focuses on sequential thinking, and the next several will introduce simultaneous thinking.

Slide 3 of 14: Introductory concepts

Also recall a key takeaway from last class: always put yourself in the other person's shoes! Let's add another key ingredient for strategic thinking to this: always be thinking about the future! Every good strategist, from managers to employees to car salespeople to Survivor contestants, thinks not just about the move at hand, but about the effects of that move on the future. The very best strategists can think many, many moves into the future and deduce the best course of action in the present.

Slide 4 of 14: Robert Frost, game theorist

A quote card: "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I — I took the road less traveled by, And that has made all the difference." We can think of the roads diverged in a wood as branches on a game tree.

Slide 5 of 14: Characteristics of game trees

Game trees consist of nodes, branches, and paths of play. To uncover what one player should do first, a tree can be constructed showing all the possible payoffs for all players at each decision node. Trees can be "pruned" as decisions are made to reduce the number of possible outcomes. Start with the terminal nodes and "rollback" or use backward induction to find your optimal strategy. Eventually, you will reach rollback equilibrium, where all players are choosing their optimal strategies. To form a strategy, all players must devise a complete course of action for all possible interactions.

Slide 6 of 14: Solving games using trees

Let's consider a question many of us face in life: should I smoke or not? Ryan has two initial choices — try smoking or do not try smoking. If he never tries smoking, let's give him a payoff of 0. If he tries smoking, he then has two choices — continue, or quit. If he continues smoking after he tries it, let's give him a payoff of minus 1. If he decides to quit smoking after trying it, let's give him a payoff of 1.

Slide 7 of 14: The smoking game tree

A game tree drawn step by step. The first node asks: should Ryan smoke? The YES branch leads to a second node: should Ryan keep smoking? Its YES branch pays minus 1, its NO branch pays 1. The NO branch from the first node pays 0. Rolling back: at the second node, quitting (payoff 1) beats continuing (payoff minus 1), so the continue branch is pruned. The path try-then-quit is highlighted in red, and the closing line asks: Ryan's best strategy here is to try smoking, then quit?

Slide 8 of 14: Solving games using trees

But what are we missing here? The cost of addiction and the presence of your future self!

Slide 9 of 14: The smoking game tree with Future Ryan

The same tree redrawn with two players. The first node asks: should TODAY'S Ryan smoke? The YES branch leads to: should FUTURE Ryan keep smoking? Future Ryan's YES branch pays minus 1 to Today's Ryan and 1 to Future Ryan; his NO branch pays 1 to Today's Ryan and minus 1 to Future Ryan. The never-smoke branch pays 0. Rolling back: Future Ryan is the addict and picks keep smoking (his 1 beats his minus 1), so the quit branch is pruned. Today's Ryan therefore faces minus 1 from trying versus 0 from never trying — and the never-smoke path is highlighted in red.

Slide 10 of 14: Advantages of sequential strategies

One of the advantages of thinking about sequential strategies is that in many real-world situations (especially business and politics) moves are often made in this way rather than being made simultaneously. Politicians might decide to veto or sign bills, to challenge or not challenge incumbents, to enter or not enter presidential races, etc. Businesses might decide to merge or not merge, to hire or not hire certain workers, to enter or not enter into markets, etc. Often you know (or at least have a good idea of) what the other side will do before you have to make a decision.

Slide 11 of 14: Application to business strategy: the first-mover advantage

The first-mover advantage represents the benefits an entity receives from being the first to enter a space. Potential rewards include market share; goodwill from press or customers; securing a rare resource. First-movers can master their domains; can preempt competitors' access to the market; and can create customer/employee loyalty. Examples: Radiohead with In Rainbows (what do other bands do in response?), CVS and cigarettes (what do Walgreens and Rite-Aide do in response?). Is the first-mover always at an advantage? We know it isn't! "Hey Siri" versus "Ok Google".

Slide 12 of 14: Other examples where backward reasoning helps

Tic-Tac-Toe. Checkers. Chess. And the programme game (a swappable slot — Azul in the university course).

Slide 13 of 14: The limits of sequential reasoning

Complexity of drawing game trees to completion (even tic tac toe is hard!). Requirement of complete information (in reality, uncertainty plagues us all). Limits of rationality in sequential decision-making (think ultimatum and centipede games).
