Applied Game Theory, Module 2 — Sequential Strategies

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 24: Title slide

Sequential Strategies. Module 2 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 24: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives, while a small game tree grows beside them, one stage per objective: one, reason sequentially about strategic decisions (a root node sprouts two branches). Two, chart a complete course of action with a decision tree (the branches split again). Three, weigh the value of the first-mover advantage (payoff boxes appear at the ends). Four, recognize the limits of sequential reasoning (one path through the tree is picked out in red).

Slide 3 of 24: Two kinds of strategic interaction

Two columns. Sequential: one side moves, then the other responds — shown as two figures with an arrow from the first to the second. Simultaneous: both sides choose at the same time — shown as two figures with arrows meeting in the middle. Below, a recalled takeaway from our last session — always put yourself in the other person's shoes — and then a new key ingredient added, highlighted in red: always be thinking about the future. A final line: this module focuses on sequential thinking, and the next several will introduce simultaneous thinking.

Slide 4 of 24: Robert Frost, game theorist

A quote card: "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I — I took the road less traveled by, And that has made all the difference." We can think of the roads diverged in a wood as branches on a game tree.

Slide 5 of 24: Characteristics of game trees

Frost's poem drawn as a tree: a box reading "two roads diverged" splits into "the road less traveled by," ending at "all the difference," and "the road more traveled," ending at "no difference." After five numbered points, tapping the tree (or the final keypress) swaps each phrase for the game-theory term it represents: the box becomes a decision node, the roads become branches, and the endings become payoffs. The five points: game trees consist of nodes, branches, and paths of play; payoffs sit at the terminal nodes; trees can be pruned as decisions are made; rollback means starting at the end and reasoning backward; rollback equilibrium is every player choosing their optimal strategy.

Slide 7 of 24: A year on, you're hooked

A second live decision, the next question on the same phone page: the habit is expensive, and it's catching up with your health. What do you do? Two options, quit now or keep smoking. A keypress reveals the same Isotype rows of answers.

Slide 8 of 24: Should you smoke, or not? — the tree

An interactive game tree, payoffs in teal. The first node asks: should you smoke? Its NO branch pays 0, annotated you never start. The YES branch leads to a second node: do you keep smoking? Its YES branch pays minus 1, annotated hooked for life; its NO branch pays 1, annotated you quit in time. Tapping the branch to prune (or a keypress) strikes out keep smoking, since quitting pays 1 against minus 1, and highlights the smoke-then-quit path in red. The closing caption asks: so the best move is to smoke for a while, then quit?

Slide 9 of 24: Should you smoke, or not? — the two-player version

The identical tree at the identical size, so the slide change reads as one tree carrying on. A first keypress reveals the caption: but who makes the second move? Future you — and future you is addicted. The tree then morphs into a two-player game: an owner tag appears over each node — today's you owns the smoking decision, future you owns the quit-or-keep decision — and each payoff gains a second number for future you in a plain face, while today's teal numbers stay exactly as they were: keep smoking pays minus 1 to today's you and 1 to addicted future you; quitting pays 1 to today's you and minus 1 to future you; never starting pays 0. Future you will not quit, so tapping prunes the quit branch, and the never-start path is highlighted in red with its payoff of 0. A last keypress adds a line computed from the room's two polls: how many said they would smoke now, and how many said they would quit a year on; that is exactly the plan future you will not keep.

Slide 10 of 24: The first-mover advantage

The first-mover advantage represents the benefits an entity receives from being the first to enter a space: market share, goodwill, a rare resource. Three cards: Radiohead, first mover — In Rainbows, pay-what-you-want; what do other bands do in response? CVS, first mover — dropped cigarettes in 2014; what do Walgreens and Rite-Aid do in response? "Hey Siri" versus "OK Google" — is the first-mover always at an advantage? We know it isn't!

Slide 11 of 24: Charting a complete course of action

To form a strategy, all players must devise a complete course of action for all possible interactions. Three rows build from chartable to impossible, each with the game's pictogram, a headline number, and one line of reading. Tic-Tac-Toe: 255,168 possible games — nine first moves, then eight, then seven, and played perfectly it is always a draw. Checkers: five times ten to the twentieth positions — a computer finally charted them all in 2007, after eighteen years of computing, and played perfectly it is also a draw. Chess: ten to the one-hundred-twentieth possible games — more games than there are atoms in the observable universe; no computer has solved it, and none is close. On the right, a link plays the Game of Thrones clip, Littlefinger counsels Sansa — fight every battle, everywhere, always, in your mind.

Slide 12 of 24: Three games

Three pictogram tiles introducing the games ahead, by their game-theory names: a game of backward reasoning — take the last card. The ultimatum game — one offer, split a thousand pounds. The centipede game — take the pot, or let it grow.

Slide 13 of 24: A game of backward reasoning

The rules, revealed one at a time: twenty-three cards are on the table between you and your neighbor. You take turns removing one, two, or three cards. Whoever takes the last card wins. Play it on your phones — whoever's first name is nearest to A goes first. The phones open the game page from ryanlamare.com/go, and a figure is added to an Isotype row for every pair that finishes, with a rising games-done counter. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 14 of 24: Solving the game

A number line from 0 to 23 with backward arcs: remove three first to leave 20, then answer every removal by landing on 16, 12, 8, then 4 — a shaded band marks the positions of one to three left, where your opponent must hand you the last card. Caption: think forward and reason backward — whoever faces four cards loses, so leave 20 after your first move, then answer every removal back to 16, 12, 8, and 4, and the last card is yours. A final keypress adds a computed line from the room's own phone games: how many of the winners were already leaving multiples of four.

Slide 15 of 24: The ultimatum game

A draggable split bar over one pot of one thousand pounds: the divider slides between ninety-nine to one and one to ninety-nine, starting at five hundred each, with the proposer's share in teal on the left and the responder's share in terracotta on the right. The rules, in full: there is one thousand pounds to split between you and the person next to you. The person whose first name is nearest to A proposes a split from their phone. The second person sees the offer land and accepts or rejects. If they reject, nobody gets anything. The game page is already open on the phones: the game is played live between the pair's two phones — the proposer picks their partner and sends an offer, and the partner's phone receives it.

Slide 16 of 24: The ultimatum game: theory vs your results

The room's real play, streaming in live as each pair finishes their game on their phones with a rising pairs counter. A keypress reveals every offer as a dot on a zero-to-one-thousand-pound axis — accepted offers in teal, rejected in red — with a dashed marker at the average offer. Further keypresses walk the reading: rollback theory says offer a token amount, it's accepted; a computed line gives the room's average offer and how many were rejected; and the lesson — a "rational" lowball can cost you the deal. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 17 of 24: The centipede game

A strip of ten turns, players one and two alternating, the pot growing from one hundred pounds to a capped thousand. The rules, in full: one hundred pounds is on the table. Player 1 can take it, or pass. Each pass adds another hundred, and the choice switches sides. At one thousand pounds the game ends, and whoever's turn it is takes it all. Played in pairs, on your phones, live between the two devices; this time whoever's first name is nearest to Z is Player 1 and moves first.

Slide 18 of 24: The centipede game: theory vs your results

The room's real play, streaming in live as each pair finishes their game on their phones with a rising pairs counter. A keypress reveals where every pair's game ended as dots over the ten turns — early takes on turns one to three in red, later takes and full runs in teal, the last position labeled RAN for pots that reached one thousand pounds. Further keypresses: rollback theory says someone takes at the end, so the logic says grab on turn one; a computed line gives the most common ending and how many pairs ran it all the way; and the lesson — short-term grabs wreck relationships worth keeping. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 19 of 24: A tree from my world — negotiating a job offer

The model branch, drawn as a vertical stem with the instructor's own situation and revealed one press at a time. At the bottom, the root: you, today, and the other player, the hiring manager. At the top, the yes as a leaf, outlined: I need the salary I asked for. Each press adds one node up the stem with its options open, and prunes the node before it, striking out the options not taken. Your moves hang to the left of the stem in teal, their replies to the right in terracotta. Round one, you open: accept the offer as it is; counter with a number; ask if there is any flexibility. Taken: ask if there is any flexibility. Their reply, one of accept, reject or counter, with accept marked not on the first ask: they counter, what did you have in mind? Round two, your next move: say my number; say a number above it; ask for something other than money. Taken: say my number. Their reply: they counter, meet me halfway. The last press prunes that reply and leaves the leaf outlined with the question the instructor puts to the room: two rounds, no yes yet, a third move or stop?

Slide 20 of 24: Build your branch

The setup for the closing exercise. In groups of three: one yes that somebody in the group needs in their work and cannot get alone, grown as a branch on one phone. Four lines revealed one at a time down the left, each with its colour. The yes you need: I need blank from blank; a signature, a sign-off, a price, a person, a date, and the second blank is the other player. You open: how do you open? Two or three ways, then tap the one you would take; the rest are pruned. They reply: they reject, or they counter; nobody gets a yes on the first ask; tap the reply you expect and write what it sounds like. Your next move: given that reply, what do you do? Then their reply again, and now they can say yes; only a yes ends it; two rounds, and a third if you need one. On the right, a QR code for the branch page at ryanlamare.com/go and a rising count of branches in.

Slide 21 of 24: The room's tree

A full-screen tree drawn live from the phones. A trunk rises from a ground line at the bottom centre; the model branch from the slide before stands straight up from it in teal, and every group's branch fans out left and right of it as its lines arrive, steep branches leaving the trunk high and flat ones low. Each branch is a zigzag of segments, one per node, with a small dot at each node, teal for your moves and terracotta for their replies; every option a group considered and did not take is a short faded twig off that node, so a group that weighed more openings grows a bushier limb and a group that needed a third round grows a longer one. The leaf at the tip of each branch is outlined while the group is still writing, fills red when they get their yes, and stays grey if the group stopped without one. A count of branches in sits bottom right; a REPLAY THE GROWTH button bottom left regrows the whole tree from the stump in the order the lines arrived. Once the room has finished, each arrow press dims the tree, turns one branch red, and opens a panel with that branch legible: the yes at the top, the root at the bottom, the rounds up the stem with the taken option in bold and the pruned ones struck through, their replies labelled accept, reject or counter with what they said, and the question for the group at the top of the panel: which of their replies are you least sure about? The final press closes the panel and lists every group's yes beside the tree. If the poll server is unreachable the tree shows marked demo data.

Slide 22 of 24: The limits of sequential reasoning

Three numbered points, each with a small pictogram: complexity of drawing game trees to completion, even tic-tac-toe is hard (a node exploding into branches); requirement of complete information — in reality, uncertainty plagues us all (two cards, one face down, one marked with a question mark); limits of rationality in sequential decision-making — think of the games we just played (a head with a jagged line of thought inside).

Slide 23 of 24: What did we learn?

Five pictogram tiles, revealed one at a time, each a lesson drawn from the module's own slides. Think forward, reason backward: start at the end, and prune your way back to today (a node exploding into branches). Who makes the second move? Future you is not today's you, and the other side is not you either (two figures, one moving then the other). First mover isn't always ahead: moving first frames the game; it doesn't win it (a flag, with a cross beside it). You don't need the whole tree: nobody charts every path; find who moves last (a tic-tac-toe grid). Real people don't roll back: a lowball costs the deal, a grab costs the relationship (the centipede's pot).

Slide 24 of 24: Top of the table

The break scoreboard, computed live from the game rooms: the top five names, revealed fifth place first, one keypress at a time, then left on screen through the break. Columns show each player's points from the last card and centipede games, plus a total that carries Module 1 points too. Top five only, never a full ranking; each participant can check their own running total privately at ryanlamare.com/go/points. If the score server is unreachable the board shows marked demo data.
