Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order.

Slide 1 of 21: Title slide

Sequential Strategies. Module 2 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 21: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives, while a small game tree grows beside them, one stage per objective: one, reason sequentially about strategic decisions (a root node sprouts two branches). Two, chart a complete course of action with a decision tree (the branches split again). Three, weigh the value of the first-mover advantage (payoff boxes appear at the ends). Four, recognize the limits of sequential reasoning (one path through the tree is picked out in red).

Slide 3 of 21: Two kinds of strategic interaction

Two columns. Sequential: one side moves, then the other responds — shown as two figures with an arrow from the first to the second. Simultaneous: both sides choose at the same time — shown as two figures with arrows meeting in the middle. Below, a recalled takeaway from our last session — always put yourself in the other person's shoes — and then a new key ingredient added, highlighted in red: always be thinking about the future. A final line: this module focuses on sequential thinking, and the next several will introduce simultaneous thinking.

Slide 4 of 21: Robert Frost, game theorist

A quote card: "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I — I took the road less traveled by, And that has made all the difference." We can think of the roads diverged in a wood as branches on a game tree.

Slide 5 of 21: Characteristics of game trees

Frost's poem drawn as a tree: a box reading "two roads diverged" splits into "the road less traveled by," ending at "all the difference," and "the road more traveled," ending at "no difference." After five numbered points, tapping the tree (or the final keypress) swaps each phrase for the game-theory term it represents: the box becomes a decision node, the roads become branches, and the endings become payoffs. The five points: game trees consist of nodes, branches, and paths of play; payoffs sit at the terminal nodes; trees can be pruned as decisions are made; rollback means starting at the end and reasoning backward; rollback equilibrium is every player choosing their optimal strategy.

Slide 7 of 21: Welcome to the price war

A second live decision, answered by scanning a second QR code: you cut, but so did your rival, and you're both bleeding money. What should you do? Two options, return prices to normal or keep cutting. A keypress reveals the same Isotype rows of answers.

Slide 8 of 21: Do we cut prices?

An interactive game tree, payoffs in teal. The first node asks: do we cut prices? Its NO branch pays 0, annotated hold steady. The YES branch leads to a second node: do we keep discounting? Its YES branch pays minus 1, annotated price war; its NO branch pays 1, annotated gain market share. Tapping the branch to prune (or a keypress) strikes out keep discounting, since stopping pays 1 against minus 1, and highlights the cut-then-stop path in red. The closing caption asks: so the best move is to cut, gain market share, then stop the discount?

Slide 9 of 21: Do we cut prices? — the two-player version

The identical tree at the identical size, so the slide change reads as one tree carrying on. A first keypress reveals the caption: but what are we missing? Price wars are addictive. The tree then morphs into a two-player game: an owner tag appears over each node — today's team owns the cut-prices node, the future team owns the keep-discounting node — and each payoff gains a second number for the future team in a plain face, while today's teal numbers stay exactly as they were: keep discounting pays minus 1 to today and 1 to the future team; stopping pays 1 to today and minus 1 to the future team; not cutting pays 0. The future team won't stop the discount, so tapping prunes the stop branch, and the hold-prices path is highlighted in red with its payoff of 0.

Slide 10 of 21: The first-mover advantage

The first-mover advantage represents the benefits an entity receives from being the first to enter a space: market share, goodwill, a rare resource. Three cards: Radiohead, first mover — In Rainbows, pay-what-you-want; what do other bands do in response? CVS, first mover — dropped cigarettes in 2014; what do Walgreens and Rite-Aid do in response? "Hey Siri" versus "OK Google" — is the first-mover always at an advantage? We know it isn't! A further keypress brings up a live write-in: now you — name a first mover who lost, sent from phones via a QR code to ryanlamare.com/go, with a rising counter. When the instructor calls time, a keypress fades the cards and fills the screen with the room's answers as a wall of coloured tags, identical answers grouped with a times-N marker. If the poll server is unreachable the wall shows marked demo data.

Slide 11 of 21: Charting a complete course of action

To form a strategy, all players must devise a complete course of action for all possible interactions. Three rows build from chartable to impossible, each with the game's pictogram, a headline number, and one line of reading. Tic-Tac-Toe: 255,168 possible games — nine first moves, then eight, then seven: small enough to chart by hand, and played perfectly it is always a draw. Checkers: five times ten to the twentieth positions — a computer finally charted them all in 2007, after eighteen years of computing, and played perfectly it is also a draw. Chess: ten to the one-hundred-twentieth possible games — more games than there are atoms in the observable universe; no computer has solved it, and none is close. On the right, a link plays the Game of Thrones clip, Littlefinger counsels Sansa — fight every battle, everywhere, always, in your mind.

Slide 12 of 21: Three games

Three pictogram tiles introducing the games ahead, by their game-theory names: a game of backward reasoning — take the last card. The ultimatum game — one offer, split a thousand pounds. The centipede game — take the pot, or let it grow.

Slide 13 of 21: A game of backward reasoning

The rules, in full: twenty-three cards are on the table. Two teams take turns removing one, two, or three cards. The team that takes the last card wins. Each team has a maximum of one minute to decide how many to remove each round. Below the rules sits the pile itself: twenty-three cards with buttons to take one, two, or three, and a remaining count. The instructor taps whatever each team calls, and the crossed-out cards and count track the game as the room plays it.

Slide 14 of 21: Now play it against your neighbor

The same game played one on one, live between each pair's phones. A QR code joins the game page: pick your partner, and whoever's first name is nearest to A goes first; on your turn you take one, two, or three cards, and taking the last card wins. A figure is added to an Isotype row for every pair that finishes, with a rising games-done counter. The unit line restates the rules. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 15 of 21: Solving the game

A number line from 0 to 23 with backward arcs: remove three first to leave 20, then answer every removal by landing on 16, 12, 8, then 4 — a shaded band marks the positions of one to three left, where your opponent must hand you the last card. Caption: think forward and reason backward — whoever faces four cards loses, so leave 20 after your first move, then answer every removal back to 16, 12, 8, and 4, and the last card is yours. A final keypress adds a computed line from the room's own phone games: how many of the winners were already leaving multiples of four.

Slide 16 of 21: The ultimatum game

A draggable split bar over one pot of one thousand pounds: the divider slides between ninety-nine to one and one to ninety-nine, starting at five hundred each, with the proposer's share in teal on the left and the responder's share in terracotta on the right. The rules, in full: there is one thousand pounds to split between you and the person next to you. The person whose first name is nearest to A proposes a split from their phone. The second person sees the offer land and accepts or rejects. If they reject, nobody gets anything. A QR code beside the rules joins the game page: the game is played live between the pair's two phones — the proposer picks their partner and sends an offer, and the partner's phone receives it.

Slide 17 of 21: The ultimatum game: theory vs your results

The room's real play, streaming in live as each pair finishes their game on their phones (QR code shown for anyone still joining) with a rising pairs counter. A keypress reveals every offer as a dot on a zero-to-one-thousand-pound axis — accepted offers in teal, rejected in red — with a dashed marker at the average offer. Further keypresses walk the reading: rollback theory says offer a token amount, it's accepted; a computed line gives the room's average offer and how many were rejected; and the lesson — a "rational" lowball can cost you the deal. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 18 of 21: The centipede game

A strip of ten turns, players one and two alternating, the pot growing from one hundred pounds to a capped thousand. The rules, in full: one hundred pounds is on the table. Player 1 can take it, or pass. Each pass adds another hundred, and the choice switches sides. At one thousand pounds the game ends, and whoever's turn it is takes it all. Played in pairs, on your phones, live between the two devices; whoever's first name is nearest to A is Player 1.

Slide 19 of 21: The centipede game: theory vs your results

The room's real play, streaming in live as each pair finishes their game on their phones (QR code shown for anyone still joining) with a rising pairs counter. A keypress reveals where every pair's game ended as dots over the ten turns — early takes on turns one to three in red, later takes and full runs in teal, the last position labeled RAN for pots that reached one thousand pounds. Further keypresses: rollback theory says someone takes at the end, so the logic says grab on turn one; a computed line gives the most common ending and how many pairs ran it all the way; and the lesson — short-term grabs wreck relationships worth keeping. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 20 of 21: The limits of sequential reasoning

Three numbered points, each with a small pictogram: complexity of drawing game trees to completion, even tic-tac-toe is hard (a node exploding into branches); requirement of complete information — in reality, uncertainty plagues us all (two cards, one face down, one marked with a question mark); limits of rationality in sequential decision-making — think of the games we just played (a head with a jagged line of thought inside).
