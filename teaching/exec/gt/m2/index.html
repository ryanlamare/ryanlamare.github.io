Slide 1 of 28: Title slide

Sequential Strategies. Module 2 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 28: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives, while a small game tree grows beside them, one stage per objective: one, reason sequentially about strategic decisions (a root node sprouts two branches). Two, chart a complete course of action with a decision tree (the branches split again). Three, weigh the value of the first-mover advantage (payoff boxes appear at the ends). Four, recognize the limits of sequential reasoning (one path through the tree is picked out in red).

Slide 3 of 28: Two kinds of strategic interaction

Two columns. Sequential: one side moves, then the other responds — shown as two figures with an arrow from the first to the second. Simultaneous: both sides choose at the same time — shown as two figures with arrows meeting in the middle. Below, a recalled takeaway from our last session — always put yourself in the other person's shoes — and then a new key ingredient added, highlighted in red: always be thinking about the future. A final line: this module focuses on sequential thinking, and the next several will introduce simultaneous thinking.

Slide 4 of 28: Robert Frost, game theorist

A quote card: "Two roads diverged in a wood, and I — I took the road less traveled by, And that has made all the difference." We can think of the roads diverged in a wood as branches on a game tree.

Slide 5 of 28: Characteristics of game trees

Frost's poem drawn as a tree: a box reading "two roads diverged" splits into "the road less traveled by," ending at "all the difference," and "the road more traveled," ending at "no difference." After five numbered points, tapping the tree (or the final keypress) swaps each phrase for the game-theory term it represents: the box becomes a decision node, the roads become branches, and the endings become payoffs. The five points: game trees consist of nodes, branches, and paths of play; payoffs sit at the terminal nodes; trees can be pruned as decisions are made; rollback means starting at the end and reasoning backward; rollback equilibrium is every player choosing their optimal strategy.

Slide 8 of 28: Should you smoke, or not? — the tree

An interactive game tree, payoffs in teal. The first node asks: should you smoke? Its NO branch pays 0, annotated you never start. The YES branch leads to a second node: do you keep smoking? Its YES branch pays minus 1, annotated hooked for life; its NO branch pays 1, annotated you quit in time. Tapping the branch to prune (or a keypress) strikes out keep smoking, since quitting pays 1 against minus 1, and highlights the smoke-then-quit path in red. The closing caption asks: so the best move is to smoke for a while, then quit?

Slide 9 of 28: Should you smoke, or not? — the two-player version

The identical tree at the identical size, so the slide change reads as one tree carrying on. A first keypress reveals the caption: but who makes the second move? Future you — and future you is addicted. The tree then morphs into a two-player game: an owner tag appears over each node — today's you owns the smoking decision, future you owns the quit-or-keep decision — and each payoff gains a second number for future you in a plain face, while today's teal numbers stay exactly as they were: keep smoking pays minus 1 to today's you and 1 to addicted future you; quitting pays 1 to today's you and minus 1 to future you; never starting pays 0. Future you will not quit, so tapping prunes the quit branch, and the never-start path is highlighted in red with its payoff of 0.

Slide 11 of 28: Charting a complete course of action

To form a strategy, all players must devise a complete course of action for all possible interactions. Three rows build from chartable to impossible, each with the game's pictogram, a headline number, and one line of reading. Tic-Tac-Toe: 255,168 possible games — nine first moves, then eight, then seven: small enough to chart by hand, and played perfectly it is always a draw. Checkers: five times ten to the twentieth positions — a computer finally charted them all in 2007, after eighteen years of computing, and played perfectly it is also a draw. Chess: ten to the one-hundred-twentieth possible games — more games than there are atoms in the observable universe; no computer has solved it, and none is close. On the right, a link plays the Game of Thrones clip, Littlefinger counsels Sansa — fight every battle, everywhere, always, in your mind.

Slide 12 of 28: Three games

Three pictogram tiles introducing the games ahead, by their game-theory names: a game of backward reasoning — take the last card. The ultimatum game — one offer, split a thousand pounds. The centipede game — take the pot, or let it grow.

Slide 13 of 28: A game of backward reasoning

The rules, revealed one at a time: twenty-three cards are on the table between you and your neighbor. You take turns removing one, two, or three cards. Whoever takes the last card wins. Play it on your phones — whoever's first name is nearest to A goes first. A QR code joins the game page, and a figure is added to an Isotype row for every pair that finishes, with a rising games-done counter. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 14 of 28: Solving the game

A number line from 0 to 23 with backward arcs: remove three first to leave 20, then answer every removal by landing on 16, 12, 8, then 4 — a shaded band marks the positions of one to three left, where your opponent must hand you the last card. Caption: think forward and reason backward — whoever faces four cards loses, so leave 20 after your first move, then answer every removal back to 16, 12, 8, and 4, and the last card is yours. A final keypress adds a computed line from the room's own phone games: how many of the winners were already leaving multiples of four.

Slide 15 of 28: How many ways could the card game have played out?

A solo game, asked immediately after playing and solving: you've just played it — how many different ways could one full game have run, start to finish? Closest guess wins. Guesses are sent from phones via a QR code, with a rising counter. A keypress reveals every guess as a dot on a logarithmic axis running from one to one billion; a further keypress lands the true number, 755,476, as a red marker — usually far to the right of the room's guesses; then a computed line names the closest guesser. The closing lesson: nobody won that game by charting 755,476 paths — you found who moves last and reasoned backward. If the poll server is unreachable the slide runs on marked demo data.

Slide 16 of 28: The ultimatum game

A draggable split bar over one pot of one thousand pounds: the divider slides between ninety-nine to one and one to ninety-nine, starting at five hundred each, with the proposer's share in teal on the left and the responder's share in terracotta on the right. The rules, in full: there is one thousand pounds to split between you and the person next to you. The person whose first name is nearest to A proposes a split from their phone. The second person sees the offer land and accepts or rejects. If they reject, nobody gets anything. A QR code beside the rules joins the game page: the game is played live between the pair's two phones — the proposer picks their partner and sends an offer, and the partner's phone receives it.

Slide 17 of 28: The ultimatum game: theory vs your results

The room's real play, streaming in live as each pair finishes their game on their phones (QR code shown for anyone still joining) with a rising pairs counter. A keypress reveals every offer as a dot on a zero-to-one-thousand-pound axis — accepted offers in teal, rejected in red — with a dashed marker at the average offer. Further keypresses walk the reading: rollback theory says offer a token amount, it's accepted; a computed line gives the room's average offer and how many were rejected; and the lesson — a "rational" lowball can cost you the deal. Slide 18 of 28: The centipede game

A strip of ten turns, players one and two alternating, the pot growing from one hundred pounds to a capped thousand. The rules, in full: one hundred pounds is on the table. Player 1 can take it, or pass. Each pass adds another hundred, and the choice switches sides. At one thousand pounds the game ends, and whoever's turn it is takes it all. Played in pairs, on your phones, live between the two devices; this time whoever's first name is nearest to Z is Player 1, so the other partner gets the first move.

Slide 19 of 28: The centipede game: theory vs your results

The room's real play, streaming in live as each pair finishes their game on their phones (QR code shown for anyone still joining) with a rising pairs counter. A keypress reveals where every pair's game ended as dots over the ten turns — early takes on turns one to three in red, later takes and full runs in teal, the last position labeled RAN for pots that reached one thousand pounds. Further keypresses: rollback theory says someone takes at the end, so the logic says grab on turn one; a computed line gives the most common ending and how many pairs ran it all the way; and the lesson — short-term grabs wreck relationships worth keeping. Slide 20 of 28: The limits of sequential reasoning

Three numbered points, each with a small pictogram: complexity of drawing game trees to completion, even tic-tac-toe is hard (a node exploding into branches); requirement of complete information — in reality, uncertainty plagues us all (two cards, one face down, one marked with a question mark); limits of rationality in sequential decision-making — think of the games we just played (a head with a jagged line of thought inside).

Slide 21 of 28: Back to Tapas Airways

The running deal returns. The drawn Tapas Airways jet and its spare engine from Module 1 sit beside bullets revealed one at a time: Tapas Airways still needs its spare engine, and a rival bidder wants the deal as much as you do. Beyond this engine sits the real prize: a long-term deal with Tapas. You play as one room: debate each choice, then vote on your phones, and the majority decides. Three strikes and you lose your shot at the long-term deal; you cannot take a move back. A QR code at the bottom joins the anonymous vote page, which stays open on each phone for the whole game — this is the only slide carrying the QR code.

Slide 22 of 28: Opening play

Move one of the long-term Tapas deal game. The situation: Tapas has invited first offers for the engine deal, a rival bidder is preparing theirs, and nobody has made an offer yet. Two options, voted anonymously on phones with only a rising votes-in counter showing: A, make the first offer; B, make the second offer. The reveal shows the counts, commits the majority, and animates a branch from the chosen option to Tapas's narrated response. Making the first offer is safe: Tapas appreciates the assertiveness, and your offer now frames the conversation. Making the second offer is a strike: the rival sets the price, and the terms of the contest. A strike meter labelled the long-term deal, with three slots, repeats on every game slide; each wrong choice fills one red.

Slide 23 of 28: What does your offer focus on?

Move two. Your offer is on the table, and Tapas wants to know what you plan to center the offer on. A, low cost: undercut every rival on price, whatever it takes — a strike: you present a highly cost-competitive price, but you have just indicated to all players that you see this as more of a price war than a long-term collaboration with Tapas. B, high value: address their underlying interests at a higher price — safe: the price raises eyebrows, but the meeting runs long, because you are the one bidder talking about what actually worries them.

Slide 24 of 28: Can you do better?

Move three. The situation reads differently depending on the room's earlier path: after a high-value offer, the rival undercuts your higher price; after a low-cost offer, the rival goes even lower than you. Either way, Tapas shows you their number and asks if you can do better. A, meet Tapas's price demands and match your rival on costs — a strike: you stay in the contest, on the one dimension where the rival can always go again; whoever wins a price race, the winner is the price. B, hold your price and restate how you answer their concerns — safe: after a high-value offer, Tapas pushes back but keeps talking, because a number can be copied in a minute and what you offered cannot; after a low-cost offer the narration instead notes that with nothing behind your number but the number, Tapas hears stubbornness — but at least the race stops here.

Slide 25 of 28: A final concession

Move four. The final meeting: Tapas wants to go with your offer, but the board asks for one last concession before they sign — a cap on what surprise repairs can cost them. A, reject making the concession — safe: the board grumbles, and signs, and you have just shown them what dealing with you will be like: straight, and worth trusting with more than one engine. B, agree to making the concession — a strike: they sign, and they also remember how easily you moved; every deal with you from now on will start with a squeeze. Endings appear as an overlay. No strikes: Tapas and your company become long-term partners, and you work together for the next decade. One or two strikes: Tapas signs a long-term deal, but with hesitation — the contract carries break clauses that let them walk away early, and a try-a-cleaner-path option is offered. Three strikes: Tapas remains interested in your business, but sees you as a short-term provider, not a long-term partner. Strikes persist across retries and reloads.

Slide 26 of 28: The whole game, drawn as a tree

The finale: the game in reduced form. Four columns, one per move, each holding both options as chips — the walked path highlighted, struck branches in red, untaken branches faded — ending in a chip naming the outcome the room reached: long-term partners; signed, with break clauses; or a short-term provider. The strike meter shows the final count. A reset control wipes the game between groups.

Slide 27 of 28: Beyond sequential reasoning

The wrap-up. Today was sequential thinking: think forward and reason backward, and know the limits of doing so. A two-player pictogram sits centre. A reveal asks: what happens when nobody moves first, and you have to choose without seeing the other side's move? Closing chip: next session covers simultaneous zero-sum games, where one side's win is the other side's loss.
