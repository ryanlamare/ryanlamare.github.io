Games with multiple equilibria

  • So far, we have considered only games where one unique solution is present
  • But there are often instances where individuals can conceive of many strategies capable of generating similar outcomes
  • Games with multiple equilibria often require some sort of coordination by the players in order to reach a useful resolution

MODULE 5 · FOCAL POINTS

Focal points and common knowledge

  • The right answer was wherever you thought the room would agree together. We need to identify a focal point where our expectations can converge
  • How might we find one? We have to recall what we know about common knowledge
  • Do you know what everyone else in the room will answer?
  • Does everyone else know that you know?
  • Are you all 100 percent sure?
The spot everyone guesses is the focal point
MODULE 5 · BEST RESPONSE

Best response and the Nash equilibrium

  • We need a comprehensive way to identify parties’ ideal strategies in all games, not just prisoner’s dilemmas or zero-sum games
  • We know to identify dominant strategies and reject inferior ones – but what if there are no clear answers even then? Can we still find an equilibrium?
  • The answer is yes – thanks to John Nash!
MODULE 5 · BEST RESPONSE

Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of two conditions:
1
Each player is choosing a best response to what he believes the other players will do in the game
2
Each player’s beliefs are correct. The other players are doing just what everyone else thinks they are doing

The investment game

  • Let’s play a game where you can win lots of money!

  • If you invest and 90% of the room invests too, you get your £100 back and I’ll give you another £50
  • If less than 90% of the room invests, you lose your £100 and get nothing back
  • We’ll play two rounds, and your wins and losses add up
  • Round one is played in silence. Then one volunteer can address the room before round two

MODULE 5 · STAG HUNT

Stag hunt

  • Two hunters set off to find food. A stag feeds many people, but it takes both hunters to catch it. A hare feeds one person, and one hunter can catch it alone
  • With no way to communicate before they set off, each has to guess what the other will hunt
  • If Hunter 1 goes after the stag, Hunter 2 should go too – but there is no way for Hunter 2 to know where Hunter 1 is going
  • Both sides need to coordinate in order to be successful

MODULE 5 · STAG HUNT

Overcoming coordination problems

  • If we could communicate, we could assure the other side that we’re better off at a specific equilibrium
  • Think of bank runs: we all keep our money in the banks only as long as we’re sure it’s safe
  • This is why key figureheads give speeches to restore confidence
  • This is also one reason why leadership matters!
  • Would strategic communication matter under a PD?

  • You’d both rather end up together than apart – and that makes coordination more difficult, and communication trickier as well
  • Commonly known as the “Battle of the Sexes”

MODULE 5 · CHICKEN

Chicken

  • Two drivers head straight at each other. Whoever swerves first loses face, and whoever holds the line wins
  • If both swerve, nobody wins and nobody loses
  • If neither swerves, both crash – the worst outcome for both of them
  • Two equilibria again – one swerves, one goes straight – but each of you wants to be the one who goes straight

MODULE 5 · CHICKEN

Solving games of chicken

  • There are two equilibria here but the incentive to go straight results in a catastrophic outcome if you can't coordinate
  • So how do you win?
  • One way is to convince the other side that under no circumstances will you swerve – we’ll return to commitment and credibility later when we talk about strategic moves
  • Another way is to alternate swerve/straight strategies if you play a repeated game (knowing if a game repeats is crucial)

MODULE 5 · WHEN IT GOES WRONG

When coordination locks in

  • The night before the Challenger launch in 1986, the engineers who built the booster rockets told NASA it was too cold to launch safely
  • NASA pushed back, and the engineers’ own managers reversed the recommendation. When the call resumed, NASA asked if anyone had anything to add, and the engineers in the room said nothing
  • This is a battle of the sexes. Thiokol and NASA had to give one answer together. The engineers preferred to wait and NASA preferred to launch, and the call settled on NASA’s equilibrium
MODULE 5 · WHEN IT GOES WRONG

How do you break a bad focal point?

  • The focal point on that call became “go”. Once everyone expected everyone else to go along with it, going along was the best response
  • If you lead the room, your job is to move the focal point: you invite dissent, and you protect the people who give it
  • One tool is the “premortem”: before a big decision, assume it has already failed, and ask everyone in the room to say why

MODULE 5 · TAKEAWAYS

a few key takeaways...

Focal points
The right answer is wherever the room lands together
Nash equilibrium
Each player is choosing a best response to the others
Stag hunt
Symmetrical preferences: assurance and trust is key
Battle of the sexes
Asymmetric preferences: coordination becomes harder
Chicken
Opposed preferences: miscoordination is disaster
How you solve these games depends on focal points, irrespective of your personal preferences or beliefs!
