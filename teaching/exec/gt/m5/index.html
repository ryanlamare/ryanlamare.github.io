Applied Game Theory, Module 5 — Coordination Games

Accessible text version. Each heading below is one slide, in order. Payoff matrices are described cell by cell.

Slide 1 of 36: Title slide

Coordination games. Beyond the prisoner's dilemma. Module 5 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 36: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, play guess two-thirds of the average, and find out at the end who won. Two, use best response analysis to find every Nash equilibrium in a game. Three, play three coordination games: stag hunt, battle of the sexes, and chicken. Four, take the common knowledge quiz and see where the room converges.

Slide 3 of 36: Guess two-thirds of the average

A live game answered by phone — a QR code links to ryanlamare.com/go and a counter shows how many numbers are in. The rules: pick any whole number between 0 and 100. The individual whose number is closest to two-thirds of the room's average wins. The worked example is drawn as a number line from 0 to 100: dots at 5, 25 and 60; a marker at their average, 30; a red marker at two-thirds of that, 20; and the dot at 25 turns red, labelled "25 wins". Remember, you already know the correct answer — but so does everybody else. Numbers lock in now; the game is solved at the end of the session.

Slide 4 of 36: Introduction

So far, we've covered sequential choices, simultaneous zero-sum choices, and the prisoner's dilemma — shown as three pictogram tiles: a game tree, a pie split in two, and a payoff matrix with one cell boxed in red. Then two lines: there are many other important types of strategic thinking that come into play both in business decision-making and in everyday life; and although the prisoner's dilemma gets a lot of attention, other theories are equally important in explaining strategic thinking.

Slide 5 of 36: Best response and the Nash equilibrium

We need a comprehensive way to identify parties' ideal strategies in all games, not just prisoner's dilemmas or zero-sum games. We know to identify dominant strategies and reject inferior ones — but what if there are no clear answers even then? Can we still find an equilibrium? The answer is yes — thanks to John Nash. Beside the bullets, a pictogram of a four-cell payoff matrix with a question mark in it.

Slide 6 of 36: Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of two conditions, shown as a numbered rail: one, each player is choosing a best response to what he believes the other players will do in the game; two, each player's beliefs are correct — the other players are doing just what everyone else thinks they are doing.

Slide 7 of 36: Best response analysis, worked

A four-by-three matrix. Player 1 rows: top, high, low, bottom. Player 2 columns: left, middle, right. Payoffs (player 1, player 2) by row — top: 3,1; 2,3; 10,2. High: 4,5; 3,0; 6,4. Low: 2,2; 5,4; 12,3. Bottom: 5,6; 4,5; 9,7. Steps circle player 1's best-response payoffs in each column in teal (bottom-left, low-middle, low-right), then player 2's best-response payoffs in each row in terracotta (top-middle, high-left, low-middle, bottom-right), then draw a red box on the one cell holding both circles — low/middle, 5, 4 — the Nash equilibrium.

Slide 8 of 36: Multiple Nash equilibria

So far, we have considered only games with one unique solution — a single pure strategy Nash equilibrium. But there are often instances where individuals can conceive of many strategies capable of generating similar outcomes. Games with multiple equilibria often require some sort of coordination by the players in order to reach a useful resolution. Beside the bullets, a before-and-after figure: a matrix with one red box, labelled "one equilibrium", then an arrow to a matrix with three red boxes down its diagonal, labelled "several equilibria".

Slide 10 of 36: The common knowledge quiz

Rules for the self-paced quiz: the goal is to get as many answers correct as you possibly can — but the right answer is the answer the room provides, and you score a point only when at least 90 percent of the room answers the way you did. No conferring: you are coordinating on instinct. Ten minutes, fifteen questions, on your phones; the scores are gone through together later in the session. A QR code starts the quiz, and a ten-minute countdown timer sits on the slide.

Slide 13 of 36: The hunting matrix

Hunter 1 against Hunter 2, choices: two-headed cow, three-headed pig, cockroach. Cow/cow: 3, 3. Pig/pig: 3, 3. Cockroach/cockroach: 1, 1. Slide 14 of 36: The investment game

Let's play a game where you can win lots of money! You have two choices — invest, or don't invest. Investing puts $10 into the pot; sitting out puts in nothing and risks nothing. If you invest and at least 90% of the room invests too, you get your $10 back plus another $5; if less than 90% invest, you lose your $10. We play four rounds and your wins and losses add up. Round one is played in silence; after each reveal, one volunteer can address the room before the next round. A QR code links to the game page, where each player picks their name and locks a choice per round.

Slide 16 of 36: The investment game in matrix form

You against everyone else, choices invest or don't invest. Both invest: plus five dollars each. You invest while the room doesn't: minus ten for you, nothing for them. You sit out while the room invests: nothing for you, plus five for them. Nobody invests: nothing for anyone. A step rings the two diagonal equilibria, and a note names the structure: the investment game is a stag hunt, and the whole room just played it.

Slide 19 of 36: Focal points and common knowledge

How will you coordinate in order to repopulate the earth together? We need to identify a focal point where our expectations can converge. How might we find one? We have to consider common knowledge, in three steps: do you know that your date knows to go to a certain café? Does your date know that you know? Are you both 100 percent sure? Slide 23 of 36: Overcoming coordination problems

If only we could communicate, we could assure the other side that we're better off at a certain equilibrium. This is why key figureheads give speeches to restore confidence — one reason why leadership matters. And a crucial difference from the prisoner's dilemma: would strategic communication matter under a PD? Slide 29 of 36: Chicken in numbers

Slide 30 of 36: Solving games of chicken

There are two equilibrium points in this game (swerve/straight). So how do you win? One way is to convince the other side that under no circumstances will you swerve — we'll return to commitment and credibility later. Another way is to alternate swerve/straight strategies if you play a repeated game. Beside the bullets, the chicken matrix again, small, with both swerve/straight cells boxed in red.

Slide 31 of 36: Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and successive elimination of inferior strategies. If everyone guessed 100, you should guess two-thirds of that: 67 — so eliminate 68 and above. But if nobody plays above 67, your winning strategy is two-thirds of that: 45 — eliminate 46 and above. And so on, ruling out 30, 20, 13, 9 — all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium. So, all of those who answered 0 got it right — or did they?? Beside the bullets, a number line from 0 to 100 that shades out the eliminated numbers step by step — 68 and above, then 46 and above, then a run of ever-smaller bands — until only 0 is left, marked with a red dot labelled "0 is the Nash equilibrium".

MODULE 5 · GUESS TWO-THIRDS

Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

  • How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and successive elimination of inferior strategies
  • Let’s say everyone in the room guessed 100…what should you guess?
  • 100 × (2/3) = 67, so we can eliminate numbers 68 or above
  • But if nobody will play above 67, your winning strategy should be 2/3 of those choices
  • 67 × (2/3) = 45, so we can now eliminate numbers 46 or above
  • And so on (30, 20, 13, 9…)…all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium
  • So, all of those who answered 0 got it right…or did they??
