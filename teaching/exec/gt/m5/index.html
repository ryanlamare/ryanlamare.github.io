Slide 1 of 13: Title slide

Non-zero-sum games: overcoming PDs. Module 5 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 13: Resolving the prisoner's dilemma

We see voluntary cooperative behavior all the time. In a world full of incentives to defect against each other, why do things often not work this way? Think here about The Dark Knight.

Slide 3 of 13: Resolving the prisoner's dilemma: the evolution of cooperation

A critical question has puzzled game theorists since the PD was created: "Under what conditions will cooperation emerge in a world of egoists without central authority?" Robert Axelrod in particular wanted to explore how and why cooperation seems to be a natural state of affairs when the prisoner's dilemma might suggest otherwise.

Slide 4 of 13: Axelrod's computer tournaments: elements of the tournament

Each entrant writes a computer program that embodies a rule to select the cooperative or non-cooperative choice on each move. Professional game theorists were invited to send entries. A round robin tournament was constructed — every entry was paired with every other entry. Each game consisted of 200 moves (or plays).

Slide 5 of 13: The game was a prisoners' dilemma with the following matrix

Cooperate/cooperate: 3, 3. Cooperate/defect: 0, 5. Defect/cooperate: 5, 0. Defect/defect: 1, 1.

Slide 6 of 13: Possible outcomes

Over 200 plays, the theoretical maximum score was 1,000 (5 times 200) and the minimum was 0. A score of 600 (3 times 200) indicates that, on average, the two players cooperated. A score of 200 (1 times 200) indicates that, on average, the two players defected.

Slide 7 of 13: Who won the tournament?

The tournament was won by Anatol Rapaport, who played TIT FOR TAT. A very simple rule: always start by cooperating and never be the first to defect; but when and if the other side defects on one play, you defect on the next play; thereafter, always do what your opponent did on the preceding play.

Slide 8 of 13: Run the tournament

An interactive simulator. Two strategies are chosen from menus (Tit for Tat, Tit for Two Tats, Always Cooperate, Always Defect, Grudger, Random) and played against each other for 20 moves of the same matrix. Each player's moves appear as a row of C and D squares with running totals, so the room can watch niceness, forgiveness, and retaliation play out.

Slide 9 of 13: Why did tit for tat prevail?

Niceness — never be the first to defect — at least before move 199. None of the lower scoring entries was "nice". Each of the "nice" rules scored about 600 points with each of the other "nice" rules. Forgiveness — is defined as a propensity to cooperate in the moves following a defection by an opponent. TIT FOR TAT is very "forgiving" — it defects for one move but thereafter is totally forgiving.

Slide 10 of 13: Why did tit for tat prevail?

Retaliatory — means practicing reciprocity. Try to reciprocate both cooperation and defection. The precise level of reciprocity depends on the environment: if immediate retaliation leads to an unending conflict spiral, you may want to be more forgiving; if being too forgiving leads your opponent into exploiting your behavior, you may want to be less forgiving. Clean — describes a strategy that has clarity.

Slide 11 of 13: Other strategies?

Participants in Axelrod's tournaments pursued many other strategies. Theoretically, any of the other strategies might have worked. There is no best rule independent of the environment. In practice, however, TIT FOR TAT usually prevails. Tit for Two Tats — a more forgiving strategy — sometimes works better.

Slide 12 of 13: Lessons of tit for tat

Don't be too envious. Don't be the first to defect. Reciprocate both cooperation and defection. Don't be too clever.
