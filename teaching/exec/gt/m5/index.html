Slide 1 of 25: Title slide

Coordination games. Beyond the prisoner's dilemma. Module 5 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme.

Slide 2 of 25: Introduction

So far, we've covered strategic thinking as it applies to sequential choices, simultaneous zero-sum (win/lose) choices, and a specific type of simultaneous non-zero-sum strategic thinking (the prisoner's dilemma). But there are many other important types of strategic thinking that come into play both in business decision-making and in everyday life. Although the prisoner's dilemma gets a lot of attention, other theories are equally important in explaining strategic thinking!

Slide 3 of 25: Best response and the Nash equilibrium

First, we need to figure out a comprehensive way to identify parties' ideal strategies in all games, not just prisoner's dilemmas or zero-sum games. We know already that we should (1) identify dominant strategies, and (2) reject inferior strategies. But what if there aren't any clear answers even after considering dominant and inferior strategies? Can we still find an equilibrium? The answer is yes — thanks to John Nash! Best response analysis.

Slide 4 of 25: Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of two conditions: each player is choosing a best response to what he believes the other players will do in the game; and each player's beliefs are correct — the other players are doing just what everyone else thinks they are doing.

Slide 5 of 25: Best response and the Nash equilibrium

Best response analysis finds all the possible Nash equilibria in a game. That is, it's a way to find all strategies for each player whereby no player can get a better payoff by switching to some other strategy. Like the other games we've studied, best response relies on considering one side's strategy as a function of others' moves. The Nash equilibrium, and using best response analysis to identify Nash equilibria, helps to square potentially circular strategies and provides clarity when there is no dominant response.

Slide 6 of 25: An example of using best response analysis to find the Nash equilibrium

A four-by-three matrix. Player 1 rows: top, high, low, bottom. Player 2 columns: left, middle, right. Payoffs (player 1, player 2) by row — top: 3,1; 2,3; 10,2. High: 4,5; 3,0; 6,4. Low: 2,2; 5,4; 12,3. Bottom: 5,6; 4,5; 9,7. Steps mark player 1's best response in each column (bottom-left, low-middle, low-right), then player 2's best response in each row (top-middle, high-left, low-middle, bottom-right), then ring the one cell marked twice — low/middle, 5, 4 — the Nash equilibrium.

Slide 7 of 25: Multiple Nash equilibria

So far, we have considered only games where one unique solution is present — i.e., a single pure strategy Nash equilibrium. But there are often instances where individuals can conceive of many strategies that are capable of generating similar outcomes for players. Games with multiple equilibria often require some sort of coordination by the players in order to reach a useful resolution.

Slide 8 of 25: You need to find some food!

Oops, we blew most of the planet up, but there's good news! Three things survived the nuclear fallout, and are edible by humans: two-headed cows, three-headed pigs, and cockroaches. Each type of animal has a known area in which it likes to roam. Two hunters set off to find food — but, since there are no phones left, they can't communicate with each other before they get to the roaming areas. Both hunters would prefer cow or pig over cockroach, but they also both must eat something in order to survive.

Slide 9 of 25: The hunting matrix

Hunter 1 against Hunter 2, choices: two-headed cow, two-headed pig, cockroach. Cow/cow: 3, 3. Pig/pig: 3, 3. Cockroach/cockroach: 1, 1. Mixed choices score 0 for a hunter who finds no partner, 1 for a hunter eating cockroach alone. Steps ring the three equilibria on the diagonal.

Slide 10 of 25: You need to find some food, resolved

Here there are three Nash equilibria — cow/cow, pig/pig, and cockroach/cockroach. If Hunter 1 heads to the area where the cows (or pigs, or cockroaches) are, Hunter 2 should also go there. But there is no way for Hunter 2 to know where Hunter 1 is going to go — and although Hunters 1 and 2 both dislike cockroaches, how are they meant to determine who prefers two-headed pork and who prefers two-headed beef? This can be thought of as a coordination game, because both sides need to coordinate in order to be successful. The name of this game is commonly known as stag hunt.

Slide 11 of 25: You need to go on a date!

Oops, we blew most of the planet up, but don't worry, there are still two of life's staples that survived: coffee and dating. Since we need to repopulate the planet, dating has been encouraged, and since there's only one thing to drink (coffee), most dating happens at coffee shops. You've got a date tonight and you agreed to meet at the coffee shop. But oh no! You forgot to tell your date which coffee shop, and there are two cafes (Café Post-Apocalatte and Café Armagaddo) in this nuclear wasteland of a town. Again, we have a coordination issue.

Slide 12 of 25: The date matrix

You against your date, choices Armagaddo or Post-Apocalatte. Both Armagaddo: 1, 1. Both Post-Apocalatte: 1, 1. Different cafés: 0, 0. Steps ring the two matching-café equilibria.

Slide 13 of 25: You need to go on a date, resolved

How will you coordinate in order to repopulate the earth together? We need to identify a focal point where our expectations can converge. How might we find a focal point in this game? We have to consider common knowledge — do you know that your date knows to go to a certain café? Does your date know that you know to go to a certain café? Are you both 100 percent sure this is true?

Slide 14 of 25: Examples of coordination games

Coordination games are very common in society. Examples of coordination problems: Sleepless in Seattle (where should Meg Ryan and Tom Hanks find each other?). Television shows (if everyone else watches Game of Thrones, I should too otherwise I won't have anything to talk about with people, even if I hate it). Bad fashion ideas (normcore? Hush puppies? The Tipping Point and coordination games). Political bandwagons (it's best to present a unified — even if incorrect — front). Bank runs (we all keep our money in the banks only as long as we're sure it's safe).

Slide 15 of 25: Overcoming coordination problems

If only we could communicate, we could assure the other side by persuading them that we're better off at a certain equilibrium (or that we'll coordinate at the right place). This is why key figureheads give speeches in order to restore confidence. This is also one reason why leadership matters! Finally, this is also a crucial difference between these types of games and the prisoner's dilemma — would strategic communication matter under a PD?

Slide 16 of 25: You need to go to the movies!

The first date went really well and you had a nice latte together, so you've set up a second date. This time, you want to take your date to the movies — only one movie theater survived the nuclear winter, so you know to go to the same place. But, oh no! You forgot to tell your date which movie you want to see together. Three movies are playing at the theater: Mad Max, Zombieland, Waterworld. Both you and your date have preferences for each of these movies.

Slide 17 of 25: The movies matrix

You against your date, choices Mad Max, Zombieland, Waterworld. Mad Max/Mad Max: 2, 1. Zombieland/Zombieland: 1, 2. Waterworld anywhere: negative payoffs, worst of all Waterworld/Waterworld at minus 2, minus 2. Mismatches score 0 or negative. A step dims the Waterworld row and column.

Slide 18 of 25: Waterworld is the worst

The Waterworld film poster, full slide. The joke is the payoffs: nobody should ever pick Waterworld.

Slide 19 of 25: The reduced movies matrix

With Waterworld eliminated: Mad Max/Mad Max: 2, 1. Mad Max/Zombieland: 0, 0. Zombieland/Mad Max: 0, 0. Zombieland/Zombieland: 1, 2. Steps ring the two equilibria.

Slide 20 of 25: You need to go to the movies, resolved

Here, unlike in stag hunt, you have a problem. There are two equilibria, but players have different preferences for each equilibrium point. You'd rather see Mad Max than Zombieland, and vice versa for your date, but you'd both rather be on a date together than to watch the movie separately (unless the movie is Waterworld). This makes coordination more difficult — and communication trickier as well. The coordination game here is commonly (and misogynistically) known as the "Battle of the Sexes".

Slide 21 of 25: A few B.O.S examples

Like the stag hunt, the battle of the sexes variant can be found throughout society. One clear example is in bargaining and negotiations (we'll return to this later). Another example can be found in the parables of O. Henry.

Slide 22 of 25: Let's play chicken!

Ren (Kevin Bacon) against Chuck (Some Other Guy), choices swerve or straight. Both swerve: tie, tie. Swerve/straight: lose, win. Straight/swerve: win, lose. Both straight: crash, crash.

Slide 23 of 25: Chicken in numbers

The same matrix with payoffs. Both swerve: 0, 0. Swerve/straight: minus 10, plus 10. Straight/swerve: plus 10, minus 10. Both straight: minus 100, minus 100. Steps ring the two swerve/straight equilibria.

Slide 24 of 25: Solving games of chicken

There are two equilibrium points in this game (swerve/straight). So how do you win? Well, one way is to convince the other side that under no circumstances will you swerve. We'll return to commitment and credibility later. Another way is to alternate swerve/straight strategies if you play a repeated game.
