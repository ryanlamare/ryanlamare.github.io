Slide 3 of 34: Guess two-thirds of the average

A live game answered by phone — a QR code links to ryanlamare.com/go and a counter shows how many numbers are in. The rules: pick any whole number between 0 and 100. The individual whose number is closest to two-thirds of the room's average wins. A worked example: with guesses of 5, 25, and 60 the average is 30, two-thirds of 30 is 20, so 25 would win. Remember, you already know the correct answer — but so does everybody else. Numbers lock in now; the game is solved at the end of the session.

Slide 4 of 34: Introduction

So far, we've covered sequential choices, simultaneous zero-sum choices, and the prisoner's dilemma. But there are many other important types of strategic thinking that come into play both in business decision-making and in everyday life. Although the prisoner's dilemma gets a lot of attention, other theories are equally important in explaining strategic thinking!

Slide 5 of 34: Best response and the Nash equilibrium

We need a comprehensive way to identify parties' ideal strategies in all games, not just prisoner's dilemmas or zero-sum games. We know to identify dominant strategies and reject inferior ones — but what if there are no clear answers even then? Can we still find an equilibrium? The answer is yes — thanks to John Nash. A Nash equilibrium is a combination of two conditions: each player is choosing a best response to what he believes the other players will do, and each player's beliefs are correct.

Slide 6 of 34: Best response analysis, worked

A four-by-three matrix. Player 1 rows: top, high, low, bottom. Player 2 columns: left, middle, right. Payoffs (player 1, player 2) by row — top: 3,1; 2,3; 10,2. High: 4,5; 3,0; 6,4. Low: 2,2; 5,4; 12,3. Bottom: 5,6; 4,5; 9,7. Steps circle player 1's best-response payoffs in each column in teal (bottom-left, low-middle, low-right), then player 2's best-response payoffs in each row in terracotta (top-middle, high-left, low-middle, bottom-right), then draw a red box on the one cell holding both circles — low/middle, 5, 4 — the Nash equilibrium.

Slide 7 of 34: Multiple Nash equilibria

So far, we have considered only games with one unique solution — a single pure strategy Nash equilibrium. But there are often instances where individuals can conceive of many strategies capable of generating similar outcomes. Games with multiple equilibria often require some sort of coordination by the players in order to reach a useful resolution.

Slide 11 of 34: The investment game

Let's play a game where you can win lots of money! You have two choices — invest, or don't invest. Investing puts $10 into the pot; sitting out puts in nothing and risks nothing. If you invest and at least 90% of the room invests too, you get your $10 back plus another $5; if less than 90% invest, you lose your $10. We play four rounds and your wins and losses add up. Round one is played in silence; after each reveal, one volunteer can address the room before the next round. Slide 13 of 34: The investment game in matrix form

Slide 16 of 34: Focal points and common knowledge

How will you coordinate in order to repopulate the earth together? We need to identify a focal point where our expectations can converge. How might we find one? We have to consider common knowledge — do you know that your date knows to go to a certain café? Does your date know that you know? Are you both 100 percent sure? Beside the bullets, a drawn figure: two red arrows converge on a clock tower that fades in — the spot everyone guesses is the focal point.

Slide 20 of 34: Examples of coordination games

Coordination games are very common in society. Examples: Sleepless in Seattle (where should Meg Ryan and Tom Hanks find each other?). Television shows (everyone else watches Game of Thrones, so I should too). Bad fashion ideas (normcore? Hush Puppies? The Tipping Point). Political bandwagons (a unified front, even an incorrect one). Bank runs (money stays in the bank only while we're all sure it's safe).

Slide 21 of 34: Overcoming coordination problems

If only we could communicate, we could assure the other side that we're better off at a certain equilibrium. This is why key figureheads give speeches to restore confidence — one reason why leadership matters. And a crucial difference from the prisoner's dilemma: would strategic communication matter under a PD? Slide 28 of 34: Solving games of chicken

There are two equilibrium points in this game (swerve/straight). So how do you win? One way is to convince the other side that under no circumstances will you swerve — we'll return to commitment and credibility later. Another way is to alternate swerve/straight strategies if you play a repeated game.

Slide 29 of 34: Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and successive elimination of inferior strategies. If everyone guessed 100, you should guess two-thirds of that: 67 — so eliminate 68 and above. But if nobody plays above 67, your winning strategy is two-thirds of that: 45 — eliminate 46 and above. If nobody plays above 45, play 30. And so on, ruling out numbers above 20, 13, 9 — all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium. So, all of those who answered 0 got it right — or did they??

MODULE 5 · TWO-THIRDS

Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

  • How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and successive elimination of inferior strategies
  • Let’s say everyone in the room guessed 100…what should you guess?
  • 100 × (2/3) = 67, so we can eliminate numbers 68 or above
  • But if nobody will play above 67, your winning strategy should be 2/3 of those choices
  • 67 × (2/3) = 45, so we can now eliminate numbers 46 or above
  • And so on (30, 20, 13, 9…)…all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium
  • So, all of those who answered 0 got it right…or did they??
