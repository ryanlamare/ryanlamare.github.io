Coordination games. Solving games with multiple equilibria. Module 5 of the Applied Game Theory executive programme. Instructor: J. Ryan Lamare.

Slide 2 of 32: Learning objectives

Four numbered objectives: one, play guess two-thirds of the average, and find out at the end who won. Two, take the common knowledge quiz and see where the room converges. Three, use best response analysis to find every Nash equilibrium in a game. Four, play three coordination games — stag hunt, battle of the sexes, and chicken.

Slide 3 of 32: Introduction

So far, we've covered sequential choices, simultaneous zero-sum choices, and the prisoner's dilemma — shown as three pictogram tiles: a game tree, a pie split in two, and a payoff matrix with one cell boxed in red. Then one line: although the prisoner's dilemma gets a lot of attention, other theories are equally important in explaining strategic thinking.

Slide 4 of 32: Games with multiple equilibria

So far, we have considered only games where one unique solution is present. But there are often instances where individuals can conceive of many strategies capable of generating similar outcomes. Games with multiple equilibria often require some sort of coordination by the players in order to reach a useful resolution. Beside the bullets, a before-and-after figure: a matrix with one red box, labelled "one equilibrium", then an arrow to a matrix with three red boxes down its diagonal, labelled "several equilibria".

Slide 5 of 32: Guess two-thirds of the average

Slide 10 of 32: Focal points and common knowledge

The right answer was wherever the room landed together: we need to identify a focal point where our expectations can converge. How might we find one? We have to consider common knowledge, in three steps: do you know what everyone else in the room will answer? Does everyone else know that you know? Are you all 100 percent sure? Beside the bullets, a drawn figure: two red arrows converge on a clock tower that fades in — the spot everyone guesses is the focal point.

Slide 11 of 32: Best response and the Nash equilibrium

We need a comprehensive way to identify parties' ideal strategies in all games, not just prisoner's dilemmas or zero-sum games. We know to identify dominant strategies and reject inferior ones — but what if there are no clear answers even then? Can we still find an equilibrium? The answer is yes — thanks to John Nash. Beside the bullets, a pictogram of a four-cell payoff matrix with a question mark in it.

Slide 12 of 32: Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a combination of two conditions, shown as a numbered rail: one, each player is choosing a best response to what he believes the other players will do in the game; two, each player's beliefs are correct — the other players are doing just what everyone else thinks they are doing.

Slide 13 of 32: Best response analysis, worked

A four-by-three matrix. Player 1 rows: top, high, low, bottom. Player 2 columns: left, middle, right. Payoffs (player 1, player 2) by row — top: 3,1; 2,3; 10,2. High: 4,5; 3,0; 6,4. Low: 2,2; 5,4; 12,3. Bottom: 5,6; 4,5; 9,7. Steps circle player 1's best-response payoffs in each column in teal (bottom-left, low-middle, low-right), then player 2's best-response payoffs in each row in terracotta (top-middle, high-left, low-middle, bottom-right), then draw a red box on the one cell holding both circles — low/middle, 5, 4 — the Nash equilibrium.

Slide 14 of 32: The investment game

Let's play a game where you can win lots of money! You have two choices — invest, or don't invest. Investing puts $100 into the pot; sitting out puts in nothing and risks nothing. If you invest and at least 90% of the room invests too, you get your $100 back plus another $50; if less than 90% invest, you lose your $100. We play two rounds and your wins and losses add up. Round one is played in silence; then one volunteer can address the room before round two. A QR code links to the game page, where each player picks their name and locks a choice per round.

Slide 16 of 32: What you just played

You against everyone else, choices invest or don't invest. Both invest: plus fifty dollars each. You invest while the room doesn't: minus a hundred for you, nothing for them. You sit out while the room invests: nothing for you, plus fifty for them. Nobody invests: nothing for anyone. A step rings the two diagonal equilibria, then narrates: two Nash equilibria on the diagonal — everyone invests, or nobody does — and investing pays only if you can trust the room to invest too. A coordination game, commonly known as stag hunt.

Slide 17 of 32: Stag hunt

Two hunters set off to find food. A stag feeds both of them, but it takes both of them to catch it; a hare feeds one, and one hunter can catch it alone. With no way to communicate before they set off, each has to guess what the other will hunt. If Hunter 1 goes after the stag, Hunter 2 should go too — but there is no way for Hunter 2 to know where Hunter 1 is going. Both sides need to coordinate in order to be successful. The matrix, Hunter 1 rows against Hunter 2 columns: stag/stag 3, 3; stag/hare 0, 1; hare/stag 1, 0; hare/hare 1, 1. A step rings the two diagonal equilibria.

Slide 18 of 32: Tapas: the bigger prize?

The Tapas stag hunt, for discussion. Both sides can see something bigger than one spare engine behind this deal. Going big pays off only if the other side goes big too; going big alone is wasted effort. Just doing the one engine is safe — and small. What would it take for both sides to go big? The matrix, RRPF rows against Tapas columns, choices "build the bigger partnership" or "just do the one engine": both build 3, 3; RRPF builds alone 0, 1; Tapas builds alone 1, 0; both small 1, 1. Both diagonal cells are boxed in red.

Slide 19 of 32: Overcoming coordination problems

If only we could communicate, we could assure the other side that we're better off at a certain equilibrium. This is why key figureheads give speeches to restore confidence — one reason why leadership matters. And a crucial difference from the prisoner's dilemma: would strategic communication matter under a PD? On the right, a JetBlue clip labeled "JetBlue customers, game theorists" is embedded behind a click-to-play cover.

Slide 20 of 32: Battle of the sexes

Here, unlike in stag hunt, you have a problem: there are two equilibria, but the players have different preferences for each equilibrium point. You'd both rather end up together than apart — and that makes coordination more difficult, and communication trickier as well. Commonly (and misogynistically) known as the Battle of the Sexes. The matrix, Partner A rows against Partner B columns, plans A and B: A/A 2, 1; A/B 0, 0; B/A 0, 0; B/B 1, 2. A step rings the two diagonal equilibria.

Slide 24 of 32: Chicken

Two drivers head straight at each other. Whoever swerves first loses face, and whoever holds the line wins. If both swerve, nobody wins and nobody loses. If neither swerves, both crash — the worst outcome for both of them. Two equilibria again — one of you swerves — but each of you wants it to be the other one. The matrix, Driver 1 rows against Driver 2 columns: both swerve 0, 0; swerve/straight minus 10, plus 10; straight/swerve plus 10, minus 10; both straight minus 100, minus 100. A step rings the two swerve/straight cells.

Slide 25 of 32: Solving games of chicken

There are two equilibrium points in this game (swerve/straight). So how do you win? One way is to convince the other side that under no circumstances will you swerve — we'll return to commitment and credibility later. Another way is to alternate swerve/straight strategies if you play a repeated game. Beside the bullets, the chicken matrix again, small, with both swerve/straight cells boxed in red.

Slide 29 of 32: Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and successive elimination of inferior strategies. If everyone guessed 100, you should guess two-thirds of that: 67 — so eliminate 68 and above. But if nobody plays above 67, your winning strategy is two-thirds of that: 45 — eliminate 46 and above. And so on, ruling out 30, 20, 13, 9 — all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium. So, all of those who answered 0 got it right — or did they?? Beside the bullets, a number line from 0 to 100 that shades out the eliminated numbers step by step until only 0 is left, marked with a red dot labelled "0 is the Nash equilibrium".

MODULE 5 · GUESS TWO-THIRDS

Finding the Nash equilibrium in the two-thirds game

  • How do we solve this game? Remember common knowledge and successive elimination of inferior strategies
  • Let’s say everyone in the room guessed 100…what should you guess?
  • 100 × (2/3) = 67, so we can eliminate numbers 68 or above
  • But if nobody will play above 67, your winning strategy should be 2/3 of those choices
  • 67 × (2/3) = 45, so we can now eliminate numbers 46 or above
  • And so on (30, 20, 13, 9…)…all the way down to 0, which is the Nash equilibrium
  • So, all of those who answered 0 got it right…or did they??
